<!-- lines 13870-13899 -->
[We left the town at 4 o'clock in the afternoon on 20 December.] Three of our good friends went with us a good way out of the town, where we once more took leave of them and climbed onto our wagon, which was drawn by 8 oxen [1]. At 7 o'clock the oxen were outspanned to graze, and we ate our supper on the wagon. Baas Teunis, who had promised to provide us with food for the journey, brought out of the wagon chest a large cloth filled with things to eat, and said that Mr Schmidt had sent this with him for us [2]. We were amazed to see what this cloth was filled with: roast fowls, meat and more meat, and a large jug of wine as well. We cannot thank this friend enough for all that he has done for us during our stay of 4 weeks in the town. No father can do more for his children or care for them better than he has done for us and cared for us, together with his son-in-law. This dear old man is heartily glad that the door is now opened for the Hottentots too; he already sees in the spirit how eager they are for the Gospel. He has often promised to come and visit us at our future dwelling place, if God spares his life. [At 9 o'clock the oxen were inspanned again, and we drove through the] night [3].

<!-- lines 13900-13959 -->
On 21 December, early in the morning at 5 o'clock, we crossed the first river, and not long afterwards the Laurens River and Hottentots Holland [4]. At 7 o'clock we outspanned again below the kloof near a small cottage, and had breakfast [5]. Here Baas Teunis, on his way to the town, had left a span of oxen at pasture, so as not to be held up here on his return journey [6]. Towards 8 o'clock the oxen left here were inspanned, and those that had brought us this far he gave to a Hottentot boy to drive after the wagon. Now the kloof had to be climbed. We went up on foot over this mountain, which one must pass and which is astonishingly high; we believed it almost impossible that the oxen should be able to bring the wagon up this height. While we were still in the town we had often wondered why the farmers who came to town with only moderately loaded wagons had 12 to 16 oxen in front of them, when to our mind 2 oxen would have been enough, since the oxen in this country are very big and strong; but now we could believe it, and are astonished that it is possible for them to load so much. Further inland there are mountains for whose ascent 24 horses must be put to a wagon [7]. This is also the reason why oxen are used rather than horses, although there is an abundance of horses in the country; thus Baas Teunis alone has 250, for his own use and the Company's, and anyone who does not have at least 50 or 100 horses is taken for a needy farmer. Here one might ask: what do they do with so many horses? Answer: not much. As a rule they go out to pasture and do not come home for 8 days. Most of their work consists in treading out the corn at harvest. It is not threshed by people, as in Europe, but spread out on the field in a large circle fenced with posts [8], and then trodden so short and small by horses, which are driven into the circle and driven round with a big whip, that not a stalk of it stays whole. At 11 o'clock we had our midday meal and meanwhile let the oxen rest a little. Baas Teunis asked us whether we would rather sleep the coming night or continue on our way. We answered that if the oxen could stand it and he was willing, we would choose the latter. He said that in this country the oxen needed no rest for 12 hours, and that the farmers usually drove by night and let the oxen graze by day, because in this hot country it was cooler to travel at night. About 2 o'clock the oxen were inspanned again, and at 8 o'clock in the evening, after we had rested a little again, we continued on our way without outspanning the oxen and drove through the night [9].

<!-- lines 13960-13977 -->
On 22 December at 8 o'clock in the morning we arrived at the Company post, Soetemelksvlei, at Baas Teunis's [10]. The word we have for each day in our little text book [11] was, at our departure from the town on 20 December, from Isaiah 43:1 [12]: "Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth." And now at our arrival here on the 22nd, Isaiah 32:17: "And the work of righteousness shall be peace; and the effect of righteousness quietness and assurance for ever." After we had had breakfast, Baas Teunis gave us a room of our own, where, as he said, we could be quite undisturbed. We were to eat and drink at his table.

<!-- lines 13978-14000 -->
In the town we had been given a letter to take to a Juffrouw at Gentskraal, 2 hours from [here] [13]. [Since we wanted to deliver it into her] hands [ourselves], we went there early on the 23rd, in the cool of the morning. Baas Teunis gave us one of his servants, a German by birth, to go with us and show us the way. The Juffrouw was surprised to see us come so far on foot, and asked us to stay for the midday meal. On this occasion we saw her garden, in which it was very pleasant to walk, going as if in a wood, in the shade of lemon, almond, apricot, fig, apple and pear trees [14]; and one finds gardens like this at most of the farmers'. One is astonished at the size and number of these trees, which grow up without much trouble and care, such as is needed in Europe, and bear so much fruit that we saw many branches broken off by the quantity and weight of the fruit. In the afternoon the Juffrouw had us driven home in a wagon with 8 oxen in front of it [15]. The road was full of mountains, which in this country are astonishingly many, and high and steep besides.

<!-- lines 14001-14043 -->
24 December was the day on which we had resolved to go out and look for a place for our future dwelling. For us this was a day of double weight, being also the day of the birth of our precious Redeemer, who by his becoming man has won so many blessings for us, of which in this land of darkness there is neither word nor sign [16]. So, moved by this double weight, we prayed our Lord very urgently in our morning blessing: first, that he would graciously let us experience in rich measure the blessings won by his birth, so full of salvation; secondly, that he would himself lead us by his Spirit and show us the place where it would please him to have us live in this country. After breakfast we drove off from Soetemelksvlei with Baas Teunis at 6 o'clock, with 8 oxen in front of our wagon. At 7 o'clock we came to Hartebeestkraal, where we found a remnant of the wall of the house built by the former Brother Georg Schmidt [17]. At 10 o'clock we came to Baviaanskloof. From here we went a little way off into a Hottentot kraal, and asked a Hottentot who lived there whether he did not know where Schmidt's house had stood. He was willing and went with us to show us the place, which was still a fair distance from the part of the wall we had seen [18]. In the garden lying next to it we found various fruit trees, of which, according to what we heard in another kraal, Georg Schmidt himself is said to have planted an almond, an apricot and a pear tree. We also saw various remains of kraals of the Hottentots who had lived with him. For the rest the garden, as can easily be imagined, was in a wild state. We stayed here 2 hours and looked at everything very carefully. In our minds all three of us were agreed that this was the place where the Lord our God wished us to live for the present.

<!-- lines 14044-14067 -->
Baas Teunis asked the Hottentot who had shown us the way whether he had heard that teachers had come from Europe to teach the Hottentots. He answered: yes, the farmers had told him that such people had come, who would treat them very kindly at the beginning; but if they let themselves be led by this to go to them, more would soon follow to carry them off and sell them as slaves to Batavia. When we had come home again to Soetemelksvlei, Baas Teunis asked a Hottentot woman whether she had any wish to learn. She answered: no, she was too old for it (though she was only 30 years old) and had no wish to do it. At this Baas Teunis said: "If you have no wish, then at least do not keep your children from it; for the government is sending you these teachers out of love, because they are good people, who [...] [and] the government has charged me to bring them to you and to tell you so" [19]. Having heard that a Hottentot woman baptised by Brother Schmidt was still alive, living by the Sergeants River half an hour from here, we went to look for her [20].

<!-- lines 14068-14136 -->
When we got there, all the Hottentot men and women who were at home came out of their huts to welcome us, and surrounded Baas Teunis with a reverence that cannot be described. He asked at once for the Hottentot woman who had been baptised by Georg Schmidt. She was fetched at once from her hut and brought to him. She had to be led, because she could no longer walk by herself, and since she could no longer stand either, she sat down on the ground. Baas Teunis then addressed the assembled Hottentots in the same tone as above, asking whether they all wished to learn, which they answered with one voice: "Yes" [21]. Baas Teunis then told the Brethren that they might now speak with them too. They asked the old woman whether she had been baptised by Brother Schmidt, and what her name was [22]. She answered the first with "Yes", and said that her name was Lena. To the question whether any more of the baptised were living, she answered: "No, I alone am left; the others have died." Question: "Do you know that Georg Schmidt has also died?" Answer: "Yes, I have heard it." Brother Marsveld asked further whether she still remembered what Brother Schmidt had said to her, which she answered with "No"; she had forgotten everything. To the question whether he had not spoken with her of Jesus, who became man for us and died for our sin, she answered: "Yes." We then told her that we were his brothers, and if she wished to learn, we would make known to her the way of salvation, as Brother Schmidt had also done. The old woman, who is at least 80 years old, said at this: "To God be praise and thanks!" These few words touched our hearts deeply [23]. When she added that she still had a book that Georg Schmidt had given her, someone ran at once to her hut to fetch it. It was wrapped in two sheepskins, and so very well kept. It was a Dutch New Testament, which she had once been able to read very well, but now that she is blind she is no longer able to. Another Hottentot woman, about 30 years old, has also learned to read, from her *neef*, who was baptised [24]. To make a trial we had her read something. It was remarkable that she opened at Matthew 2, the birth of Jesus, and read it aloud very nicely. When she had done so, we asked her whether she understood what she read, and whether she knew who the child Jesus was. Answer: "No, that I do not know or understand." We said: "If you come to us, then, we will explain it to you." For the present we told her only this, that tomorrow was the day of remembrance of the birth of Jesus, of which, however, she and the other listeners had not the least idea. On this occasion Baas Teunis said to them: "These teachers whom you have now seen mean to build a house by the Sergeants River, where Georg Schmidt lived in former times [25]. Lend them a helping hand in it, and do not let the ill-disposed farmers make you believe anything. Do not believe them, but believe me; for you know that I have always sought and advised what is best for you." They promised him everything, for he stands in great esteem with them. We then went on speaking to them, and they listened to us sitting on the ground. Afterwards, however, Baas Teunis told us that though they confirmed everything with "Yes", one could not always rely on it.

<!-- lines 14137-14157 -->
From here we drove to the house that Baas Teunis's son is building, which is not yet quite finished. He is our nearest neighbour, and lives about an hour and a half from us. He is still unmarried, and not a disagreeable person. At 7 o'clock we got home again. The season being too far gone to begin building this year, we shall make a [start] with the beginning of the new year, [and Baas Teunis will] help us in it as far as he can [26]. The building timber has to be fetched from 4 hours further inland, and so from a distance of 6 hours from the place where we mean to build our house [27]. There is wood here for burning, but not for building; it is only bushes, from which one can hardly cut pieces for props, for the tremendously high mountains are mostly bare and without forest. The farmers must buy all their timber from Baas Teunis; what the Company needs of it at the Cape must also be taken there, a good 3 days' journey; and what grows at our place is so surrounded by rocks and mountains that it seems almost impossible to get it out from among them.

<!-- lines 14158-14173 -->
On 25 December, the first day of Christmas, there was a meeting, which 12 of Baas Teunis's servants attended, and we 3 brethren too. In this meeting first psalms were [sung], then a sermon was read, and then psalms were sung again; and with this the divine service for these Christmas days was over. Baas Teunis assured us that many farmers knew nothing at all of Christmas. This ignorance and indifference towards a festival that is so important to us caused us great sorrow. We went into our room, worshipped our God who became man, and implored him to let us, his poor servants, whom he has sent into this country so darkened, share all the more richly in the blessings of his becoming man, and to make us fit to shine as a light in this country, and to carry out that for which he has sent us here.

<!-- lines 14174-14216 -->
On 26 December Baas Teunis asked us to go with him on an ostrich hunt. This is usually done on horseback; but since we cannot ride, he had a wagon with 8 oxen inspanned for us. His intention was to shoot one of these birds on its nest, one of which was known to him [28]; but when we got there, the bird was gone and the eggs broken, except for one, which was taken along. When we found another egg on the road, Baas Teunis said that the Hottentots were very quick at looking for and finding these eggs, and brought them to sell. Three men have enough in one such egg to eat their fill, and their taste surpasses that of hens' eggs. The ostriches, of which we saw many more on our way but could not get near, cannot fly, but run so fast that no horse can overtake them. A Hottentot boy ran in front of our wagon, which is always done, to lead the front oxen. One must marvel how fast these boys can run. He must go through everything, through water too, even if it came up under his arms, and such a boy can keep this up day and night, however hard he has to run. The driver sits on the wagon and drives the oxen on with an astonishingly long whip, whose handle is 9 and the lash a good 15, so together 24 ells long. They use both hands for it, and with it they know how to hit exactly the ox they mean. At 6 o'clock in the evening we came home. The weather was so hot that we were badly burned by the sun. [NB. in December] [29]. Because of the heat the wagon had to be open, so as to be able to see everything; otherwise covered wagons are used [30]. This increased the burning of the sun, which was so strong that one could hardly believe it was Christmas time. But here in this country 24 December is the longest day, the sun then shining until 10 minutes past seven [31]. On Sunday 30 December a sermon was read again and psalms sung, which happens every Sunday [32]. In the evening Baas Teunis's children, who are 6 in number, must read a chapter from the Bible and close with a hymn, at which we were present too. He, as well as his wife, are respectable people, and they bring up their offspring well.

<!-- lines 14217-14231 -->
He keeps a schoolmaster of his own for them, who is also very skilled in music, and teaches the daughters to play the clavier and the sons the violin. This evening the Landdrost of Swellendam came to take part in the festivities, which began this night and went on until 2 January. According to the custom [33], this night is the merriest of all, and is held to be the greatest festival of the whole year, for which many come together [...] eating and drinking, for which the most costly things are brought [34]. This costs Baas Teunis a great deal, because by the custom of this country, where hospitality is general, all who come, who at these festivals are very many, must be fed and entertained.

<!-- lines 14232-14252 -->
On the evening of 31 December we 3 brethren looked for a spot out in the field among the bushes, to thank our tenderly beloved Lord and Saviour humbly for all the mercies and kindnesses we have experienced in this year now ending, and to beg forgiveness for everything in which we have grieved him and not listened obediently to his voice. We prayed likewise that he would bless us abundantly in the coming new year, lead us by his Holy Spirit, give us tender love for one another, take away from us everything that does not befit a true servant of Jesus, be and remain our counsellor in all things, and let us speak to this people, to whom he has sent us, such words as are after his heart and may bear fruit through the working of his Spirit, to open their hearts, so that the word of his meritorious suffering and dying may find entrance into their hearts. Then we gave one another our hands to serve him faithfully, and he graciously acknowledged us poor ones and let us feel his peace.

## Notes

1. The German diary has 12 oxen.
2. *de Heer Schmit*: Father Schmidt of Cape Town, the brethren's host. The Dutch settles the doubtful German *unſer Sel. Schmidt* (A363595, entry 02, note 1), which should be read as a living man. The German has "roast turkey, fish and meat"; the Dutch *gebrade Hoenders, Vleesch en nog ander Vleesch*, "roast fowls, meat and other meat".
3. Line 13898 is missing. The transcription runs *te komen bezoeken.* / *Nagt door.* The German diary has "At 9 o'clock the oxen were inspanned again and we drove through the night"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there.
4. The Laurens (Lourens) River at what is now Somerset West, and the district of Hottentots Holland; see `glossary.csv`.
5. *naby een klein huisje*, "near a small house". This settles the German *bey einem kleinen Händelgen* (A363595, entry 02, note 5), which is probably a misreading of *Häusgen*.
6. *op zyne heen reize naer de Stad*, "on his way out to the town". This settles the German *ſeiner Eheweibe*, "his wife" (A363595, entry 02, note 6), probably a misreading of *ſeiner Hinreiſe*.
7. *een Wagen met 24 paarden*, "24 horses". The German diary has 24 [oxen], which fits the next sentence, where oxen are preferred to horses; *paarden* is probably a slip of the Dutch copyist.
8. *met paalen omtuind*; the German diary has a circle "edged with stones".
9. The Dutch leaves out the German diary's stages of this day: the Steenbras River at 9 o'clock, the Palmiet River at 11, the Knoflokskraal River at 4 and the Bot River at 7. *weder wat geruyst hebbende* is read as *gerust*, "rested".
10. *Zoete melks Valley*: Soetemelksvlei, the Company post on the Sonderend River; see `glossary.csv`.
11. *Loofungboekje*: the *Losungsbüchlein*, the Moravian book of daily texts; *Loofung* is a misreading of *Loſung*.
12. *Jes: 43, 1*: the verse quoted is Isaiah 42:1, as in the German diary. The Dutch gives the two Bible verses without the hymn verses that follow them in the German. The Bible verses are given in the words of the King James Version.
