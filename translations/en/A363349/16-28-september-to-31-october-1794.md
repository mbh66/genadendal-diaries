Diary of the 3 Brothers Marsveld, Schwinn and Kühnel at the Cape, from 28 September to 31 October 1794.

<!-- lines 5523-5539 -->
Early on 28 September Brother Schwinn set out for the Cape with a Hottentot of ours, accompanied by our heartfelt good wishes. The meeting at 10 o'clock was very well attended. Brother Marsveld spoke on Matthew 7, verses 18 to 24 [1]. The Spirit of God made himself powerfully felt on the hearts of these people. On Sundays we speak on the Sunday Gospel, as is our custom. Today the Gospel was Matthew 6, verse 24, where among other things it says: "Take no thought what ye shall eat or drink," and so on. [We have to pass over these texts, for the Hottentots] take no thought for the next morning [2]: when they have something to eat, they eat it up all at once if they can, and keep nothing of it for the next day. We must much rather commend thrift to them.

<!-- lines 5540-5577 -->
On the 29th an old woman came who had been ill, and was still so weak that she could hardly reach our house with a stick in her hand. She said: "I have long wished to come here and speak with the teachers. I have come from so far away to hear God's word and to live by it, since God has shown us such great mercy and sent us teachers." (She is from about 20 German miles from here.) "Since I came here I have heard God's word twice, for I fell ill. Now I would gladly think about it and thank our dear Saviour for his great love and mercy [3]. But there is an old man in the kraal where I am, who has also come here. He always talks badly and speaks of nothing but bad things, and makes so much noise [4] that I cannot possibly bear it; for I want to thank our God now, and can no longer listen to such things. And so I have come here to tell you this." We said that she should tell this man to come to us at once; we wanted to speak with him. He came at once; he is lame and must walk with a stick. We asked him why he had come here. He answered: to hear God's word. We put his behaviour to him, and asked him whether he wanted to serve two masters, and said that if he wanted to serve the devil he could not live here, but could go back where he had come from. He was silent and began to weep.

<!-- lines 5578-5595 -->
At midday a sick man sent to ask that one of us would come and visit him. Brother Marsveld went to him. He asked him among other things whether he had anything to say to him. He said: "No." Question: if he should die now, did he know where his poor soul would go? Answer: "I pray that the dear Saviour will have mercy on me." Question: whether he had heard in the meeting that he must pray to the dear Saviour? Answer: "I have not yet been in any meeting; I fell ill as soon as I came here. Those who go to the meeting and come to visit me have told me that I should pray to the dear Saviour for forgiveness of my sins, and I do this too." In the evening Brother Kühnel read the 5th and 6th chapters of the Gospel of Matthew in the meeting [5].

<!-- lines 5596-5622 -->
On the 30th several came who complained of the unrest of their hearts. Our sisters Anna and Sophia came too. The first said: "I am often very perplexed about myself, for I feel so bad that I believe it is going backwards with me. That I am as bad as I now feel, I could never have believed; and I feel worse every day." She was directed to the Saviour, to whom no sinner is too bad. The Holy Spirit is going very thoroughly to work with her, for she believed that she was good by nature and much better than other people. Sophia said that for some time she had been very content. Question: about what? Answer: "When I think about what our dear Saviour has borne for us, I feel such a joy in my heart. [Then I wanted to ask you: when I lie down to] sleep, I pray the dear Saviour to let me lie in his bed [6]. Was that praying rightly?" She was told that she should just pray simply; the dear Saviour does not look at the words, but at the heart and what it desires.

<!-- lines 5622-5640 -->
On 1 October we were to speak with the candidates for baptism, but we let it lapse this time, and made it known in the meeting that those who wanted to speak with us could come to one of us. A sick woman also sent to ask that one of us would come and visit her. Brother Kühnel went there. He asked her how she was. Answer: "I always think of our dear Father, and pray for grace and mercy." He commended to her the love of Jesus for poor sinners. Many others were present, and a blessed feeling was there. Several of the candidates for baptism also came and spoke very openly with us [7].

<!-- lines 5640-5648 -->
On the 3rd several came again who were very uneasy. 2 of the candidates for baptism also came and asked with many tears for holy baptism. The singing meeting, which Brother Kühnel took, we had to hold in the house. It was full moon, but cold; even when it is hot by day, the evenings are often not only cool but even cold.

<!-- lines 5648-5657 -->
On the 4th towards evening Brother Schwinn arrived back with us safe and well. We found much cause to thank the dear Saviour with him for all the protection he has experienced on this journey. In a report of it he tells the following:

<!-- lines 5658-5687 -->
"On 28 September I set out from our brothers with a Hottentot. At 3 o'clock we came to the Bot River [8], which was still very high, so that the horses went into the water up to the saddle; but we got across safely. In the evening we came to a farmer named Guber, to spend the night there. I asked him whether I could not stay here this night for money and good words. Question: 'Where do you come from?' Answer: 'From Baviaanskloof.' Then I got no answer any more. He [did not] tell me to dismount and come into the house, which by custom here is the first thing [9]. I got off my horse and went into the house, but no one spoke a word to me; the man went away and left me sitting. I said to the wife that they might grant me a little place in their house and give my horses their fodder; I would gladly pay for it. She said: 'I have nothing against it, but my husband is against it,' and so on. Gladly as I would have ridden on, I had to stay here this night and let them do with me as they would, for the road I had before me was very dangerous. But I was glad and thankful that I could be under a roof, and that my horses got their fodder.

<!-- lines 5688-5715 -->
"On the 29th I rode away again early, after I had paid, without taking anything, and at half past 6 came to the Palmiet River [10], which was very dangerous; for it is very broad and deep and has many holes, so that if a man does not know the right way, both he and his horse can perish, as has happened in these days, with wagon and horses. By good fortune I found someone who showed me the way, and so we got across safely. [Towards midday we came below the Kloof,] where I had my horse fed [11]; and at half past 5 we came to the last house before the Cape Flats [12], from which one still has 4 long hours to ride (until one comes to a house again; 4 hours of hard riding is more than 4 miles on foot). Here I wanted to stay the night; but this man, instead of advising me to stay here, although he knew well how dangerous it is in this wilderness, advised me to ride on: I could still get to the Cape well enough. I thought: here it will go no better than the night before; and I said to the Hottentot that we would just ride through this night.

<!-- lines 5716-5733 -->
"But he had no wish to, yet did not tell me why until we were in the middle of the wilderness. Then he said: 'Let us ride as hard as our horses can run. Many slaves who have deserted stay here, and they have already killed many people.' I said: why had he not said so before? But there was nothing to be done; we had to go through. I commended us to the dear Saviour, and we rode as hard as our horses could run; and although we did see fires on both sides, nothing befell us, and at half past 9 in the evening we arrived at the Cape safe and sound. We were very kindly received by the children of our friend Martin Schmidt, who wondered that I came so late, and said that I might have come to harm.

<!-- lines 5734-5762 -->
"On the 30th I went at once to the Governor [13], who received me very kindly. When he had asked me about everything, how it goes with us and among us, and I had told him the truth, he wished with joy that it might go on so further. I now began to speak with him about our place, and about the rumours that we heard, and those from such persons as one could believe to know something, that we would have to leave this place, which would be painful to us, because of the blessing of God that rests on it. He said: 'My dear friend, this place where you now live you are to keep. The Commissioners-General gave you this place, and I will also assure you that you shall remain living there in peace until they have come back from Batavia. The Directors,' he went on, 'gave you permission for yourselves to settle only where there is no Christian congregation [14], and then you would have to withdraw right among the Kaffers; for everywhere that Christians live can be called Christian congregations. But I will hold to it that you can remain living there in peace.'

<!-- lines 5762-5791 -->
"I thanked him for his goodwill, and also put to him the narrowness of the room in our house, so that more than half the people must stand outside the door when we hold a meeting, and that there were good friends who had partly seen this matter for themselves and judged that it was necessary to build a meeting house, and wanted to take a real share in it. He said: 'That I do not allow, and cannot allow either; it must stay as you found it. If you build a bigger house, at once it will be said, you are building a church, and then I am overrun again; and that you may not do where there is a Christian congregation.' I answered that we [lived far from a Christian congregation] after all, and a farmer's place was not to be called a congregation [15]. Answer: 'I cannot allow it. You have too many enemies, and had I not held to it so, you would long ago have been driven off as far as the Kaffers.' There was nothing more to be done about it. I went on to pay a visit to several gentlemen of the government, who were very friendly and glad that the word of God goes on so blessed, and said: 'You must keep this place and not be driven away.'

<!-- lines 5791-5811 -->
"On 1 October I went to the Gezaghebber, Mr Rhenius [16], who to all appearances is a true friend of ours. When he had asked about everything, he asked what the Governor had said, and gave me the advice to go to him again (but not to give him away) and ask him for permission to put up a Hottentot hut, so as to be sheltered from wind and rain. I thanked him for his good advice and went to the Governor again; but I was not well received by him. He said: 'What I have said stands. Just tell your brothers that they are to keep dead quiet; and when you come here again, come to me, and then we will speak of this matter again.' I could do nothing further about it than commend our whole situation in which we find ourselves to the dear Saviour.

<!-- lines 5811-5818 -->
"On the 2nd I went once more to some gentlemen and friends, to take leave and commend us to their protection and remembrance. Mr Lesueur, who is a gentleman of the government, gave me a small present, as he said, for our recreation, of 25 rixdollars, and our friend Schönberg 10 rixdollars [17]. The Saviour bless them for it!

<!-- lines 5818-5845 -->
"At 4 o'clock in the afternoon we went on with our journey, to pay a visit to our friend Mr Lücke [18] and stay the night there. He has bought a country place two hours from the town, not far from the road that we had to take, so that we could take our way home again early in the morning, and be at home again in two days if possible. For the enmity of many a farmer towards us is as great as towards the Hottentots, so that they do not like to grant us a night's lodging. Mr Lücke received me very kindly and entertained me very well. Mr Heuser, a watchmaker, who is a good friend of ours and gave me his company, rode back to town in the evening [19]. He also told me where I should stay the next night. But this night I spent very uneasily until morning; I was very anxious, but did not know why. Afterwards I opened on a text, which was: 'Out of six troubles the Lord has saved me, and in the seventh no misfortune shall touch me,' Job 5:19. In how much need has the gracious God spread his wings over us!

<!-- lines 5845-5875 -->
"In the morning, that is on 3 October, I set off from there early. An hour from there we came in sight of a small house, and rode towards it. My Hottentot dismounted and asked the way, but I stayed in the saddle. My horse, which was very young, wanted to throw me off, and reared up on its hind legs with us and went over with me. I came to lie under the horse, and although I was very much shaken, the dear Saviour sent his angels, who kept me so that no misfortune befell me; I kept no further feeling of it than some pain in the small of the back. I now took the Hottentot's other horse and mounted it at once, and we rode [on our way, praising] our Lord, who has kept me so graciously [20], and I asked him to keep us further, which he has done. My Hottentot was so frightened that he felt more pain than I. At 7 o'clock in the evening we came to the farmer I had been directed to, who received me kindly and lodged me well. In the morning at 4 o'clock we rode off from there, and towards evening arrived safe and sound with my dear brothers in Baviaanskloof, who received me very warmly. Praise and thanks be to the dear Saviour for all that he has done for me on this journey."

<!-- lines 5877-5889 -->
On 6 October a Hottentot woman came who had been at a farmer's, and said that a slave woman had stolen milk from her master, and she had drunk some of it: had she committed a sin by it? Question: whether she knew that the slave had stolen the milk? Answer: "Yes!" She was told that she was to be looked on just like the one who had stolen the milk. She asked for forgiveness and promised not to do it again, and said that she had believed that as long as she did not steal, it was no sin.

<!-- lines 5889-5914 -->
Since it is very dry in our garden, we wanted to water it today. This costs much trouble, but it did not work, because there are so many moles everywhere, which run through everything, so that the water runs away. We do not know how we are to get wet what these little creatures have not completely burrowed through (and so dried out). We have a big garden indeed, but we get little from it. There is also a kind of small beetle that eats everything off down to the ground; when something is planted for seed, it cannot flower; many thousands sit on one plant. When something is sown, they eat it as soon as it comes out of the ground. When these are gone, the caterpillars come and eat what is left. But it is not only so with us, for these complaints are everywhere. In the fatherland we were told of the splendid cauliflower that grows at the Cape; but there is a great difference between the Cape and here. We have not failed in sowing, but so far we have got little [21].

<!-- lines 5915-5933 -->
On the 7th a woman came and said that the others had told her she must slaughter an ox, so that they would have something to eat. (She asked whether she ought to do this? She is one of those who have only just come here.) We told her that she should not do this; she should keep her cattle. She still has some cattle, and it is the custom among the Hottentots that when someone still has something, he must give to those who have nothing more; and so they live in common in everything. Then she also asked what it could be: others had told her that lately a light had gone from kraal to kraal (or house)? We explained that this was nothing: there were certain little creatures that flew by night, which she could often see. There are certain little creatures here, somewhat bigger than the glow-worms in our fatherland, which fly a great deal at this time of year. Brother Kühnel held the singing meeting in the evening.

<!-- lines 5933-5936 -->
On the 8th the dear Saviour owned the meetings in a special way. On Wednesdays there are four, early in the morning for the ... [22]

<!-- lines 5938-5947 -->
... with some we had to speak earnestly, since it seemed as if they were beginning to fall asleep; but on the whole one could rejoice. The night before, and all day today, a heavy rain fell, so that an astonishing flood came out of the mountains; [whole pieces of the Hottentots' gardens were] also washed away [23]. Early on the 16th the water had already fallen somewhat, and today was a fine day. Today many Hottentots went to the farmers again to weed the corn.

<!-- lines 5948-5963 -->
On the 18th, at the beginning of our conference, we had a heartfelt conversation with one another. We were much ashamed before our dear Lord, and presented ourselves before him as debtors, when we look at the blessing he has given us and see ourselves so far behind in all that we owe him. We asked him for forgiveness, and at the same time that he would bless us poor ones and our 4 fellow partakers of his body and blood this evening. We also put before our dear Lord our Maria and Anna, the first as a confirmand for Holy Communion and the second as a candidate for it; of which he assigned us the second as a candidate for Holy Communion.

<!-- lines 5964-5975 -->
In the afternoon Baas Teunis's son came; he had two more with him, whom we did not know. Young Mrs Teunis sent a letter with him, in which she wrote that the daughter of Mr Lesueur was with her and wanted to visit us tomorrow; she would also like to attend the meeting. Mr Lesueur is one of the gentlemen of the government at the Cape. In the evening we held Holy Communion, with the Saviour owning us poor ones.

<!-- lines 5976-5980 -->
On the 19th towards 10 o'clock the daughter of Mr Lesueur came, and his sister too, and some gentlemen from the Cape, accompanied by Baas Teunis and his wife, making 5 persons in all.

<!-- lines 5980-5997 -->
After they had drunk coffee, the meeting began. Brother Marsveld spoke on the Sunday Gospel, Matthew 22, verse 34 to the end. There was great emotion among the Hottentots and among the visitors too. Baas Teunis could not hold back his tears, although he did everything to prevent it. Both he and his wife were quite silent afterwards. Those who had come from the Cape said: "This is a work of God, and no one will be able to hinder it, although many would gladly destroy it altogether." They stood amazed. Even Baas Teunis said: "Where do all these people come from? And half are still standing outside."

<!-- lines 5997-6025 -->
On the 20th. It was said lately that many of the Hottentot women had gone to the farmers to weed the corn; among these were 3 of our baptised. Today some came back, and 2 of our baptised, Eva and Susanna. They came at once and told us how it had gone with them since then, and related with sorrow that Dorothea had got drunk at the farmers'. The farmer's wife had given each of them a bowlful [24] of wine, but they had not drunk of it until they were about to leave, and then Eva took a glassful. But Dorothea, as she was leaving, had yet another bowlful, and so much brandy besides. They would gladly have had the other 2 come away, but could not get them away; nor 2 others who go to school but are not in close fellowship with us. These, and Dorothea too, did not come home with the others, but put up at another farmer's. We are finding out now more than before how things go, for until now we have heard little bad, since the Hottentots are very loyal to one another, and one will not betray another.

<!-- lines 6025-6046 -->
In our school [we gave very earnest warnings during the teaching]. Since big and small care very much about the school and the teaching [25], we have said (and we will keep to it) that if they behave badly, whether here in the kraals or at the farmers', whether in talk or in drinking and so on, they may no longer come to school: we will take their book from them, and they can go where they like; for we do not want people in the school who serve the devil, and spend so much trouble on them. It would be better for them if they had not heard the word of God, for the punishment of those who know it and do not act on it is all the greater. And those who knew that one or another had done something bad, and kept quiet and told us nothing of it, we would throw out of the school just like the others. They are all very much afraid of this; and those who know something come and report it to us, even if they are not more closely acquainted with us.

<!-- lines 6046-6072 -->
On the 21st those who had been at the farmers' came and asked to have their books, to go to school (for when anyone goes to a farmer to work, they must hand in their books for so long). Dorothea too came before the house to ask for her book. She was told nothing more than that she did not need a book; we would like to speak with her alone. The other came into the school; she was told at once that she could go away again, for we did not want people in the school who behave as she did. It is a sad thing about the drinking. They say that the farmers now give them more than before, so as to make them drunk if possible. So Eva too said that one day she was given a whole bowlful of wine, and when she declined it, a bottle of wine was set before her and she was told to drink and help herself; but she did not do it. Today the old woman was buried (our Jacob's mother).

<!-- lines 6072-6100 -->
On the 22nd Dorothea came and said that she had drunk at the farmers', and asked for forgiveness. We put it to her that she had not drunk, but made herself drunk. This she would not admit, and we could not see much remorse for it in her either, so that we were obliged to exclude her and to tell her that from now on we could no longer recognise her as our sister, and did not want to be called brother by her either. Those who call us brother must do the will of God, and not the works of the devil. With our Hanna we were obliged to do the same. We have looked on with her with much patience until now; she has also been with us pleading, but she has never spoken honestly with us, and now we have heard much more, that she has behaved badly in the kraals. In the evening we made it known in the meeting of the baptised, and commended these two straying sheep to the prayers of all before our dear Saviour, that they might fall down at his feet and ask for forgiveness; and that they [the baptised] might stay faithful to the Saviour, and pray him to keep them from all bad things. This caused a great stir among them.

<!-- lines 6101-6107 -->
2 others also came, who are not yet in closer fellowship with us, who had also got drunk, and asked us to take them back into the school, which we refused them this time [26].

<!-- lines 6109-6116 -->
Early on the 23rd, when we came out in front of our house, a large snake was lying by the vine in front of the house, which is fenced round with stakes. We first shot at it, and afterwards stabbed it through the head with the bayonet, at which it opened its mouth so wide that a hen's egg could easily have lain in it. It was about 4 feet long, and as thick as a man's thigh.

<!-- lines 6117-6133 -->
We have said several times already that we wanted to build a little house as a workshop for Brother Kühnel, whose foundation has already been laid. When Brother Schwinn was at the Cape lately and spoke with the Edle Heer about a meeting house, he said that we were to build nothing; everything should remain as he had found it until the 2 Commissioners-General came back from Batavia. Brother Schwinn did speak of a meeting house, but not of a workshop. Yet the answer of the Edle Heer was: everything should stay as he had found it until the 2 Commissioners came. This has put us in great perplexity about what we should now do.

<!-- lines 6136-6154 -->
Our dear brothers have already given money for various tools for Brother Kühnel and sent them to us, for example: an anvil, a grindstone, emery and so on [27]; he also brought files with him from Amsterdam; and it seems that he could earn something to support this mission, for which, as our dear brothers know, he must have a workshop. But what shall we do now? If we build, it can be said to us: "You are disobedient, because everything must stay as it is at present until the 2 Commissioners-General come from Batavia." Yet we could not get it out of our minds to contribute something to lighten the costs of the mission. We have often sat together and said: What shall we do? Many a time we thought: we will build; and then again: if we do this, we might perhaps give the whole mission a blow [28].

<!-- lines 6154-6221 -->
On the 24th early at 5 o'clock the 2 who had been excluded came. Hanna asked with many tears for forgiveness. Her words were fine, but her conduct did not agree with them. Dorothea did not yet seem to take her strayings much to heart. We had no confidence yet to take her back again. We directed her to the dear Saviour, whom she had grieved very much.

One of the others also came and asked to be taken back into the school. She too was directed to the dear Saviour, and had to stay out of the school for now. Because of the cold and the rain we put off the singing meeting today.

<!-- lines 6226-6231 -->
On the 26th the meeting was very well attended. Afterwards several came who declared their longing to be saved. A woman asked earnestly to be taken into closer care; she said that she wanted to live only for Jesus in this world.

<!-- lines 6231-6241 -->
On the 27th 6 more children, 4 women and a man came and asked for permission to go to school. Today we sent 2 children out of the school: one had behaved badly in the kraals, and the other had got drunk at a farmer's. This means of forbidding them the school does good service: they come quickly and beg and promise everything good. They also come at once and say so if one has got into a quarrel with another.

<!-- lines 6241-6245 -->
On the 28th 2 women and a man came and said that when they had been at a farmer's they had taken a glass of wine too many [29]. They were sent out of the school for the present.

<!-- lines 6245-6246 -->
On the 29th in the morning the candidates for baptism were spoken.

<!-- lines 6246-6267 -->
We cannot determine [the number of hearers] [30], but there are almost always at least 200. Now our dear brothers can imagine that, since the Hottentots are very richly provided with lice, we get plenty of them too, the more so because they sit so close to us. The schools of the women and children are now mostly held in the garden under the pear tree when the weather allows; for the house is no longer big enough for the school either, of the women as well as of the children, since each of these schools consists of about 70 persons. The men's school is in the little house (of which it has already been said) that we built last year; their number is some 60 [31], so many of them go to school. Then there are still many old people who come to the meeting, so that the number amounts to at least 200.

With this we close our diary again, and commend ourselves and our Hottentots to the prayers and remembrance of all the congregations before our dear [Lord].

## Notes

1. The German diary has verses 15 to 24.
2. Line 5534 is missing. The transcription runs *Zorgt niet, wat gy eeten of drinken zult &c. Deezen* / *zorgen niet voor den anderen Morgen*. The German diary has "But we have to pass over these texts, for the Hottentots take no thought for the next morning". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
3. *Nu wilde ik gaarn daarover denken, en onzen l: Hld ... danken*. The German diary has "Now I would gladly die thankful, and thank our dear [Lord]", where its transcription reads "thank our dear teachers" (its note 1); the Dutch has *Hld*, the Saviour.
4. *maakt zoo veel Leeven*: Dutch *leven*, "noise, din". The German transcription has *Liebes*, which its note 2 took for a misreading; the Dutch settles it.
5. *het 5 en 6 Capittel van het Evangelie: Matth:*; the German diary has "Mark".
6. Line 5616 is missing. The transcription runs *eene zulke vreugde;* / *Slaapen legge*. The German diary has "Then I wanted to ask you: when I lie down to sleep". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. *in Zyn Bedste*: probably *bedstee*, a bed or box-bed, "his bed"; the German column has *in Seinem Bett*. The German diary reads *in Deinen Vater*, which its note 4 took for *in Deinen Wunden*, "in thy wounds". To be checked against the image.
7. The German diary goes on with a young crane brought to the brethren (1 October) and a visit from young Mrs Mareis (2 October); the Dutch has neither.
8. *'t Boter Rivier*; see Bot River in the glossary.
9. *hy beval my af te stygen, en in huis te komen*, "he told me to dismount and come into the house", without a negative; the German diary has "He did not ask me to dismount". The sentences that follow (the man leaves him sitting and no one speaks to him) need the negative, which is supplied in square brackets.
10. *om halv 7*, half past 6; the German diary has half past 3, which its note 11 doubted because the brethren were below the Kloof towards midday. The Dutch time fits. *Perlemitte Rivier*: see Palmiet River in the glossary.
11. Line 5698 is missing. The transcription runs *en zoo kwamen wy er gelukkig over.* / *alwaar ik myn Paard liet voeren*. The German diary has "Towards midday we came below the Kloof, where I had my horses given some fodder". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
12. *het laatste Huis voor de Kaapsche Heide*, the sandy flats between the mountains and Cape Town. The German diary has "the last house before the Cape, Hottentots Holland".
13. Abraham Josias Sluysken, called the Edle Heer on the 23rd; see the glossary.
14. *hebben U permiſſie voor te zelven gegeeven, om U aldaar neder te zetten, waar geen Christenen Gemeente is*. The Dutch confirms the reading of the German diary's note 15: the brethren might settle only where there was no Christian congregation.
15. Line 5780 is missing. The transcription runs *Ik beſcheidde, dat wy nochtans* / *en eene Boere plaats geen Gemeente te noemen was*. The German diary has "I replied that we lived far from a Christian congregation after all". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
16. Johan Isaac Rhenius; see the glossary.
17. *De Heer Leſieur*: see Lesueur (1794) in the glossary. *Schonberg*: Mr Schönberg; see the glossary.
18. *den Heer Lucke*; see Lücke in the glossary.
19. *De Heer Heuser een Horologiemaaker*. The German diary has "Brother Heuser", probably the misreading of *Br.* for *Hr.* noted for A363595; the Dutch has *Heer*, "Mr".
20. Line 5862 is missing. The transcription runs *en wy reeden onzen* / *Heere, welke my zoo genadig heeft bewaard*. The German diary has "we rode on our way, full of praise and thanks to my good Lord, who had kept me so graciously". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
21. *Aan het Zaaien heeft het by ons niet mankeerd*, "we have not failed in sowing". The German diary has "We have not lacked food", perhaps a misreading of *Säen* as *Speiſen*.
22. Page 96b ends with *Woensdags zyn er 's morgens vroeg vier voor* / *de* (lines 5935 to 5936), and page 97a begins *ben, met eenigen moesten wy ernstig spreeken* (line 5938). The German diary has much more between these points: the order of the Wednesday and Saturday meetings, Jacob's complaint to the Landdrost about the farmer Gildenheuser (9 October), a flood (10th), the baptism of Lena (12th), a woman torn between her husband and the mission (14th), and the speaking of the baptised on the 15th, where "With some we had to speak earnestly, because they seem to be growing somewhat sleepy" matches the Dutch. The page numbers written in the Dutch manuscript (28 on image 93a, 44 on image 100a) leave room for two pages more than the images between them hold, so a leaf of the Dutch copy is probably lost here, with pages 36 and 37. To be checked against the images.
23. Line 5944 is missing. The transcription runs *er waren ook uit de* / *spoeld*, the end of *weggespoeld*, "washed away". The German diary has "it also washed away whole pieces of the Hottentots' gardens". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. This is the rain of 15 October.
24. *een Spoelkom vol Wyn*: a *spoelkom* is a slop basin or rinsing bowl. The German diary has *Pullumpen*, which its note 38 could not explain.
25. Line 6026 is missing. The transcription runs *In onze School hebben wy by het* / *Daar grooten en kleinen aan de School*. The German diary has "Now in the school, during the teaching, we gave very earnest warnings. Since [they all care] very much about the school". The words in square brackets are supplied from there, and the Dutch settles the garbled opening of the German (its note 39).
26. Line 6108, the first on page 100a, is missing; the repeat of the page keeps it at line 6165: *voor ditmaal weigerden. Den 23 vroeg, als*. The words "this time" and "Early on the 23rd" are translated from there.
27. *Aanbeeld, Slypsteen, Smergel*, "anvil, grindstone, emery". The German diary has "lathes, grindstones and screw clamps" (its note 43). The anvil is probably the one the ship *Noord Holland* was bringing in March (entry 13).
28. The German diary goes on to say that the brethren at last put the question to the Saviour by the lot, which answered that they must wait for the Commissioners-General. The Dutch does not have this.
29. *een Glas Wyn te veel*; the German diary has *im Sylab Wein zu viel*, which its note 49 could not explain.
30. *Wy kon,* (the last word of page 102a) / *niet bepaalen* (page 102b). The German diary has, between the speaking of the candidates on the 29th and "We cannot say exactly, but at least 200 always come", an account of the speaking, letters from Brother Treschow in Copenhagen, the entries for the 30th and 31st, and the size of the brethren's room (16 feet by 12). The Dutch does not have them; the catchword is missing too. The words in square brackets are supplied from the sense.
31. *eenige 60*; the German diary has "some 30".
32. The German diary has 13 candidates for baptism, which gives a total of 30 against its stated 32 (its note 53). The Dutch has 15, and its figures add up to 32.
