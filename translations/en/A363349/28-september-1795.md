<!-- lines 10981-11002 -->
On 1 September a Hottentot brought us word, who had got leave at the Cape to serve with a farmer in our neighbourhood. He told us that all our Hottentots were alive; of these, 4 men, among them our Jacob, arrived here on the 7th, having got leave to visit their wives and children for some days. As they left there, 15 more English ships had arrived at Muizenberg, and the English fleet was now 35 ships strong. On 8 September Baas Teunis sent a letter which he had received from the Edle Heeren, in which it is ordered that the wives and children of the Hottentots present at the Cape are to be provided with food and victuals, and that he is to consult with us about it, since we are best acquainted with their needs; and the report was to be signed by him and us jointly. He also asked us to see to the enclosed letter to Andreas Otto, in which he is ordered to bring us a load of flour, to distribute among the Hottentots and their children.

<!-- lines 11003-11020 -->
On 9 September we had a pleasant conversation with our Jacob about our Hottentots at the Cape. He said "that they came together every morning and evening, and he sang some verses with them". The other people, seeing this, asked him why it was done. He answered: "In the evening we thank God that he has kept us from all harm through the day, and at the same time pray him for his gracious keeping through the night. In the morning we thank him for his protection that we have known in the night, and commend ourselves to it for the day." On that occasion he also reminded our Hottentots that we were bound to pray for our teachers, that God would take them into his keeping, since they do the same for us.

<!-- lines 11021-11039 -->
On 10 September we had all those called together among whom flour was to be distributed by order of the Edle Heeren, and wrote down their names. There were 42 women and 74 children, together 116 persons, all of them relations and kin of Hottentot men who are doing service at the Cape. Besides these there are many more women who have no husband [, and many children who have no father] [1]; and also many old and sick men, whose wives and children do not come under the number above. Further, there are many more who left here on 18 July, whose number is just as great as the number of those who live with us; besides those who live at Baas Teunis's, who must also be provided with food [2]. So Brother Kühnel rode to Baas Teunis on 11 September, to make arrangements with him about the distribution of the flour ordered by the Edle Heeren.

<!-- lines 11039-11053 -->
Having had no Communion with our Hottentots since 3 July, and they having declared a great longing for it, we decided to hold this sacrament with them again, and to fix 12 September for it, because our Jacob could not wait for the proper Communion day [3], having to go back to the Cape. They all melted in tears when this was made known to them, and Jacob in particular was thankful. An excellent peace of God went with the partaking of Holy Communion.

<!-- lines 11054-11100 -->
On 14 September Baas Teunis came and brought a written order from the Edle Heeren to bring together as many Hottentots as possible and send them to the Cape. All who were here had to appear, and of them 10 were picked out, two included who had otherwise got leave to stay here. Among these was our Abraham too. Now none of our baptised stayed at home but David alone, because of his illness; we were also allowed to keep our farmhand. In accordance with Baas Teunis's order, messengers riding round were sent out everywhere to look for Hottentots; for the Cape was being attacked by the English with all their might. On 15 September the Hottentots ordered out yesterday left, and so did our two brothers Jacob and Abraham. We gave them the necessary admonitions for the road. But now everything that can so much as crawl has gone [4]. Baas Teunis sent us a copy of the English commander-in-chief's summons to surrender the town of the Cape willingly, with the answer that followed it, that they would defend themselves to the utmost and lend no ear to any summons [5]. So all the greater was our astonishment, early on 18 September, to hear from 4 Hottentots coming from the town that the town had surrendered, that all the Hottentots had got their discharge, and that each Hottentot had received a rixdollar. Towards midday 18 men came at once, and then 2, 3 or 4 together, and among them our Isaac too. All agreed that not one of our Hottentots had fallen in the fighting. According to what they said, the English had marched from Muizenberg, which they had taken, to Cape Town, after the burghers had fallen back close to the Cape. On the 13th [6] the Hottentots marched into Cape Town, the farmers for the most part went home, and the Hottentots were released from their military service. Now we are, as far as we know, under the supreme authority of the English, which was confirmed by a messenger of the Edle Heeren to the Landdrost of Swellendam, who passed through here [7].

<!-- lines 11101-11129 -->
On 19 September many more Hottentots came home, and on 20 September we missed only very few, so that there were as many present in the meeting as we had not yet seen [8]; for many others came too, who had not been here before, and asked leave to [move here] with their families. [We told them that they might wait a little] longer, since we now have new authorities, of whose disposition we ought first to have closer knowledge [9]. The meeting had to be held outside in front of the house, because the house could not hold a fourth part of the hearers. A great stirring of hearts was to be perceived among them. Many said that they could not thank God enough for keeping them safe. Many times the fighting had been such as if it were raining bullets, which fell among them like sand, without their being harmed by them. A bullet had passed between the legs of one of them without injury. In general the Hottentots have the renown of being brave soldiers [10]. They formed the vanguard everywhere, and were followed by the burghers at a reasonable distance. On 23 September our Jacob came back from the Cape too, having been unable to come sooner because of a commission given him by Baas Teunis [11].

<!-- lines 11130-11153 -->
On the 24th we received through Baas Teunis a proclamation from the English commander, in which the inhabitants are released from their obligation to the Company and assured that the best care would be taken of them, and that the grievances of everyone would be heard. This proclamation was signed by Major-General Craag. With it we also received the 13 articles of capitulation, among which was also the article that all religions should be respected undisturbed. These articles were signed by General Clairk [12]. In the following days many farmers came to hire Hottentots. We closed this month with the heartfelt wish that a way might soon be opened to have free communication with our brothers in the congregations. For the present we enjoy rest from the side of the farmers and Nationals; for the change that has happened in the supreme government makes them timid and afraid to carry out their purpose [13]. But now and then one still hears threatening utterances.
