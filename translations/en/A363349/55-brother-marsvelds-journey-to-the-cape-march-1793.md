Translated from the Dutch diplomatic transcription and checked against the German translation in the normalised column and against the German diary of the same months (A363595, entry 05). The diarists' words for Khoekhoe people ("Hottentots") and Xhosa people ("Kaffers") are kept as they wrote them; see `glossary.csv`. Square brackets mark editorial additions.

<!-- lines 15105-15121 -->
"After I had made the necessary arrangements with my brothers Daniel Schwinn and Johann Christian Kühnel, I went, strengthened by their brotherly blessing, to Soetemelksvlei (where Baas Teunis lives) to travel to the town. He received me very kindly, and after all the necessary arrangements for our journey had been made, we set out on 6 March with two wagons [1]. At 6 o'clock in the evening we met a cart whose driver had letters for Baas Teunis, which he handed over, adding that he also had a small cask of wine in his cart. Baas Teunis said to me, 'The wine is for you'; and when I answered that I had not ordered any wine, he gave me a letter to read that was enclosed in his, from which it was clear enough that it was so." (The letter was written to Brother Marsveld, and had the same contents as the one already mentioned on 19 March [2].) "I had the cask and the letter taken to Soetemelksvlei, and we continued on our way.

<!-- lines 15122-15153 -->
"Driving through the night, we crossed the Bot River early on the 7th at 4 o'clock [3], outspanned the oxen so that they could graze, made a fire, drank coffee under the open sky, and then went to sleep on our wagon until 9 o'clock. When we wanted to inspan our oxen again, they were gone. Baas Teunis, I and 4 more Hottentots [went to look for them, and found them only at 1 o'clock]; then in the afternoon at 4 o'clock [we inspanned], after which we continued our journey further [4]. When we came to the foot of the mountain called Houhoek [5], we went forward very slowly, and when we had driven halfway up the mountain our oxen could go no further, which forced us to put 20 other oxen in front. On foot one needs 1½ hours to climb to the top of this steep mountain. I went on foot, and when I had reached the top of the mountain I had to turn back again, for the wagon had stuck among the rocks not far from the place mentioned before and could not be brought forward [6]. So all of us who were with the wagon had to help push it back, and we did not reach the top of this steep and rocky mountain until 7 o'clock in the evening. It gives those who sit on the wagon such jolts that the very bowels are hard shaken by them. We continued on our way, and at half past two in the night came to the Hottentots Holland Kloof [7]. Here the oxen had to pull the wagon up over steep rocky steps, each step as high as a chair, and we came to the top of the mountain at 4 o'clock. We went down the mountain on the other side on foot, while the weather was so raw and the wind so strong that we had to hold on to one another [8].

<!-- lines 15154-15173 -->
"When I had got down safely, I sat down in a cave in the kloof, poured out my heart to the Saviour with many tears, and begged him earnestly to strengthen my brothers who had stayed at home and to give them what they needed. I was filled with shame within myself that he has counted me, a poor sinner, worthy to be called to so weighty a service, when on coming to the congregation I desired nothing else than just to become a blessed brother in its midst and to earn my living with the work of my hands, however humble it might be. Thinking this over [9], I said to the dear Saviour: 'Thou hast chosen for thyself something lowly for thy pleasure' [10]. At this I felt the nearness of my Lord in my heart in the most powerful way, and so went all the way down the mountain refreshed and comforted. At 7 o'clock in the morning we were safely down the mountain. When we had outspanned the oxen to rest and graze a little, we made a fire, cooked coffee, and afterwards lay down under the open sky to sleep [11].

<!-- lines 15174-15191 -->
"At 12 o'clock midday Baas Teunis asked me whether we should not have a piece of meat roasted. I answered: 'But we have no gridiron to roast it on.' But he called a Hottentot and ordered him to roast a piece of the meat we had brought with us [12]. I thought to myself: 'How will that go?' But see, the Hottentot took a little stick, split it at the end, bent it into a fork with two prongs, and stuck a piece of meat on it; (he made a fire to roast the meat on it) and when one side was done, he laid the meat on the ashes and turned it once more. When this was done and the meat so roasted, it was laid on the grass, as we had neither table nor plates. I took a piece of rock that I could just about lift, to serve me as a table. At 3 o'clock we drove on and came to Hottentots Holland, where we put up at the widow Morgel's [13].

<!-- lines 15192-15211 -->
"She was very friendly and offered us all kinds of refreshment. I asked only for a glass of water, which she brought me at once. After a stay of half an hour we continued our journey, and at 8 o'clock arrived at Jacob Konterman's, where Baas Teunis had meant to pay a visit [14]. He was very friendly, and asked Baas Teunis who I was. Hearing from him that I was one of the 3 missionaries who had been sent to the Hottentots to preach to them the Gospel of Jesus Christ, he was very glad to make my acquaintance, and I had to drink a glass of wine with him. He is an old man who could still remember our predecessor, Brother Schmidt [15], and we had a pleasant conversation together on the matter of our salvation. When I asked him where in future, on my way to the town, I could and might call in to spend a night, as Brother Schmidt too in his time often took his night's lodging with a farmer, he answered with great warmth: 'With me, with me, my friend!' After an hour [of rest here we set out again] and drove through the night [16].

<!-- lines 15212-15220 -->
"On 9 March we came to the Diep [17], where we outspanned our oxen by a poor Negro hut and asked for a pot to cook meat in; but it was so poor and broken that little use could be made of it, and so the cooking turned out very badly. At 8 o'clock we drove on [18], and the same evening arrived, happily and safe and sound, at our friend Martin Schmidt's in Cape Town. He was as glad as a child, and I could not tell him enough about our settlement at Baviaanskloof [19].

<!-- lines 15221-15253 -->
"On 10 March a farmer and his wife came to visit him, having heard that a Herrnhuter was lodging with him. On seeing me they looked me over from head to foot. Then, when we were sitting at table, I began to speak of something concerning our salvation, which so changed their minds that they asked me on no account to pass by their house if I ever came into their neighbourhood. I answered: 'But I am a Herrnhuter, after all.' 'Yes,' they replied, 'that will not disturb us.' On the 11th I went to pay my respects to the Commandant, Mr Rhenius [20], who was very glad to see me. Having no time left to speak with me more fully today, since there were still others he had to hear, he said to me: 'Come back to me tomorrow evening at 7 o'clock, and then we will talk together until 9 o'clock' [21]. So I went away again, and he accompanied me as far as the street, which is a great mark of honour. From here I went to Colonel Gordon, who received me with these words: 'Why, Brother, are you here again? There is a chair; sit down, and tell me how you are getting on at your place.' When I had told him one thing and another, he said: 'Here are grapes; do eat them.' As I was taking a big bunch of them, some distinguished officers came into the room, for whom I rose from my chair to pay them my respectful compliments; but the Colonel said: 'Brother, just go on eating your grapes undisturbed.' After staying half an hour with this gentleman, I went to the honourable Commissioners [22], who likewise honoured me with a gracious audience, and though others had to speak with them standing, they had me take a seat on the chair, to tell them in full how things were with us at our place; which also gave occasion to speak of the Spirit [23]. When I took my leave, they gave me their hands and wished me many blessings from God.

<!-- lines 15254-15285 -->
"On the 12th I visited the awakened souls here who are joined together, in their houses [24]. On the 13th I went to the Commandant, Mr Rhenius, according to the wish he had expressed yesterday [25]. He offered me a pipe of tobacco, which I accepted, and asked whether I would rather drink wine or beer. I chose beer. He showed me a valuable collection of paintings, and also of shells and stones found in this country. On this occasion I could also speak with him about our dwelling place, as I had brought our written documents from the authorities with me for that purpose. When I asked him whether we could not buy the place, he answered: 'That is not necessary, for the place belongs to you as it is.' To the question whether we had to pay anything for it yearly, the answer was: 'Nothing!' At this I said that I wished this matter had been expressed in words in our documents; for though I was assured that during the lifetime of Your Honour no difficulty on this point was to be feared, yet Your Honour's successor might perhaps demand our place back, and we be driven out of it. To this he answered that even if a new Governor were to come, they could not do that, because it had been so ordered by the Assembly of Seventeen [26]. Question: 'How do things stand with buying and selling between us and the Hottentots?' Answer: 'You may without hesitation buy and sell everything that belongs to your own consumption and is needed for it, provided only that you refrain from carrying on trade with it, as I myself do too, giving them in exchange for what I receive from them what may be of use to them.' When I took my leave, he gave me a slave to take me to my lodgings with a burning lantern, because it was very dark, and invited me to come and dine with him at midday the day after tomorrow.

<!-- lines 15286-15309 -->
"On the 14th I visited the Reformed ministers, with one of whom I had to drink tea [27]. I was invited to a midday meal with Colonel Gordon, who promised to get me a dog, which one needs very much in this country. On the 15th I went to dine at midday with the Commandant, Mr Rhenius. The conversation ran chiefly on [the politics of the fatherland. His wife was] very pleased to see me and to speak with me [28]; both showed me as much respect as if I had been a learned minister. After the meal he showed me his paintings and curiosities once more, after which I took my leave, and also visited the Lutheran minister Kolver [29], with whom I had a pleasant conversation about the sufferings of the Saviour. On the 16th I visited Colonel Gordon once more, who ordered his adjutant to supply me with powder and flints for a gun. To that end I went with him into his garden, where I had to drink a glass of Cape wine [30]. As I left, he wished me many blessings from God. On the 19th I paid my last farewell visits to the Commandant at the Castle and to Colonel Gordon, who, besides many good wishes, assured me that on their side they would do everything possible to further the preaching of the Gospel [31]. After I had also taken leave today of all good friends in the town, I began my return journey with Baas Teunis on the 20th.

<!-- lines 15310-15337 -->
"At 9 o'clock we came to a country estate called the Brewery [32], where we had to stay for some time. From here I visited the minister van Lier, who was staying at a country estate close by. He reached me his hand, since he could not speak because of his great weakness. I stayed there only a short time, but I felt a very blessed feeling in his presence. We spent the night at the Brewery, and one party after another came driving up to visit the good Domine van Lier. On the 21st, early at 4 o'clock, a messenger came with the news that he had died [33]. At 8 o'clock we drove on, came on the 22nd to the Hottentots Holland Kloof, and early on the 23rd to the warm bath [34], where we stayed the day. Curiosity moved me to go into this bath once, but I could not stay in it long, because the water was so exceedingly hot that anyone going into it suddenly was in danger of scalding himself. At 9 o'clock in the evening we set out on the road again, came in the night of the 24th to Soetemelksvlei, and I arrived on the 25th at 12 o'clock at night with my dear brothers at Baviaanskloof [35], with a heart filled with praise and thanks for the gracious guidance and keeping of my kind Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ. [It was] a very difficult and dangerous journey. Baas Teunis's many affairs were the reason why I had to stay so long in the town."

## Notes

1. The German diary says that Brother Marsveld went to Soetemelksvlei on 4 March and left with Baas Teunis on the 6th "at 1 o'clock in the afternoon". Compare the Dutch *d. 1 Maart* in entry 52, note 10.
2. The words in parentheses are the copyist's or editor's; they speak of Brother Marsveld in the third person. The letter is the one from Mr Teifseur, which reached Baviaanskloof on 19 March (entry 54, note 2). The German diary names the sender here (*Eß-Le sieur*) and says he had not given the brethren a breakfast before they left.
3. *de Botter rivier*: the Bot River; see `glossary.csv`. The German diary has "on the 7th at half past midnight", and before this tells that an ox fell from the second wagon.
4. Line 15128 is missing. The transcription runs *Baas T:, Ik, en nog 4 Hot⸗* / *dan Namiddag te 4 Uure*. The German diary has "Baas Teunis, I and four Hottentots went to look for them, but did not find them until 1 o'clock in the afternoon. At 4 o'clock we drove on"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there.
5. Houhoek, the pass on the road from the Overberg to the Cape; see `glossary.csv`. The German diary has "when we had climbed for a quarter of an hour" where the Dutch has "halfway up the mountain".
6. *niet ver van de voorgenoemde Plaats*, "not far from the place mentioned before". The German diary has "not far from the murderous spot", perhaps a misreading of the same words. To be checked against the images.
7. The German diary adds that they crossed the Palmiet River at 2 o'clock.
8. *terwyl het Weër zoo guur*: *guur* is the Dutch for raw, bleak weather. The German diary has *es wehete ein Poeter und ſo heftiger Wind*, whose *Poeter* its translation could not explain (A363595, entry 05, note 3); it may be a misreading of a word for the weather such as *guur*. The German also says that Brother Marsveld had to take off his cloak.
9. *Dit hardenkende*: probably *Dit overdenkende*, "thinking this over".
10. *Gy hebt U wat Slegts tot vermaak uitverkooren*: *Slegts* is probably *slechts*, "poor, lowly", and *tot vermaak*, "for [thy] pleasure". The German diary has "Dear Saviour, thou hast chosen something poor for thy servant". The Dutch leaves out the German's "I was a wonder to myself that I should still live to see such a thing".
11. The transcription has a stray *Sla* before *onder den blooten Hemel*. The Dutch leaves out the German diary's description of the sparks struck by the locked wheels of the wagon coming down the pass.
12. Line 15178 is empty, at the foot of page 252b; the sentence runs on without a gap.
13. *de Wed.e Morkel*: the widow Morgel; see `glossary.csv` (Morgel).
14. *Jacob konterman*: the German diary has *Jacob Contermann*. Not identified.
15. *onzen Voorganger Fr. Schmidt*, and below *gelyk ook Fr. Schmidt*: *Fr.* is probably a misreading of *Br.*, "Brother". Georg Schmidt, who left the Cape in 1744.
16. Line 15210 is missing. The transcription runs *Na een Uur* / *en Reeden den Nagt door*. The German diary has "After we had rested here for an hour, we set out again, drove through the night once more"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there.
17. *de Diep*: perhaps the Diep River, which runs into Table Bay north of Cape Town, or a place on the Cape Flats. The German diary has *de Duÿn*, "the dunes". To be checked against the images.
18. The German diary has "at 3 o'clock". It also tells more about the broken pot, which would have held 6 cans if whole but held only 2.
19. *en ik hem met genoeg ... vertellen kon*: *met* is a misreading of *niet*, "not". The German diary gives Martin Schmidt's address as the *Saxſtraat*, which the Dutch leaves out.
20. Johan Isaac Rhenius, acting head of the government; see `glossary.csv`. The German diary says that Brother Marsveld went to the Castle.
21. The German diary has "at half past 6".
22. The Commissioners-General Nederburgh and Frykenius; see entry 49, note 7.
23. *'t welk gelegenheid gaf ook van Geest te ſpreeken*. This supports the German normalised *Geist*, "spirit", for the transcription's *Geis* (A363595, entry 05, note 13); the sense seems to be that the talk also turned to spiritual things.
24. The society of awakened people in Cape Town described in entry 49.
25. *volgens deszelfs op giſteren beloofde verlangen*: Rhenius had asked him on the 11th to come "tomorrow evening"; both copies put the visit on the 13th.
26. *de Vergadering der Zeventien*: the Heeren XVII, the directors of the Dutch East India Company. The German diary has "Even if 10 governors came".
27. The German diary adds that the ministers were "very dry", and names the one with whom he drank tea as Vleck.
28. Line 15292 is missing. The transcription runs *De Geſprekken liepen voornamelyk over* / *zeer Vergenoegd my te zien en te ſpreeken*. The German diary has "the conversation was chiefly about the politics of the fatherland. His wife was very pleased to see me and talk with me"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there.
29. *den Lutherſchen Pred: kolver*: probably Andreas Lutgerus Kolver, minister of the Lutheran church in Cape Town; the German diary has *Paſtor Colver*.
30. The transcription reads *Ik ging ten ſlot dien einde met hem in zyn Tuin, waar ik een Glas Kaap Wyn Ste moeſt drinken*, with the stray words *ten ſlot* and *Ste*. The German diary has "to his house, where I had to sit down and drink a little glass of sweet wine". To be checked against the image.
31. The transcription has struck-out or repeated words here (*de ha*, *van de hun*), which are left out.
32. *de Brouwery*: a country estate at Newlands outside Cape Town.
33. The transcription reads *D. 23.*; the German diary has the 21st, which is the date of van Lier's death, and the next day is the 22nd. *23* is a slip.
34. The hot springs at present-day Caledon; see `glossary.csv`. The German diary has "at 3 o'clock in the morning".
35. The German diary has "at half past 11 at night".
