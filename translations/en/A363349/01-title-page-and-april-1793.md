To the Gentlemen of the Unity [1]. Diary from the Cape, no. 1803–05. 1.) From 1 April to 31 August 1793. 2.) From 1 September 1793 to 9 April 1794. Note: the diary from 10 April to 31 August 1794 is missing. P A T [2]

<!-- lines 00012-00016 -->
Diary of the 3 brethren Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn and Johann Christiaan Kühnel at Baviaanskloof on the Sergeants River in Africa, from 1 April to 31 August 1793 [3].

<!-- lines 00017-00020 -->
On 1 April at 10 o'clock we held a church meeting, in which the Gospel was spoken about; from now on it is to be held every Sunday.

<!-- lines 00021-00029 -->
On 2 April we began the school, as agreed in our conference: in the morning at 8 o'clock with the men, then with the children until midday. In the afternoon from 2 to 3 o'clock with the children again, and then with the women, after which the instruction follows. As the children's school began today, the parents brought their children to school solemnly, which was very lovely to see. A farmer also sent us two hens as a present.

<!-- lines 00030-00032 -->
On the 3rd he came himself to visit us for the second time. He seems to be a decent man and treated us very kindly.

<!-- lines 00033-00039 -->
On the 4th a Hottentot came from a day's journey away and told us that when he let the farmer he had served know that his time of service was now up, and that he meant to give notice so as to go and learn at Baviaanskloof, the farmer had him made to lie down on the ground and had him beaten pitifully.

<!-- lines 00040-00045 -->
On 5 April our pupils came diligently to school and put all their strength into learning. When they come home to the kraals, they sit with the book in their hands, one teaching the other.

<!-- lines 00046-00055 -->
On 6 April we had a free day, of which we have two a week, namely Wednesdays and Saturdays. On these two days we hold no school, but the instruction in the evening goes on. The farmers are already beginning to know that there is no school on these two days, and they use them to come and visit us. On these two days we can also do some work in our house and garden, if we are not kept from it by visitors.

<!-- lines 00056-00070 -->
On 7 April, after the instruction, we saw a Hottentot woman going home weeping and sighing to God to have mercy on her, and we notice already that some are becoming uneasy and weep and plead for grace and the forgiveness of sins. The woman said: "I feel so strange." When we asked what was the matter with her, she answered that she could not say it in words, but pointing to her heart she said: "Here I feel such a wonderful feeling." We asked whether she diligently thought over the sufferings of the Lord Jesus, which he had to undergo on the cross for our sins, and she answered with tears in her eyes: "Yes!"

<!-- lines 00071-00075 -->
On 10 April Brother Schwinn rode to a farmer's wife to buy some vegetables for us as a store against the winter. [He came back] without having done his business [4]: when he came to Baas Teunis at the post, he heard that she had gone to the Cape, so he went no further on his way and turned back home.

<!-- lines 00076-00082 -->
On 11 April a Hottentot woman who does not live with us, but has attended our school and the instruction a few times, came and said that her husband would not let her go to school with us.

<!-- lines 00083-00091 -->
On 12 April we had many visitors. Before midday a Juffrouw came with her son, and another distinguished, wealthy woman who lives two hours' ride on horseback from here. After midday Juffrouw Teunis came too, with her son-in-law and children, together with another farmer's wife. They brought us all kinds of things, among them planks, which were very welcome to us, since we needed them badly.

<!-- lines 00092-00116 -->
On 13 April we held a conference to settle the most necessary matters concerning our school. For the days are now beginning to grow shorter, and the number of women who would like to learn is growing larger, so we are obliged to give up the afternoon school for the children, because otherwise the time for the instruction would become too late: several people who come from places an hour away attend it and have to go home again. So the school for the men now begins in the morning at 7 o'clock, that of the children shortly after, until 12 o'clock. At 1 o'clock the women come to school, and then the instruction follows. The children have behaved so far as orderly and quietly as one could wish. Their number is growing fast: in a time of 14 days it has risen from 15 (as many as we began with) to 26. They are very eager to learn, and we are now beginning to teach the children as well as the old people to sing verses. At the first attempt we did wish that some of our brothers and sisters had been present; they would have been amazed at the lovely beginning.

<!-- lines 00117-00120 -->
On 14 April after church a farmer came on horseback with a Juffrouw who lives close to the Cape, to visit us. As it was just the time of the midday meal, they ate with us and then went on their way.

<!-- lines 00121-00126 -->
On 15 April an old Hottentot woman came with her son, whom we have taken into service, together with another woman who has 5 children, three of whom will go to school here. The first is going to build a house close to us, because her son lives with us.

<!-- lines 127-131 -->
On 16 April a Hottentot came of his own accord to complain to us that a quarrel had arisen between him and his brother, and that it had gone so far that they had struck each other; he now felt great remorse for it.

<!-- lines 132-138 -->
On 17 April 3 Hottentots who live a long way from us came to ask us whether they might attend our evening meeting. In this way we are becoming better and better known. One of our pupils came after the meeting to ask us whether he should say his prayer to God standing or kneeling.

<!-- lines 139-170 -->
On 18 April a captain of the Hottentots came to visit us. He lives under the jurisdiction of Graaff-Reinet [5], which borders on the land of the Kaffers [6], and needs 4 weeks to make the journey here. [He had come to the Cape] [7] to complain about the cruelty that the farmers practise on the Hottentots. He told us among other things that they take the Hottentots' cattle away from them, sinfully abuse their wives, and make slaves of their children. When the men ask for payment for their service, the farmer has them laid on the ground and beaten pitifully: that is their wage. The government at the Cape acknowledged that the captain was in the right [8], and gave him a letter to take to the Landdrost, ordering him in it to put a stop to this punishable ill-treatment. But, he added, since the Landdrost and the farmers are on good terms with one another, he did not believe that this letter would be of much use. The government does not approve of such ill-treatment, but because the Hottentots live too far from the Cape and are thereby prevented from bringing their complaints and grievances to the government's knowledge, and the Landdrost knows this well, he does as he pleases in the meantime. The captain asked in the same way for permission to attend the evening meeting.

<!-- lines 171-186 -->
On 19 April he came again with 7 more Hottentots who had made the journey with him and were now going home again with him. They were very attentive and wished that they too might be taught. A captain of the Hottentots of this kind is appointed by the government, but receives no income as captain. When the Hottentots have reason to complain, they go to him, whose office it is to deal with it, or to the Drost, or to the government at the Cape itself. When he is appointed captain he receives from the government a cane with a brass knob on it, on which the captain's name is engraved; this is the mark of his captaincy.

<!-- lines 186-204 -->
On 20 April we received a letter from Baas Teunis's son, in which the old man let us know that there would be preaching at his place at the post of Soetemelksvlei the next day. Baas Teunis had spoken to us about it before and wished that all 3 of us might come up to this service, and one could see that it mattered a great deal to him. Although we had no great desire to go, since that church lies 5 hours' walk from us and we had a church service of our own to see to, we still wanted to try to please him in this, and Brothers Schwinn and Kühnel decided to ride there on horseback, while Brother Marsveld would see to the church service at home.

<!-- lines 205-250 -->
On 21 April Brother Schwinn was kept from it by an indisposition that came over him, and so Brother Kühnel was left to ride there alone, with a Hottentot as his guide. The minister of Swellendam [9] preached, speaking on the first 4 verses of the 4th chapter of Paul's epistle to the Thessalonians. "I had not expected such an exposition and explanation from him," Brother Kühnel writes; "about 30 persons heard it. He asked me what was to be expected of the Hottentots, and when I answered that we had reason to hope for much good of them, he showed great satisfaction at it. He asked me to visit him, as he has already visited us once. If he did not live so far from us (it takes 2 days to get there on horseback), [we would] do so [10]. Another man came to tell me that his mother had been at the Cape and had received a little parcel for us from Mr Martinus Schmidt, which had come for us from the fatherland. 'From the fatherland!' I replied; 'that cannot be.' But he assured me once more that it was from the fatherland, and that he had handed the parcel to Baas Teunis. I went at once to fetch it, and when I had cut it open with great eagerness I found in it the weekly reports from the Unity Elders' Conference, together with other congregation reports and letters from Zeist. Although it was now very late, I would not stay the night there but hurried home to share my joy with my brothers, although Baas Teunis and his wife were not at all pleased with this." This news came to us quite unexpectedly, since by our own rough reckoning we did not expect it before the month of August. So much the greater was our joy and thankfulness. A letter from Brother Rothe told us of the falling asleep of our dear Brother Spangenberg [11].

<!-- lines 251-254 -->
On 22 April the captain mentioned above attended our evening meeting again with his travelling companions, after which he took a grateful leave and went on his journey.

<!-- lines 255-269 -->
On 23 April a farmer who lives a day's journey from us came on horseback to visit us, asking permission to take one of the Hottentots who live with us into his service for a time. For none of the Hottentots who live here goes away without our knowledge and permission, even if it is only to a distance of an hour, which surprises us all the more because we have never required this of them. The children do the same when for one reason or another they have to leave the school because of a bodily need.

<!-- lines 270-271 -->
On 24 April we were busy painting our windows and doors.

<!-- lines 272-285 -->
On 25 April we began to read out the congregation news we had received, very thankful that it had come into our hands so fortunately. This can only be done in the evening, after everything else, because we are busy with school all day long, one school following the other without any interval, so that all 3 of us cannot be together. In the weekly reports we found the painful news of the devastating fire that has struck the congregations at Gnadenfrei and Gnadenfeld. We fervently implored the Lord to be a comfort and help to these our beloved brothers and sisters in this dreadful calamity.

<!-- lines 286-303 -->
On 26 April we made a bell out of a piece of sheet copper that we had bought at the Cape to put to various uses in our house, so as to bring our pupils together to school by it; until now we have had to call them ourselves, and for that alone, which was a great burden to us. It took us a lot of work to bring 3 pieces of flat sheet copper into the round form of a bell by soldering them together, but it succeeded, and we have now fastened a little bell to a post and set it up. It has been in use for 8 days and serves well, even if its sound, as one has reason to expect, is not very clear and melodious.

<!-- lines 304-312 -->
On 27 April, being Saturday and so holding no school, we were busy planting potatoes and peas. [So far we have had fine, warm weather, but the nights are cool. The rainy] season [12] is late this year; it usually begins to come in the middle of April. On the whole it is a very dry year, and people are longing for rain, so that grass may spring up for the cattle.

<!-- lines 313-325 -->
On 28 April the mason who did the work on our house was present in our church for the second time, and asked if he might come again the following Sunday. He seems to have unrest in his mind, and said that he would try from now on to lead a more orderly life. He is working at present at a place only an hour from here, but will soon have to leave to work further away. He is a Hollander by birth, but has lived in this country for many years and has led a very disorderly life until now.

<!-- lines 395-416 -->
After the meal [13] we felt like taking a walk and going to a wood that lies an hour from us, in the middle of the mountains. We took the Hottentot who is in our service with us, because the Hottentots know their way very well in these mountains. Each of us took a musket with him, thinking that we might meet baboons or wolves or other animals that could be shot. Directly behind our house we had to climb mountains, until after much climbing we at last reached the wood where we wanted to be. The wood itself is only small. There are heavy trees in it, but they are not tall, and nearly all crooked, so they cannot be used for anything but firewood. There is no possibility, however, of getting the trees out, because of the mountains that surround them: one can hardly get there and back on foot, so it would be even less possible to haul the trees away from there with oxen.

<!-- lines 416-474 -->
As we came near the wood our Hottentot said: "I must whistle to lure a bird that will bring us to a bees' nest." He had hardly done so when a bird came to the edge of the wood and piped too. "Now," said the Hottentot, "we must follow the bird; it will surely bring us to a nest of bees." We did so, and the bird always flew a couple of trees ahead of us. When we had gone quietly on for some way, following the bird, it stayed sitting still at the top of a tree and piped, just as a person whistles. "Now," said the Hottentot, "there must be a bees' nest here." Looking the tree over, we found that it was indeed hollow and that there was a nest of bees in the hollow. The Hottentot said: "Now we need fire to be able to get the honey out." But we had neither a flint and steel to make fire, nor a little vessel to put the honey in. Then the Hottentot said: "If only we have fire to get the honey out, we will take a few twigs from the trees, lay a honeycomb on them, and carry it home like that." To get fire, then, we fired a musket, took the cord on which the powder horn hung over our shoulders, unpicked it, put powder on the pan of the musket, set it off, and so made fire. Now the Hottentot climbed the tree, where the nest was found high up [14], and took a burning piece of wood, whose smoke made the bees leave their nest, so that we could get at it. But when we wanted to take the honey out, it turned out to be a young nest that had not long begun to be worked, so that the quantity of honey we found in it was no more than we could eat on the spot. In this way, however, one often gets a rich haul, and the farmers who live further inland can fill great casks with honey from it. All the farmers make use of the bird mentioned above; from this it has got the name tell-tale and bee-eater [15]. One must beware, however, that this same bird does not bring one to a wolf [16], for it leads people there too by its piping. But when it knows of neither a wolf nor a bees' nest, it will not lead anyone. When it has brought someone to a bees' nest, [and the honey has been taken out, it eats] what is left [17] and has its fill of it. For the rest, we saw nothing on our walk that we could have shot.

<!-- lines 475-476 -->
On 29 April a farmer named Conrad, who lives two hours from us, visited us.

<!-- lines 477-489 -->
On 30 April a couple of Bastard Hottentots came to visit us. These are people who have a Christian for a father and a Hottentot woman for a mother. One can tell them by their hair: the real Hottentots have short, woolly hair, but the Bastards have proper long hair [18]. They told us that at the place where they live there were many Bastards who would like to come here and learn. They only thought of first earning something, so as to be able to live on it here, and they were minded to do so. They attended the meeting in the evening.

## Notes

1. *D. Hrn Unitas*: probably *Den Heeren [der] Unitas*, "to the gentlemen of the Unity", as the normalised column reads it. This page is the cover of the volume. The number *1803–05* is probably an archive number; it is left as written.
2. *P A T*: perhaps initials or a shelf mark; left untranslated.
3. This diary, in Dutch, covers the months for which the German diary A363595 has lost its first nine pages (images 56 to 64 of A363595; see A363595 entry 06, note 4, and entry 07, note 1). From 21 April onward the German resumes, and the two can be compared.
4. The transcription reads *en onverrigter Zaake [margin insertion] maar bÿ Baas Teunis op de post komende*: the words in the margin are not transcribed. The sense is that he came back without having bought anything. To be checked against the image.
5. *Graaf Reneth*: Graaff-Reinet, the frontier district and drostdy.
6. *Cafferen*: the diarists' word for Xhosa people. It became a severe racial slur in South Africa; it is kept here because it is what the diary says. See the glossary.
7. Line 143 is missing from the transcription; line 142 ends *te volbren-* (for *volbrengen*, "to make [the journey]"), and line 144 begins *over de Onbarmhartigheid*. Something like "he had come to the Cape" is needed for the sense; the German normalised column also lacks the line. To be checked against the image.
8. *Kende den Capiteijn regt toe*, "granted the captain [his] right", that is, found in his favour.
9. *De Ds van Swellendam*: *Ds* is *Dominee*, the minister (of the Reformed church at Swellendam). The German diary (A363595 entry 07, note 1) could not name the preacher, because its page is lost; this passage settles it. The text was 1 Thessalonians 4:1–4.
10. Lines 224 and 225 are missing from the numbering; the sentence runs *Indien hÿ niet zo ver van ons afgeleegen woonde (hebbende 2 Dagen van nooden om te paard derwaards te Komen zulks te doen*. The German diary has "If it were not so far, we would do it too". "We would" is supplied from there.
11. The Dutch names *Br Spangenberg*; the German diary has *Bruder Joſeph*, the name by which August Gottlieb Spangenberg was known in the Church. He died on 18 September 1792. Brother Rothe has not been identified.
12. Line 307 is missing from the first copy (image 6), so that sentence reads *Tot nog toe hebben wÿ schoon & tÿd blÿft dit Jaar lang agter*. The second copy (image 7, lines 373 to 374) has it whole: *Tot nog toe hebben wÿ schoon & warm weer, egter zÿn de nagten koel. De regentÿd blÿft...*. The words in square brackets come from the second copy. The second copy in turn loses part of the mason's story (line 386 runs *om aldaar maar reeds veele jaaren*). The manuscript page numbers (8, 9, 8, 9, then 10 on image 8) show that nothing is missing between the two copies.
13. *Na den Eeten*: this continues 28 April, a Sunday; the German diary has the same walk after the midday meal on that day. The number *10.* at the start of line 395 is the manuscript page number.
14. The transcription reads *waar de Stok, ^(Bijl) bell om hoog gevonden wierd*; the normalised column has "high up". The German diary has "about six ells high", so *bell* may be a misreading of *6 el*. To be checked against the image.
15. *verklikker en Bijen eter*: "tell-tale" and "bee-eater", names for the honeyguide.
16. *wolf*: in Cape Dutch usage, the hyena. See the glossary.
17. Line 471 is missing from the numbering. The transcription reads *en de ho[...]bleevene op*; the German diary says "when the honey is out, it claims its share and is content with that". The words in square brackets are a conjecture from the German.
18. The diarists' explanation of "Bastard", the colonial term for people of mixed Khoekhoe and European descent. See the glossary.
