Diary of the 3 brethren Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn and Johann Christian Kühnel, of their stay at the Cape and their further journey into the country, beginning on 23 November 1792 and ending on 31 March 1793 [1].

<!-- lines 13708-13723 -->
From 23 November 1792 to 20 December we stayed in the town. In this time of a good 4 weeks we became acquainted with many people in the town, among others with a society of pious souls, who come together twice a week for an exercise [of devotion] [2]. They are more than 60 in number, and we were asked at once to attend their meeting. There really are dear people among them, who seek to be saved only through the merits of Jesus, and plead at his feet for the forgiveness of their sins. Their meetings are conducted quite simply. They have read the *Idea fidei fratrum*, the work of our late Brother Spangenberg, with blessing [3], and say that the teaching of this book has removed a great many false ideas about the Brethren.

<!-- lines 13724-13749 -->
Domine van Lier had provided for us in advance in many things; and because he meant to make a journey into the country for his health, he took particular care that we should be supplied with good lodgings, if we arrived in the town before his return [4]. On 28 November he came home, but much weakened: he has consumption, and to all human appearance will not live much longer, which would be a great loss for us, since he is our true [friend] [5]. We had to dine with him twice. The pious society mentioned above is heartily glad that God has now opened the door for the Gospel to be preached to the poor Hottentots. When we took leave of them, they gave us to understand that they had wished, and also believed, that we would set up some arrangements in their society after the manner of our congregation. But we answered that we had not come anywhere with such a purpose, nor here into this country, and that we were far from meddling with such arrangements, which could have very harmful consequences both for them and for us.

<!-- lines 13750-13777 -->
Meanwhile we were busy writing and packing our things. We looked everything over carefully, to see whether anything had been spoiled on the ship, and to our great joy found it in good condition. We also had many visits to pay in the town, on which occasions we were often invited to meals. The Commandant, on whom we waited several times, received us very kindly each time [6]; so did the State Commissioners who are here at present [7] (and also Mr Gordon, the head of the military and the second authority of this country) [8]. We had to dine with him in his garden outside the town, which is very splendid [9]. Our well-meaning friends proposed many places to us where we might go and settle; but the gentlemen of the government wished us to wait until Baas Teunis arrived, whom they expected any day, and whom they would order to take us with him and look after us. He is in the Company's service, and as Baas of a Company post he must obey their orders. He is a respectable man, and one of authority in the whole country, whose jurisdiction reaches so far that the farmers as well as the Hottentots are under his command.

<!-- lines 13779-13795 -->
On 2 December a gentleman of Constantia named Clüde sent us a message asking us to come to him the next day, and had us fetched in a wagon with 8 horses in front of it [10]. With such a wagon one needs 2 hours to get there. It is the place where the Cape Constantia wine grows, whose equal is not to be found in the whole country. This gentleman told us that he had gone to school with our late Brother Schmidt (the former missionary) [11]. He asked us to stay with him for some days, but we could not accept, because we did not know whether Baas Teunis might not arrive in Cape Town in the meantime; so this gentleman was kind enough to have us brought back the next day in the same way.

<!-- lines 13796-13821 -->
After our return to the town we heard various rumours that were being spread in the town by the farmers. Some said: "If the Hottentots are to be taught, we might well want to be Hottentots too." Others said: "If the Herrnhuters come into the country to teach the Hottentots, we will kill them at once." Baas Teunis said: "The first have spoken the truth, for there are many farmers who cannot read a word." (Meanwhile we do not let ourselves be disturbed, trusting that our God will order and direct all things according to his good pleasure.) A gentleman in Amsterdam who was born at the Cape advised us to keep quite quiet at the beginning and make no mention of the purpose of our coming here, for otherwise the farmers would make our cause very hard. And indeed we had hardly arrived in the town when farmers came to our friend Schmidt [12] to find out whether it was true that Herrnhuters had come to go into the country and teach the Hottentots [13]. This rumour spread like wildfire through the whole country, and many believe that we are very special and extraordinary people.

<!-- lines 13822-13851 -->
Baas Teunis arrived in the town on 17 December, and on the next day (18 December) the acting Governor (since no Governor has yet been appointed) [14] sent word to both us and Baas Teunis to come to him. His purpose was to recommend us to Baas Teunis in the best terms, and to charge him to take us to the dwellings of the Hottentots (which they call kraals), so that we might look over these districts and choose there the place that pleased us best, to build a house for ourselves; in which he would help us in every way. Baas Teunis promised to make every possible effort to that end. The gentleman went on to say that he had consulted the State Commissioners on the matter, and they were of the same mind as he, that it would be most advisable for us to build first of all at Baviaanskloof, where our former missionary had lived. We then gave His Honour to understand that we could not well bind ourselves to a place before we had seen it. The acting Governor replied that we were free to go and look over this district first; and Baas Teunis added that most of the Hottentots lived in that neighbourhood. It was remarkable to us that these gentlemen advised us to go to this neighbourhood, whereas others had proposed the Tigerhoek to us [15].

<!-- lines 13852-13870 -->
On 19 December we took leave of the Governor, commending ourselves to his further love and goodwill. His Honour promised to serve us wherever he could; the two State Commissioners gave us the same promise, honoured us with a special audience, assured us of their love and goodwill, and wished us God's best blessings. Further, we took leave of the 4 ministers in this town, 3 of whom belong to the Reformed and 1 to the Lutheran Church. They were all very friendly, and assured us of their willingness to be of service to us as far as they could. Then, after we had also taken a hearty leave of our other good friends, who had shown us much love and kindness, we left the town at 4 o'clock in the afternoon on 20 December.

## Notes

1. The heading stands at the foot of page 235b, below the end of the second copy of the reports for 1797 and 1798 (entry 48). This diary is the Dutch version of the German diary A363595, entries 01 to 06; it is shorter in places, and in others it has details the German lacks. The German in the normalised column (*Tagesbericht der 3 Brüder*) is a modern translation of this Dutch text; the German diary is a separate manuscript.
2. *tot eene oeffening*: an "exercise", a devotional meeting of the kind held by pious societies in the Dutch Reformed world. The opening parenthesis in the transcription is not closed.
3. *Zal: Br Spangenberg*: August Gottlieb Spangenberg (Brother Joseph), who had died on 18 September 1792; see `glossary.csv`. The *Idea fidei fratrum* is his summary of Moravian doctrine (1779); the German diary does not name its author here.
4. *Dom: van Lier*: Helperus Ritzema van Lier, minister of the Reformed church in Cape Town; he died on 21 March 1793 (entry 55). The Dutch explains what the German leaves unclear: van Lier arranged the lodgings because he expected to be away when the brethren arrived.
5. Line 13734 is missing, one of the lines lost at every 82nd line ID (see CLAUDE.md). The transcription runs *vermits hy onze waare* / *Wy moeſten tweemaal by hem eeten*. The German diary has "In him we are losing a true friend. He is much loved in the whole town"; the lost line probably held *Vriend is* and the words about the town. The word in square brackets is supplied from there.
6. *Den Commandant*: probably Johan Isaac Rhenius, acting head of the government, whom Brother Marsveld calls *Comandant Rhenius* in entry 55. See A363595, entry 01, notes 6 and 11.
7. *Staats Commiſsarisſen*: the Commissioners-General Sebastiaan Cornelis Nederburgh and Simon Hendrik Frykenius, sent by the Dutch East India Company, 1792 to 1793.
8. Robert Jacob Gordon, commander of the garrison. The parenthesis opens with *(* and closes with the diarist's *:)*.
9. The transcription reads *Wy moeſten by hem in zynen Thuin, buiten de Stad spyzen, die zeer heerlyk is, spyzen*, with *spyzen*, "dine", written twice.
10. *Clude*: probably Hendrik Cloete of Groot Constantia; see `glossary.csv` (Mr Clüde). The German diary says the wagon came at 6 o'clock in the morning.
11. *by onzen Zal: Broeder Schmidt (: de voorige Misſionarius :) ter School had gegaan*: "the late" Brother Schmidt, Georg Schmidt, the first missionary at Baviaanskloof (1737 to 1744), who died in 1785. The Dutch settles the doubtful German *jetzigen*, "present" (A363595, entry 01, note 9): the German manuscript probably had *ſel.*, "the late". Since Georg Schmidt left the Cape in 1744, "gone to school with" must mean that Cloete had been taught by him as a boy, or his words were remembered loosely. To be checked against the image.
12. Father Schmidt of Cape Town, with whom the brethren lodged; see `glossary.csv`. Not Georg Schmidt.
13. Line 13816 is missing. The transcription runs *om* / *landwaards in de gaanſen de Hottentotten te onder wyzen*, where *gaanſen* is garbled; the sense, "to go into the country and teach the Hottentots", agrees with the German diary, so the lost line probably held little. To be checked against the image.
14. *de adinterim Gouverneur*: Rhenius; see note 6. The German diary calls him "the Governor or commander".
15. *de Tygerhoek*: a farm and area on the Sonderend River east of Baviaanskloof; see `glossary.csv`.
