<!-- lines 11831-11856 -->
On 6 February we enjoyed Holy Communion, at which Louise was present as a confirmand [1]. On the 8th one half of the roof rafters was put up, and everything went off happily: but on the 11th a great accident might easily have happened. While the masons were at the gable, one of them stepped too far out on the end of a plank, and just as it threatened to tip over with him, he was seized by the other mason and a Hottentot and saved: for this protection we thanked the Saviour from our hearts. [On the 13th we stopped the] threshing, since we believed we should have enough straw [2]. On the 14th Rosina, who had several times shown much repentance for her sins, was accepted again, which was made known in the meeting in which the church litany is prayed [3].

<!-- lines 11857-11920 -->
Today a Hottentot told us that Baas Teunis had said that the farmers of the lower country would come this very week, and make an end of Baviaanskloof. We have often heard such rumours for some time already, but paid no heed to them. It is certain that Satan rages and storms anew, and that Baas Teunis is one of his tools. Only lately he assured the Hottentots with heavy curses that not one of them should set foot in the new house. We had it in mind not to be disturbed by such rumours this time either; but at midday a messenger came to us from Baas Teunis with this spoken message, that tomorrow or the day after a commando of farmers would come to us; he believed, in the night: whether they meant to destroy Baviaanskloof or not, he did not know. How we felt on hearing this message we cannot describe! The poor Hottentots too were seized with terror and fear. The month before they had been with the English General at the Cape, who gave them a letter to take to the Landdrost at Stellenbosch. And this Landdrost in turn gave them a letter, which they were to show to the farmers if they should come. We have this letter in safe keeping; it is favourable enough to us: but since the said farmers are in revolt against the English government, and intend to starve out Cape Town, we cannot count on it much otherwise. A few days before, the Landdrost had said to the Hottentots that as soon as they heard that the farmers were really on the march, they must let him know at once. So the greater part of them set off this very day, some on horseback, some on foot, for Stellenbosch, meaning to go on from there to the Cape as well. Many asked us what they should do; but we did not know what advice to give them. For our own persons too we knew nothing to do but wait for the Saviour's help: it is his work after all.

<!-- lines 11920-11947 -->
The 15th and 16th passed quietly, and no farmers appeared. On the 17th we even heard that Baas Teunis was said to have told a Hottentot that the whole thing was a lie, and that he showed his displeasure that the Hottentots had [run] at once to the Landdrost [4]. On the 19th young Teunis came to tell us that today a mounted orderly had come to his father, bringing a sharp letter from the Landdrost about the Hottentots' complaints: the General had ordered that the least act of violence that might be done to the Hottentots should be severely punished: his father had also received a strong reproof for not having reported the farmers' plan. He now asked that one or two of us should come to him tomorrow, as he had to speak with us urgently.

<!-- lines 11947-12013 -->
In answer to this request the Brothers Marsveld and Kühnel rode to him on the 20th. He had also sent for the field corporal Otto. When we came to him, he took us with the corporal into a separate room, and put to us what confusion this running of the Hottentots to Stellenbosch and to the Cape had caused: he would have that Hottentot come, and have him taken to Stellenbosch in chains [5]. We answered him that not only this one, but more Hottentots too had heard such words from him; which he could not deny, but tried to excuse. He said further that the General had meant to send a strong commando of soldiers to Stellenbosch: but he had written to him that it was all lies. He said further: "It is true indeed that a commando of 40 farmers is about to come to you, and I expect them here today: but they mean to do you no harm, only to seek out those Hottentots who are in their debt and must work for them for it, and drive them on before them" [6]. To which we answered: "So it is the truth after all that they mean to come; and is it not called violence if they mean to catch the Hottentots and drive them on before the horses?" He promised to do his utmost to prevent their coming to us; and we believe too that he will do it; for he has to answer for it to the government. The debts of the Hottentots, which are now taken as a pretext for oppressing them, have for the most part come about because the men have had to do service as soldiers at the Cape for so long, [and] during that time the women have been forced to borrow from the farmers what they needed to keep themselves and their children. The Landdrost has also written to us that we should admonish the Hottentots to pay off their debts to the farmers by working; and we do not fail in admonitions, and many do what they can; but it cannot [all be done] at once. [The two brothers] came home again today [7].

<!-- lines 12014-12053 -->
On the 21st a Hottentot came running from the post, and said that the commando would come tomorrow. He had already seen 18 of it at the post, and 30 were at another place an hour from there, all armed. At another place our mason had spoken with 12 men. Until now people had always spoken of farmers of the lower country; but now it turned out that it is those from our own parts, among whom some live not far from us. Today there were not many in the meeting, since most of the mothers had fled into the mountains with their children for fear of the farmers. When the Brothers Schwinn and Kühnel visited in the kraals after midday, they found at a Bastard's a farmer who had also been with the commando, and had just now come from Baas Teunis. He said that as soon as he had heard the letter read, he had ridden off with 3 others, and they would have nothing to do with the matter. In the evening this Bastard came to us and said that he intended to ride to the General tomorrow to give him an exact report of everything that had happened, and asked our advice about it.

<!-- lines 12053-12079 -->
We judged that this would be very good; for Baas Teunis will hardly report the truth to the government. On the 22nd some of the commando, who had in all likelihood thought better of it, came back again from Baas Teunis, and rode quietly past half an hour from us. We wondered that the Hottentots had not yet come back from the Cape; so Brother Marsveld rode to Wolfrum on the 25th to make inquiry about it; but could learn nothing. At Otto's he heard that the rebellious farmers meant to draw up a paper about the Hottentots, to lay it before the General. Their number comes to a good 150. On the 26th 2 Hottentots passed through here on their way to the Cape [8]. They had served with the first commandants of the farmers; but had run away for fear. They told us that they had had to cast many small bullets for their masters.

<!-- lines 12080-12114 -->
On the 28th we had the memorial day of our moving into our house 3 years ago, and considered with thankful hearts the help in so many circumstances that we had received from our dear Lord since that time. On the 29th all the Hottentots at last came back from the Cape. The General had heard them very kindly, and promised them that the farmers should do them no harm: meanwhile they should just go to Stellenbosch, until [the matter had settled down again] [9]. This they had done, and had also found work there. They brought letters from the Landdrost for 6 field corporals, and one for us too, in which [he] writes that we should admonish the Hottentots to patience; further, that the General had it in mind to raise a corps of soldiers of 3 to 400 young unmarried men, but all volunteers, from the Hottentot nation; and he asked us to be willing to help with it [10]. The General had said this to the Hottentots himself, adding: "It is for the protection of your own nation." This is a hard thing for us, but we must keep silent about it.
