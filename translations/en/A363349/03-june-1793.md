<!-- lines 956-1003 -->
On 1 June we partook of Holy Communion with a blessed sense of the nearness of our merciful Saviour.

On 2 June, after church, Brother Marsveld and Brother Schwinn went to visit a farmer named Plaisir, who lives two hours' walk from us. He had already sent twice asking us to come to him, after he had been to see us a few times; and he would have come more often if riding on horseback were not so hard for him because of his age and stoutness. When the brethren got there, he did not know what to do for joy. It happened by chance that, just while the brethren were with him, this farmer received a letter together with two big sacks filled with articles of clothing for the slaves, with the instruction to have this gift distributed among the slaves by a Christian who could read. On each piece stood the name of the slave who very much needed these clothes. The letter and clothes had been sent to him by his master [1], who lives two days' journey from him, for the farmer is not the owner but only the overseer of that place, and has been for many years. This man can read neither print nor handwriting, which he much lamented. He has two Hottentot women in his service, whose two daughters go to school with us. He urgently admonishes them to be diligent and obedient. He is already looking forward with joy to their being able to read the word of God to him once they have learned to read, which he longs for very much. He showed great joy that we had come into this country, "and also because," he said, "I now believe and trust that if I should fall ill and send to ask you to come to me, so as to hear a word about Jesus from you, you will not refuse me." Brother Schwinn had to read him something from the Reformed confession of faith, which he had in his house, and he listened to it with the greatest attention.

<!-- lines 1004-1049 -->
While Brothers Marsveld and Schwinn were visiting the farmer mentioned above, Baas Teunis's son came to Baviaanskloof with his young wife and his sister and paid a very friendly visit, which Brothers Marsveld and Schwinn, who came home again on 3 June, were very sorry to have missed. Two Bastard Hottentot women also came to look at our place, being inclined to come and live here if they liked it. They live two days' journey from here and go decently dressed. If they had not said so themselves, we would not have taken them for Hottentot women. They attended the school and the evening meeting and went away again on the 5th, declaring at parting that they had liked it here very much.

A little boy who yesterday had not kept quiet in school, or had talked a great deal, was struck with a stick by a Hottentot woman who had heard it in the kitchen and came into the school; at this he took his bench and went home. Today in the children's meeting, to which all the children had come together, he was told in the hearing of all that we did not want disobedient children in the school; for all who come to us in school must behave quietly and orderly and be intent on learning to know and love the dear Saviour as their Creator and Redeemer. Any of the children who will not do so can and must stay away from the school. Accordingly the boy was sent out of the school. This was so painful to him that the next morning he came to beg for forgiveness and to promise everything one could wish, only to be accepted again and let into school, which then was done.

<!-- lines 1050-1094 -->
On the 7th one of the Bastard Hottentots who had come back from the Cape said that if we would clear our garden of weeds and the roots of the trees, he would plough the land as far as he could, since there was no one to be had to dig it. This offer was very welcome to us, but we still saw no way of clearing the land.

On the 8th, being Saturday and no school, we had word sent to the women and children in their kraals that as many of them as had a mind to should come and help us clear the garden. 30 came together at once for the purpose, and they did it, the children especially, with great [delight] [2]. We gave them something to eat at midday for their trouble, though it was very little, since we have not much ourselves. But they were content with it. By their diligent work they brought together into a heap an unbelievable quantity of wild grass and Hottentot figs [3], with which one could well have loaded thirty wagons. But now we were at a loss how to get this great heap out of the garden, after we had tried in vain to burn it, since everything was too green and too damp to catch fire. The Hottentot women knew what to do, however, and hit on the following idea. One sat down on the ground, while another spread out behind her on her arm the skin with which they are covered, which with some hangs from the shoulders down to the ground, and put on it as much of the gathered weeds as she could. When this was done, the first took round her neck the two lower ends of the skin, handed to her by the other, and then, standing up, carried the great bundle on her shoulders out of the garden.

<!-- lines 1094-1127 -->
When they had diligently repeated this all day, by evening half of the weeds had been carried away, which made us think that if one went on with this work for another day the garden could be wholly cleared. For that purpose we spoke about it with our Hottentot women on Sunday 9 June after the church meeting had ended, to hear whether they had a mind to take up Saturday's work again on Monday, promising them to hold the school, which could not take place then, on Wednesday; this made them all very ready to agree to our proposal. For both old and young are very keen on the school, and both come to it with great pleasure. When the children hear that one of them is praised for learning well, the others become very sensitive about it out of self-love and are strongly spurred on to make every possible effort not to stay behind. When the school is over (and it lasts 4 hours), some always come asking whether they may say their lesson once more. This also happens in the evening, and if we were willing we would have them with us the whole evening. We allowed it to some a couple of times, and now many more are already coming. There is no possibility of it, however, the evening being set apart for those who cannot come to school during the day because of their work. Outside school hours we often hear the Hottentots in their kraals singing verses about Jesus' suffering and death, which touches us deeply and is a great joy to us.

<!-- lines 1127-1159 -->
As Brother Kühnel celebrates his 32nd birthday tomorrow, 10 June, and on that day we would hardly have rest and time, because of the work arranged in our garden, to give him a little special pleasure we had planned, we decided to do it today. So after wishing him many blessings for his heart, we treated him and ourselves at midday to a roast cock, which had run away from us and, not letting itself be caught, had been shot dead. At this we called two things to mind: first, that last year we were able to celebrate this birthday of his much more solemnly at Zeist, and it was also the last day of our contented stay there; second, that we already have more to eat now than on Brother Schwinn's birthday on 18 March, when we had to be content with a piece of dry bread. Our outward [circumstances have] noticeably improved [4]. We still live very frugally, it is true, but we have good hope that our garden will provide us with the vegetables we need. We have already planted a piece of land with cabbage, which is coming on well, and we expect the same of the peas and turnips; radishes we can already have every morning with our breakfast. Meanwhile we are also finding out the truth of the common proverb: every beginning is hard.

<!-- lines 1160-1194 -->
On the 10th, being Monday, the Hottentot women and their children came as agreed to do the promised work in our garden, and were busy at it diligently all day. It was a pleasure to see the children especially working and behaving quietly and orderly while they did it. At midday we gave them dumplings of meal to eat and in the evening bread, and now the garden was cleared so that it could be ploughed. This was done on 11 and 12 June; but much was still left that had to be dug over, because it could not be ploughed, partly because of the trees standing on the land, partly because there is no room to turn 8 oxen, which people usually harness to a plough, and sometimes as many as 12. Even so the plough does not go deep, so that after ploughing one still has to dig over a good deal, and the plough serves only as a relief. In ploughing 3 people must always work together. The first goes in front and leads the front oxen with a thong; the second has a great whip, which can hardly be managed with 2 hands, to make the oxen go forward with it; the third steers the plough. The oxen in this country look as big and strong as those of Europe, but they do not have the same strength; they go faster, though, for when driving a wagon the oxen mostly go at a trot.

On 13 June we had a rainy day. We sowed some barley, to have feed for the hens, and, as we were told, to make the couch grass [5] die off by it.

<!-- lines 1195-1229 -->
On 15 June, taking counsel about our Hottentots, we received an indication from the adorable Saviour [6] to count 4 of our Hottentot women as candidates for baptism (or future baptismal candidates), so that now, with the first, whom the Saviour had already indicated to us for it 14 days ago, we have 5 candidates for baptism. We intend to hold two special meetings with them each week, namely on Wednesdays and Saturdays, no school being held on those days.

On the 16th a Bastard Hottentot came intending to live and learn here. But towards evening we were informed by the two Bastard Hottentots who already live here that the Hottentot mentioned, who lives two days' journey from here, had stolen a horse, which one of them had already taken into safekeeping [7]. They asked us whether they might speak to him about it in our presence. When this was done he tried to deny it, but it was plain from his words that he had stolen it. The horse had to stay here, and he was told to bring the man from whom, by his account, he had bought the horse. He promised to do so and went away to fetch the man; but it is very likely that he will not keep his word. It happens very seldom, however, that a Hottentot steals.

<!-- lines 1230-1266 -->
On 17 June two of our women, who can hardly see any more, came to ask us whether they would grieve the Lord by it if they [did not learn to read] [8]. We answered, as we have also told other old people like them, that since all the efforts they truly make to learn to read were in vain for want of being able to see, they could become Christians and children of God even without learning to read, if only they truly sought and found grace and the forgiveness of sins at the feet of Jesus, and implored God to open their hearts, so that the word of Jesus' suffering and dying might find entrance with them and they might be moved by it to live for him alone and to follow his mind and will in all things. To that end they would do well to attend the evening meetings and the weekly sermon on Sunday diligently; but if they wanted to go on with their former sinful and heathen life, they would grieve the Saviour exceedingly by it.

On 18 June a Hottentot woman who lives here came to tell us that a farmer's wife named Groenewald [9], whom she had visited today, had offered her brandy, which she used to be very fond of, but that this time she had drunk only very little of it, for fear of grieving the Saviour by it. We told her that her fear had not been groundless, and that if she wished to become a child of God she ought to abstain altogether from that drink, which she also promised.

<!-- lines 1267-1294 -->
On the 19th we had the first meeting with our 5 candidates for baptism, whom we had fetched from their kraals, which seemed strange to them. When they had come to us, we told them that the Saviour had given us the confidence to count them as candidates for baptism, and by further instruction to make them understand what holy baptism is and what is imparted to us by it. The feeling that could be perceived in this meeting is beyond all description. They all declared that they wished with their whole heart to share in this great grace, of being received and accepted into a covenant with God through holy baptism, if it pleased God to count them worthy of it.

On 20 June another Hottentot woman came to tell us that she had a great desire to be baptised. We advised her to lay her longing before the Saviour, and assured her that if it was her heart's concern to live only for the Lord Jesus in this world, he would also let this great grace fall to her share.

<!-- lines 1295-1311 -->
On the 21st we heard that some of our Hottentots who had been summoned to the Cape had come back from there and had said that more would follow them for a certain time.

On the 22nd Brothers Marsveld and Schwinn went to visit Baas Teunis's son, to congratulate him and his young wife, since they had not been at home when they had visited Brother Kühnel at Baviaanskloof.

On the 23rd it rained fairly hard again, after we had had fine, warm weather the whole week; nevertheless the mountains were still covered with the snow that fell 8 days ago, though on the flat land no snow was to be seen. During the night it is also fairly cold, so that there is hoar frost, but still it does not freeze.

<!-- lines 1312-1367 -->
On 24 June Brother Kühnel rode to fetch a two-wheeled wagon. It had been [made] for someone else, [who, no longer wanting it] [10], has had a four-wheeled wagon made. We cannot do without such a wagon, for when we want wood we must fetch it an hour away by the river, and when we want meal ground we must ride a whole day to reach a mill. The Hottentot in our service has 4 oxen in his charge, which we can borrow from the Hottentots for a moderate price, but not a wagon; for a wagon that looks no better than a dung cart in Germany costs 250 Rixdollars here. When one wants something carted by a farmer, one must pay for one day 2 Rixdollars for the wagon and also two Rixdollars for 4 oxen (for they do not harness fewer), 4 Rixdollars together. Baas Teunis let us have this wagon, or cart, for 60 Rixdollars. If we had had to have it made at the Cape, we would have had to pay 100 Rixdollars for it.

When Brother Kühnel, being at the post, also asked about various necessities of life and where one could buy them, young Mrs Teunis said that a young married farmer's wife had told her that when the brethren came to the post to fetch the cart she would ask them to call on her, as she meant to provide us with some vegetables, which she believed we would need. So Brother Kühnel rode there and asked for butter and potatoes, to cook. The young farmer's wife, named Lind [11], received him very kindly. She accepted payment for the butter, but the potatoes she gave for nothing, and likewise a store of dried peaches, beans, pumpkins [12] and several heads of cabbage, which came very opportunely to us. According to the inhabitants it has been a lean year in every respect. Mrs Teunis said that never before in any year had she lacked vegetables in her garden the whole winter through, but she did this year, because the weather was first too dry and now too cold, which hindered all growth.

<!-- lines 1368-1396 -->
On 26 June Brother Kühnel came home again safely, although it was dangerous to travel now because of the swollen rivers, which have to be crossed 3 times on the road to the post. In summer the water comes up to the knees, but now up to the horse's saddle. Driving with a wagon is still more difficult and dangerous, because the water comes up to the middle of the side ladders of the wagon, so that one is obliged to stand on top of the ladders; and that is only the case when one has only small rivers to cross. Big rivers cannot be crossed at all, and that is the reason why one cannot get to the Cape now. When one has to drive through the rivers with a wagon, a Hottentot goes ahead each time to lead the front oxen, and the Hottentots are used to going into the water up to their necks. Here it serves them very well that they do not need to take off clothes, which would be very unpleasant, uncomfortable and time-wasting; instead, their skin, called a kaross [13], can be quickly put aside when they notice that the river is very deep.

<!-- lines 1397-1447 -->
Our old Lena, baptised by Brother Schmidt [14], has often been an object of our consideration. She misses no meeting, not even the school, so as to learn to read better; but as she is almost quite blind, we have told her that her efforts are fruitless [15]. We have often spoken with her and reminded her that she made a covenant with the dear Saviour at her baptism. To the question whether she still kept this grace freshly in mind, she seemed unable to answer well, and one perceived little inward life in her. When one asks her: "Lena, how are you?" her answer is: "Well and not well, for it is very cold, and my kaross, or skin, is very torn." To the question "Would you like to be saved?" she answered: "God grant it." We thought that her baptism, and what Brother Schmidt had said to her on that occasion, must surely have stayed in her memory vividly, since she could tell many other things about Brother Schmidt, among others that he had read something to the Hottentots every evening of the week from the big book (that is, the Bible) and spoken about it, just as we also do. We therefore resolved on 25 June to speak with her alone about it more closely. On that occasion she told us that Brother Schmidt had baptised her by the river that runs past near the bake oven, about 50 paces from our house, and after the baptism had said to her that she had now made a covenant with God, to live only for the Saviour. To the question whether she now believed she would be saved when she was called out of this temporal life, her answer was: "Yes, now I believe that the Saviour will take me to himself after my death." She further told us that her sister too had been baptised by Brother Schmidt in her presence, in another river, and also 3 Hottentot women; but in which river, that she did not know.

<!-- lines 1448-1481 -->
On 27 June one of those who are to be baptised came to tell us that while chopping wood she had felt very anxious and sad, had fallen on her knees and implored the Saviour with her whole heart for the forgiveness of her sins; at this she had felt as if someone said to her, "Your sins are forgiven you," which filled her heart with great joy and had moved her to come and tell us. We pointed her to the Friend of sinners, to call on him with pleading to keep this blessed feeling for her and to make his becoming man, his suffering and dying for poor sinners still more living to her.

As for our daily meetings, we must say that the Holy Spirit is working powerfully on the hearts to glorify the suffering and dying of Jesus Christ in their hearts. The Lord's walking in the midst of this Hottentot meeting, and their many tears, are the speaking proofs of it, and they often let it be known that their whole heart is set on being saved.

On 28 June a Bastard Hottentot came from Hottentots Holland [16], two days' journey from here, to attend our meeting. He came at midday and of his own accord, without anyone having asked him, helped dig our garden.

<!-- lines 1482-1541 -->
On the 29th a crowd of Hottentot women came to complain to us sorrowfully that they and their children had nothing to eat and so must die of hunger, since their husbands did not come back from the Cape. Brother Kühnel, being at the post on the 25th, had already [spoken] with Baas Teunis about [whether the] [17] Hottentot women with their children still had what they needed to live on; to which he had answered him that things looked very bad with them in that respect, and that they complained bitterly that their husbands did not come home, although some time ago they had been given hope of it but had been disappointed in their hope. Baas Teunis said on that occasion that a whole year might well pass before that happened. If they asked for leave, they would probably get it for a short time. But the Hottentots themselves did not care much about it, since their wives and children do not lie very close to their hearts, and meanwhile they suffered no want at the Cape, having plenty of food and drink there. Baas Teunis added that if the Hottentot women would only come and complain to them, he would report it to Mr Gordon, the head of the military at the Cape, and ask him to give the married Hottentots leave to go back to their wives for a time. This made us advise the poor Hottentot women to go to Baas Teunis and ask for his intercession, which they promised to do the next morning without delay. For when their husbands are at home, they go to work for the farmers and receive wheat for it, which in this country is called corn. They boil it and eat it to keep themselves alive; it never even comes to baking bread from it. Now their lack of food has risen so high that whoever still has a sheep or a goat is obliged to slaughter it, so as not to die of hunger. So far has everything been eaten up, which is most pitiful to see, and God knows what the poor people are to live on from now on. In the evening of this day we partook of Holy Communion, at which the Saviour made himself known very powerfully to our poor hearts.

<!-- lines 1542-1553 -->
On 30 June all the Hottentot women came to ask us to write to Baas Teunis to do his best to get their husbands back from the Cape. We could not ask or expect this for all of them, but only for those whose wives are very old or have small children. We admonished the others to work diligently and earn their needful living. 5 of these Hottentot women went to Baas Teunis with a letter from us, in which we named the 5 in greatest need.
