<!-- lines 490-499 -->
On 1 May Brother Marsveld rode to Baas Teunis, who had asked him to come and see him. The reason was that he wanted to know what was going on in the fatherland, having heard that we had received letters from there. He was born at Maastricht, but has been in this country for 33 years [1].

<!-- lines 500-500 -->
On 2 May Brother Marsveld rode home again.

<!-- lines 501-503 -->
On 3 May a farmer named Henrich Tirnhold [2] came to stay the night with us; he was travelling to the Buffels River.

<!-- lines 504-510 -->
On 4 May we were busy fencing in our garden, so that no cattle could get in, since we had sown a small piece of land in it to provide ourselves with vegetables. In the evening we partook of Holy Communion, with a blessed sense of the nearness of the dear Saviour [3].

<!-- lines 511-516 -->
On 5 May we had a visit from someone named Redlichhausen, from Ganzekraal, 6 hours from here. He is a wagon maker, who with his wife is well disposed towards us. He attended our church meeting and rode home again in the evening.

<!-- lines 517-524 -->
On 7 May we announced to the Hottentots in the evening meeting that from now on we meant to begin instruction twice a week with the children too, and had fixed Wednesday and Saturday for it. We also heard that all the Hottentots had been summoned to the Cape, for fear of the French, who were said to be on their way to the Cape [4].

<!-- lines 525-552 -->
On 8 May we began the instruction with the children, whose number was 31. It has already been remarked that in this country all unmarried persons count in the class of children. So we have only three divisions with which we hold school, namely the men, the women and the children. Each of us has one division to look after. The number of those who come to school comes to 80. They were very attentive when the birth of the Saviour was read to them and spoken about. We received a letter from Baas Teunis, whose bearer is a captain of the Hottentots. In the letter he tells us that we must let all the Hottentots who knew how to use a gun go to the Cape. The captain had orders to command them, and they had to leave the very next morning. We could do nothing about it, but we had them all come to us once more before they left. We foresaw that not many of them would be left with us, since they all knew how to handle a gun, having learned it from the farmers. No Hottentot may have a gun of his own; that is forbidden.

<!-- lines 552-587 -->
[On 9 May, Ascension Day, we] intended [5] to hold our usual church meeting, in order to set before the Hottentots the importance of this day, but were prevented from it by the commission of the Hottentot captain. He came to us with all the Hottentots he was taking with him to the Cape (of whom only two were left behind, one being lame and the other sick). When they were gathered together, we admonished them warmly to think diligently on what they had heard with us of the Lord Jesus, their Saviour, and to turn to him in all circumstances, and in a prayer on our knees we commended them to God's keeping and protection. How it will go meanwhile with the women and children, and where they are to get the food they need, God knows; for it is now the very worst and leanest time of the year, bare of fruit and crops to keep themselves alive on. Several of them already have nothing but honey, which they seek out for their food with great trouble in the bush among the mountains. The Hottentot in our service was given permission by the captain to stay with us, which was very dear and welcome to us. For where would we get what we need, now that all the other Hottentots are gone? We also still have a great deal of work to put our garden in order, and every day we expect sheep; and who would herd them, if we had had to let our Hottentot go too?

<!-- lines 588-592 -->
Since after the departure of the Hottentot men no more school could be held for them, on 10 May we began to hold the school of the children (whose number is fairly large) in the morning at half past seven.

<!-- lines 593-602 -->
On 11 May, being Saturday and so no school, we were busy working our garden. We must do this with all diligence as soon as we have a little time left over for it, because the last sowing will soon have to be transplanted; besides, there is still land to dig, which we had put off doing, thinking we still had time enough for it.

<!-- lines 603-609 -->
If we had known that all the Hottentots would have to leave us, we would have had this work done long ago; for now we cannot get it done, and we are not able to finish much in one day, because the land has to be dug over a good 1½ ells deep on account of the many roots in the ground.

<!-- lines 610-611 -->
On 12 May we were united in spirit with the congregation at Herrnhut, which was celebrating its congregation festival [6].

<!-- lines 612-621 -->
We had heard much about the warm bath, 4 hours from here, and had become acquainted at the post at Baas Teunis's with the man who has charge of it and had promised to visit him; we also thought it necessary to get to know the farmers of that neighbourhood, so as to counter the many false stories about us. So Brother Schwinn and Brother Kühnel decided to make this little journey there today. They report the following about it:

<!-- lines 622-672 -->
"At 7 o'clock in the morning we rode away from here, with our Hottentot as guide, because one can still get over the river now, which is no longer possible once the rainy season begins, which is expected any day. Two hours from here we came to the first farmer, named Maree, with whom we had breakfast, and whose wife had already visited us once. An hour further on lives a farmer whose house we rode past. Another hour further we came to a fine [place] [7]. The people of this place did not know us and asked who we were. Answer: the teachers at Baviaanskloof. They knew of the place. They received us very kindly, had our horses unsaddled and fed, and offered us wine. They asked whether our Hottentots were learning well; when we answered yes, they seemed pleased with it. When the horses had eaten their fodder, we thanked them and went on our way to the warm bath. The name of the superintendent of it is Andreas Otte [8]. When we came to the bath he was not at home, but as he was not far off, they sent for him at once. He was very glad to see us, and there were just then few visitors to the bath. A farmer and his wife promised to visit us, saying that they were very curious to see and hear for themselves how the Hottentots were learning. They seemed to be good-natured people. They had heard many false things about us, and we had the opportunity to inform them better according to the truth. Among other things they had been told that we had been sent here by the Company and received wages from it; likewise that all the Hottentots who came to learn with us got their living from the Company, and so on.

<!-- lines 672-700 -->
"Towards evening we went to bathe. The bath lies at a fair height on a mountain, and the water is so hot that one can hardly keep one's hand [9] in it for a few minutes. There is no cold water to add to it for cooling, the mountains being wholly shot through with hot water. The superintendent said that a good twenty bath houses could be built, for wherever one dug, one found springs of hot water. Nevertheless a short way from the warm bath, going down the mountain, there was a plentiful spring of very good fresh water. We stayed there overnight, and the superintendent invited us to stay another day, but our circumstances at home did not allow us to do so. He also meant to give us some vegetables to take with us, but we could take nothing but some cabbage plants. He is a German by birth and a pastor's son, and has studied at universities. He came into this country as a soldier and was appointed by the government as superintendent of the warm bath.

<!-- lines 699-721 -->
"Early the next morning we rode home again. We chose another road, so as to get to know still more farmers. About an hour from the warm bath we came to the first farmer. We meant to ride past, but were prevented, because when we had already passed the house, they called after us loudly, why would we not stop with them; this made us turn back. They showed us every possible friendliness and asked at once how the Hottentots behaved towards us and whether they were learning well. 3 others, whom we had seen shortly before when we meant to ride past this house, and who had got off their horses there (they were on their way to the Cape to keep guard), were also very pleased about it. At parting we were wished much success in our school work by all of them. The farmer's name is Peter Meelman.

<!-- lines 721-764 -->
"When we had ridden an hour further, we went into the house of another farmer, named Otto Fries. Here too we were asked who we were, and when we had answered that we were the teachers at Baviaanskloof, we were treated in the friendliest way, to our surprise. Going on our way another hour, [we came to Christian Kuns, who] is a rich farmer [10]. Here we had our horses unsaddled and had our midday meal. The house was full of farmers, and also many corporals and commandants, [who were there] to command the Hottentots who have been called up to the Cape from this neighbourhood. Here we came as though among a swarm of bees; but all of them treated us very kindly and asked us about many things. Among other things someone asked what it was, then, that we taught the Hottentots. Answer: we teach them what is taught us in the Bible. To this he replied that we were said to have quite another doctrine. But if our doctrine agreed with the Bible, then it was plain that we taught the Hottentots no other doctrine. He had long wished to know what we really taught. Before he asked this question, he begged that it should not be taken amiss. Another asked whether the Hottentots were also learning to write. We answered that they had not got so far yet; our chief aim was that they should learn to be able to read the word of God; when they had learned that, we would see what more could be done about writing. They said that this was not good; but when asked what harm would come of it, they had no answer. If they had been willing to tell the truth, they would have answered: if they are made able to read the Bible themselves, we shall no longer be able to cheat them.

<!-- lines 765-802 -->
"Now the corporals rode on, and when they were gone our Hottentot came to tell us that he had been commanded to the Cape and would leave for it tomorrow. This was very unwelcome news to us; but the wife of the farmer Kuns comforted us by saying that we were not obliged to let him go, since the government's ordinance only ordered that whoever has two Hottentots in service is bound to give up one of them; so we, having only one Hottentot in service, were not obliged to let him go. If our Hottentot had told us this at once, we could have spoken to the commandant about it. After staying here a couple of hours, we went on our way home, which was still two hours from here. No farmers lived along this road, and in the end we felt quite worn out, as did our horses; for we are not used to riding so far, and the Hottentots' horses have little strength either, since they must keep themselves on what they find in the veld, which is now so parched that one hardly sees a green blade of grass, and still no rain comes. Towards evening we reached home again. On the way we have seen and spoken with many farmers who treat us kindly in our presence but disparage us behind our backs [11], and would rather we were far away from them. When we speak with them they seem to be our friends, and afterwards we hear that they are our enemies."

<!-- lines 802-830 -->
When we had come home, we considered what we should do about our Hottentot servant, whether we would have to let him go to the Cape or not; and so as not to expose ourselves to harmful consequences, we wrote to Baas Teunis about it and asked him to advise us as he saw fit. We sent this letter on 14 May by a special messenger, and received from him in [answer that, as we had only one Hottentot, we were] [12] not obliged to send him away, since we ourselves needed his service very much. We followed this advice and kept our Hottentot with us. At the same time he told us that his wife, having been at the Cape for 14 days, had received some money for us and brought it back, and he asked us to come and fetch it from him. Brother Schwinn rode there for that purpose on 15 May and came back on the 16th, bringing us word from Baas Teunis that ships were about to sail for the fatherland, by which we could write. We did not fail to do so, and sent our letters to Baas Teunis, who will send them on by way of the Cape to the fatherland.

<!-- lines 831-861 -->
On 19 May, the first day of Whitsun, we had very stormy weather. The storm had risen in the night and had already taken away part of our roof, which obliged us to secure it early in the morning against further damage. When we had done this, we held our morning blessing and soon afterwards the service of worship, so as not to be kept from it by the many visitors we had reason to expect. We can truly confess that the Saviour was in our midst and let us feel his nearness. We implored him that it might please him to work by his Spirit both in our hearts and in the hearts of the Hottentots, so that we might speak to the poor Hottentots after his heart and instruct them in the way of salvation. At 10 o'clock was the sermon, to which more than 30 women had come. They came to all the meetings and do not miss a single one without urgent need. In the sermon the Gospel for this day was spoken about, and it was heard with great attention. The storm went on all day and through the following night too, and was accompanied by heavy [13] rain. Because of this raw weather we had only a few visitors, which was very welcome to us.

<!-- lines 862-877 -->
On 20 May, the second day of Whitsun, there was a sermon again, in which the chief purpose of the feast of Pentecost was set forth, and in the prayer those Hottentots who have had to go to the Cape were also remembered. In the afternoon the storm became very violent again, and once more we had to try to make the roof of our house fast, so that it should not be torn away by the storm. Our house stands just before the entrance of a valley that runs upward between the mountains. So when a fierce wind blows, it falls straight on our house. As only two days of Whitsun are kept in this country, we closed the feast with the hymn of praise to the Holy Spirit, and on 21 May began to hold school again.

<!-- lines 878-903 -->
On 22 May we intended to visit Baas Teunis's son, to congratulate him and his new wife, whom he had recently married at Stellenbosch, and he had now come home again; and by this to avoid being invited to attend the real day of merrymaking, which in this country does not take place on the wedding day itself but some time afterwards, the day being spent with much merriment and a formal dance. But we were prevented in our intention by heavy rain, which went on on 23 May and was accompanied by a sharp cold, with the result that the next [morning the mountains were] covered with snow [14]. So one can mark the beginning of winter from the first day of Whitsun, which was 19 May. The storm of 19 and 20 May also destroyed the peas that had come up in our garden and covered the sprouting seeds with sand, so that we must do the sowing over again. The 24th and 25th were two fine warm days.

<!-- lines 904-919 -->
On 26 May it rained very hard again. Hence the Sonderend River cannot be crossed. The rising water has washed away a piece of the dam along which the water is led to our house and our garden. This made us much work, and makes us fear that it will happen again, since the dam is very defective, and we will hardly be able to make it hold by our own work; and to have others do it is too costly, because it ought to be made of stones. There are stones enough here, but too big for us to be able to carry them from one place to another.

<!-- lines 919-927 -->
The dam is 500 paces long and must be 4 ells high in some places, because of the water that sometimes swells up high around the mountains. Because of the heavy rain there could be no church today. We also suffered the following damage from it: a coop for our hens, which we had been given as a present, built of clay by our Hottentots, with a roof also of clay that had not yet dried, was soaked through and fell in.

<!-- lines 928-938 -->
From 27 to 30 May the weather was fine and warm again. We used it to build another hen coop, as far as our time allowed. This time we took for it shrubs that are found on the banks of the river, so as to be able to close it tight; for if this is not done, there is an animal called the muishond [15] that fetches the hens at night and eats them, as has already happened to 5 of our hens before we had a coop, which is why we have been obliged to build one.

<!-- lines 939-955 -->
On 31 May the two Bastards who live here came back from the Cape, having asked for and received permission, until further orders, to sow something at home in the present sowing time. One of them also means to sow something for us, so that we need not buy everything. The land that is to be sown is on the far side of the river, for on this side the land is not so good and has to be manured, and we have no manure, whereas the land on the other side of the river needs no manure. But the river having now risen so high that one cannot well get over it, not much will come of the sowing unless the water begins to fall.

## Notes

1. The German diary has "for 30 years" (A363595 entry 08). The Dutch diary is shorter than the German in several places this month: it leaves out the German's report of the single sisters' festival on 4 May and the whole of 6 May (the farmers' resistance to the new taxes), and later the news from the Cape and the letters to Europe.
2. The German diary has *Heinrich Fernhold*, who came from the Paarl and was travelling to *Caffer = Revier* (line 3105); the Dutch has *Henrich Tirnhold* and *de Buffels rivier*. One of the two names is probably a misreading. To be checked against the images.
3. *Heilands*: the Saviour, as in the German *Heiland*. See the glossary.
4. France had declared war on the Dutch Republic on 1 February 1793.
5. Line 553 is missing from the transcription, which runs from *dat is verboden.* (line 552) to *neemens onze gewoone Kerkvergadering te houden* (line 554). The words in square brackets are supplied from the German diary, which dates this to 9 May, Ascension Day. Ascension fell on 9 May in 1793.
6. Herrnhut kept 12 May as a festival, in memory of the Brotherly Agreement of 12 May 1727.
7. Line 635 is missing; line 634 ends *kwamen wij aan een fraaije*. The German diary has "a very fine place, which looked very grand, and there we stopped".
8. *Andreas Otte*, as in the German diary. Compare Andreas Otto, the field corporal, in the glossary; the two may be the same man. To be checked.
9. *het ... daarin houden*, "keep it in there"; the German diary has "one cannot bear it". "One's hand" is a guess at what *het* refers to.
10. Line 729 is missing; the transcription runs *Onzen weg een uur verder vervolgen-* / *rijke boer is*. The words in square brackets are supplied from the German diary, which names Christian Kuns here. His wife is named a few sentences later.
