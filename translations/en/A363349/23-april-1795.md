<!-- lines 9336-9339 -->
Continuation of the diary of our 3 missionaries among the Hottentots at Baviaanskloof at the Cape, from 1 April to 6 December 1795 [1].

<!-- lines 9340-9371 -->
On 1 April we spoke with our baptised [2]. In some of them we wished to discover a more living heart. With regard to the communicants, we had reason to rejoice over them. In our conference Susanna was appointed a confirmand. In the evening we went on with the reading of the story of the Passion. On the 2nd we had a blessed partaking of Holy Communion. On the 3rd, Good Friday, we went on with the reading of the story of the Passion, and at the passage "He bowed his head and gave up the ghost" we thanked the Saviour in a prayer for his suffering and death, and implored him that he would be pleased to imprint on our dear Hottentots an impression of it that could not be wiped out; and a penetrating feeling of grace was to be perceived at it. We meant to end this meeting with a couple more sung verses on the suffering and death of the Saviour; but the loud weeping of the Hottentots prevented us from doing so, and we ourselves could not hold back our tears. When the people had parted, they scattered everywhere into the bushes, and they could be heard praying and weeping aloud. Although all the meetings in this week had been crowned with a special blessing, that of this day stood out above all the others. On the Great Sabbath we held a lovefeast with our baptised, and afterwards an evening blessing.

<!-- lines 9372-9384 -->
On 5 April, the first day of Easter, we prayed the Easter liturgy with the baptised and the candidates for baptism. We had told them to gather with us at the break of day next morning; but they had already come together an hour earlier [3]. After the sermon they were told that there would be another meeting in the evening, in which the story of Jesus' resurrection would be read. The Hottentots gathered as early as 2 o'clock, and settled all round our house. On the 6th in the early morning we sang with our baptised the liturgy to God the Son, and then there was the sermon.

<!-- lines 9385-9417 -->
On the 8th the Brothers Marsveld and Schwinn went over to the far side of the mountains behind our house to visit a farmer. On the 9th Brother Kühnel had a vi[sit from the son of Baas Teunis, who has in his] possession the place an hour from us [4] which has given occasion to all kinds of questions about the Hottentots' cattle, as appears from our last diary. Our reader will also remember that not long ago we had a notable visit from a certain gentleman named Claude [5]. The Hottentots went to this gentleman to complain, and since then he has laid their complaints before the authorities, showing that the Hottentots had been cheated. In particular he showed the groundlessness of the pretext that the Hottentots' cattle had not room enough at that place to graze and live, and asserted that a good 500 head of cattle would find a rich living there. The best of it is that Mr Claude has now bought this land, according to a report which young Teunis himself sent us. The order that the Hottentots should take their cattle away was also formally withdrawn by the government in Cape Town. At the same time Mr Teunis told us that Herrnhuters had arrived at the Cape on a Danish ship [6]; but he did not know where they had come from, only so much, that they were not bound for the Cape.

<!-- lines 9417-9485 -->
No one can imagine how eager this made us to be informed more nearly of it, and how great our joy would be if we had the good fortune to see brothers or sisters with us. In the evening the Brothers Marsveld and Schwinn came home again, and gave us the following account of their journey: "The wife of the farmer who lives behind our mountains has asked us several times to come and visit her; and we would have done it sooner, if the high mountain behind our house had not made us afraid, since we had heard from the Hottentots that the climbing of this mountain was attended with danger to life. We took our Jacob with us to be our guide. Many times it seemed impossible to go further, especially close to the highest peak of this mountain. After long consideration we at last ventured to climb the summit of this mountain. Here the most glorious view showed itself before our eyes: on the one side the open sea, with a ship under full sail upon it; on the other side a great plain with several farmsteads [7]. Climbing down the mountain on the other side, we found it just as steep as the side we had climbed up. At 11 o'clock we came to the house of the farmer's wife mentioned above, who received us with great friendship. Her son-in-law asked us to hold a meeting, from which, however, we excused ourselves, and instead held conversation with them about the way of salvation. After midday another farmer, who lives a good hour's walk away, came to ask us to come to his house too, offering to send a couple of horses next morning to fetch us. We said that we would like to be home in good time tomorrow; but as he kept on asking us, we went to his dwelling that same evening, at which he showed great joy, and entertained us very well. One of his slave women, of whom we had heard from our Hottentots that she was troubled about her salvation, lamented her need to us with many tears, saying that whenever she began to [pray, it called out within her:] 'Depart, thou cursed one, into the everlasting fire prepared for the devil and his angels!' [8] We told her that such people as see themselves as lost are just the ones who are welcome to the Saviour, and commended to her his great love for sinners as strongly as we could. On 9 April at 6 o'clock in the early morning our horses were already saddled, and we left after a short breakfast, our farmer guiding us as far as the mountain. The way back over it was again so hard for us that we shall hardly cross this mountain again without pressing need. Towards midday we arrived back at Baviaanskloof." So much for this.

<!-- lines 9486-9509 -->
On 10 April the farmer who lives a mile from here told us that he had brought things for us from the Cape, which we could have fetched from his house. He also confirmed the news that brothers and sisters had arrived at the Cape, and handed us letters from Brother Stael, Brother Motz and Sister Woltersdorf [9]. The last two tell us that they would have liked to visit us, but that the ship in which they had come meant to sail from the Cape again as early as the 12th, which made it impossible for them to come to us. But they did very much wish to see us and speak with us. How glad we were to know brothers and sisters so near, we cannot describe. So we took counsel that, since we could not all three leave Baviaanskloof, the Brothers Marsveld and Kühnel should make the journey to the Cape, which was put into effect at once, that same day at 1 o'clock [10].

<!-- lines 9509-9535 -->
Brother Schwinn, who stayed behind, meanwhile goes on to note the following in the diary: "Today, 10 April, I had to exclude from the school 6 young people, mostly big girls, and among them a candidate for baptism, because they had behaved badly while serving with the farmers [11]. On the 11th a new family of Hottentots applied to be allowed to stay here and hear God's word. In the afternoon a gentleman came driving towards our house with 8 horses. I was as glad as a child, in the hope that it was brothers and sisters from the Cape. But it was not; it was the superintendent of the warm bath with a party of 11 persons, who had come to attend the meetings of the next morning; for that is what they are used to here in this country [12]. So on 12 April they were present at the prayer of the litany and at the sermon, and declared about it with feeling: 'The Hottentots put us Christians to shame. If we lived nearer, we would come here every Sunday.' In the afternoon they went back again.

<!-- lines 9536-9578 -->
"On 13 April a Bastard said that he did not know what to do for the anguish of his heart, and could not even speak with us without fearfulness. This pinching unrest had come over him in Passion Week, and was so gripping that he could neither eat nor sleep [13]. To the question why he had not come to us sooner, he answered: because I felt anger against you in my heart. And why that? Because he had not been one of the first to be baptised. At every baptism he had become so hotly angry that he was moved to curse and [to run out of] the meeting, [and had often wished] to be far away from us [14]; and he had not been able to look at the baptised without hatred and vexation. The testimony in the meetings, that everyone who did not come to Jesus Christ as a sinner must be lost, he could not hear without envy and spite, since he had formerly kept company with many worthy Christians who had told him that someone who lived as he did must surely be saved. Of the baptised he had known many who had lived in sin and shame, and yet had been baptised before him. These and many other thoughts had made him so confused that he had often wished we had never come into this country. But when in the last Sunday meeting those who thought themselves better than others, because they had not lived in open works of the flesh, were called upon to examine themselves closely, how things stood in their hearts as to love of God and of their neighbour, that had struck his heart like a thunderclap, and made him say to his wife, our Sister Anna [15]: 'I am lost, I am going to hell,' and so on. When I commended to him, on this confession of his, the love of Jesus for sinners, he said on going away: 'I thank you, and commend myself to your prayers.'

<!-- lines 9579-9592 -->
"On 14 April I was called late at night to one of our candidates for baptism, who was very ill. I found her in a state that gladdened me. On 15 April I read the 11th and 12th chapters of the Acts of the Apostles in the meeting. After this meeting many came and asked what kind of linen sheet that had been, which held so many different animals. That gave occasion to make known and commend to them the Saviour's love for sinners among the heathen, and many tears were shed at it."

<!-- lines 9593-9641 -->
On the 16th, late in the evening, my two dear Brothers Marsveld and Kühnel came back from the Cape, and gave the following account of their journey: "Before our departure on 10 April several of our sisters, who had come to know of our journey, came to bid us farewell and wish us a safe journey, declaring at the same time how great their joy would be to see here the brothers and sisters who had arrived at the Cape, and asking us to greet them most warmly. This was the common feeling of all our Hottentots. On the 11th we continued our journey in the company of our Jacob, and had all kinds of hardships to undergo; among other things we had to spend the whole day without food. On top of this, towards evening someone met us who told us that the brothers and sisters who had arrived at the Cape had already taken their leave in the town, to set sail tomorrow. Our horses were already so tired that we could hardly have hope of reaching Cape Town. So we implored the Lord to speed our journey, and we had the good fortune to arrive before ten o'clock at our friend Schmidt's in the town, and to find our dear brothers and sisters there, who were still sitting at supper. How great the joy was on both sides cannot be expressed in words. Tired as we were, the night passed almost without sleep. To our great joy they could stay over one more day, and did not go on board their ship until the 13th. So on the 12th we could spend the day together in peace and contentment; and since these brothers and sisters must go without Holy Communion for a long time, they asked us to hold it with them. Accordingly, with Brother Motz and Sister Woltersdorf and her two daughters, we had a blessed [partaking of Holy Communion, which the Saviour blessed with his] feelable nearness [16]. On 13 April in the early morning they went on board their ship, Brother Kühnel escorting them as far as the ship. The leave-taking at the house of our friend Schmidt was so moving that he declared he had never yet known such a leave-taking. He shed many tears at it.

<!-- lines 9642-9691 -->
"After Brother Kühnel had come back from the ship, we both visited the Lutheran and the Reformed ministers. On 14 April the ship, which had had to stay lying in the roads on the 13th, had set sail, so that early in the morning it was already out of our sight. We visited the Commissioner, the commander Rhenius and the Commandant Gordon, as well as some other gentlemen of the government, and were received very kindly by all; but the gentlemen were all so overloaded with work that we could speak with them only a little. On the 16th many ships were to set sail for Holland, from where two ships had arrived in False Bay on the 17th [17]. We tried to learn whether letters and news had come for us too; but there was nothing for us. We had also wished to be able to speak further with the gentlemen of the government about our place, but we did not find it feasible. So we decided to set out on our journey back that same afternoon. Shortly before, the son of Father Schmidt told us that our Jacob had spoken with several Hottentots who stand guard here, and had addressed and admonished them in such a way that he had stood amazed at it. He said to them among other things that they should seek Jesus Christ while he is still to be found; and they declared in turn that they very much wished to get free of the guard, so as to be able to go back to Baviaanskloof and hear the word of God. Jacob replied: 'They should only show faithfulness at their post; for if they kept it faithfully too, they would experience the nearness and help of the everywhere-present Saviour, who lets himself be found willingly by all who seek him from the heart and ask after him' [18]. When after this address he was asked by a woman who came up: 'whether it looked like that in his inmost heart too, as he spoke here?', he answered: 'If Juffrouw could see into my heart, you would see that what I have spoken is truth in my heart.'

<!-- lines 9692-9710 -->
"We left the Cape with two other good friends, and on our way visited our friend Licko [19], who was very weak. He rejoiced greatly over our visit and the love shown him by it, saying that it was a true strengthening to him. On taking leave he gave us to understand that we would hardly see each other again in this life. He is going into eternity as a poor sinner who praises and spreads abroad the grace of Jesus alone. On 15 April we continued our journey, and in the evening found such poor lodging that we had to go to bed hungry. After undergoing many more hardships and difficulties, we arrived on the evening of 16 April at our Brother Schwinn's [20]."

<!-- lines 9711-9749 -->
On 18 April two persons were appointed candidates for baptism in our conference. On the 19th, in the meeting at the church prayer, we delivered the greetings of the brothers and sisters whom we had spoken with in Cape Town, [and told the people that we] wished to remember them by name in this prayer [21], and to pray for a prosperous sea voyage for them. All our people wished they had been at the Cape too, to see the brothers and sisters, and said to our Jacob that he had reason to give thanks to the Saviour, who had let this good fortune come to him. This Jacob also knew so much to tell, and was asked so many questions, that he could not finish with his stories. Sister Woltersdorf had made us a present of a sister's cap, which we had our Sister Martha try on; and it pleased the Hottentots so much that they could not see enough of it. "Oh," they said, "if only we might soon have the joy of seeing sisters here!" To the question "what good would that do them?" they answered: "It is quite another thing, after all, when one can talk with people of one's own sex." In the evening a man visited us who was born at Lobenstein in the Vogtland, and has now lived 33 years in this country. He has fathered a daughter by a Hottentot woman, who was 20 years old, and whom he would gladly bring to our school, so that she could be baptised in time. He asked how long he would have to leave her with us for that end. We answered that we did not take anyone into the school in order to learn something, but in the hope that such a one could be converted to the Saviour. Anyone who was not set on that we sent away again [22].

<!-- lines 9750-9794 -->
On 23 April Brother Schwinn was called to a sick Hottentot, and found him in a gladdening state of soul. Old Lena also asked to be visited, since she was so weakened by age that she was no longer able to walk. Brother Marsveld could rejoice over her. On 24 April Andreas Otto came with the son of Mr Claude, to look over the place bought in our neighbourhood. On the 25th some more women and children arrived to live here. On the 26th, after the meeting, several Hottentots came to speak with us about the unrest of their hearts [23]. Among these was a man who was so shaken that he trembled and quaked and could not stand on his feet, which made us fear that the falling sickness was coming over him. To our question what ailed him, for a good while he could answer nothing. At last he burst out in a flood of tears and said: "I hear the great words of God and have not lived by them," and so on. He was directed to the Friend of sinners. On the 28th [24] we spoke once more with Susanna, who is soon to partake of Holy Communion with us, and she was confirmed on the 29th. On the 30th several of our baptised came to be spoken with, and we could rejoice greatly over the humble state of their hearts and the declarations that flowed from it. This time we put off the general speaking; but everyone who wished to was free to come. As to our outward living, we were in need, because our 4 oxen had become so thin that we could make no use of them, and we were obliged to borrow oxen from the Hottentots to have the necessaries fetched with our cart.

## Notes

1. From here the Dutch is set out as a continuous "continuation" for readers, with page numbers of its own; it speaks of "our reader" and gives the missionaries' own accounts in quotation marks. It corresponds to the German *Von den Hottentotten. Aus dem Diario von Baviaanskloof* (A363596, entries 03 to 10).
2. *Den 1. April ſpraken wy met onze gedoopten*. The German diary has "was the washing of the brothers and sisters", which its note 1 takes for the foot-washing; the Dutch shows that *Waſchen* is a misreading of *Sprechen*, the speaking, as in A363595. The next sentence, *By ſommigen van hun wenſchten wy een meer levendig hart te ontdekken*, settles the German diary's note 2.
3. The Dutch settles the German diary's garbled readings here: the "Easter liturgy" (its note 6), the gathering "at the break of day" (its note 7), and on the 6th "the liturgy to God the Son" (its note 8).
4. Line 9388 is missing. The transcription runs *Den 9ᵈ had Br: Kuhnel een be⸗* / *plaats, een Uur van ons afgelegen in bezit heeft*. The German diary has "Brother Kühnel had a visit from Baas Teunis's son, who laid out the place an hour from here". The words in square brackets are supplied from there and from the sense.
5. *eenen zekeren Heer met naame Claude*. The Dutch, like the German diary, treats Mr Claude as the gentleman of the visit of 18 February 1795, whom the February diary calls Clüde (entry 21); this supports the glossary's guess that Claude and Clüde are the same man. The "son of Mr Claude" appears on the 24th.
6. *dat met een Deenſch Schip Herrnhutters op de Kaap waren aangekomen*. This settles the German diary's *Herrn Gütter* (its note 10). Herrnhuters: a common name for the Moravians.
7. *eene groote Vlakte met verſcheiden boere hofſteden*; the German diary has "a great plain with grazing cows and oxen". The German diary also calls the host "a friend" and names "Mr Koster", where the Dutch has "the wife of the farmer" and "her son-in-law".
