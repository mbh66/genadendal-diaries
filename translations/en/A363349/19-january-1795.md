<!-- lines 7412-7416 -->
Diary of the 3 Brothers Marsveld, Schwinn and Kühnel at the Cape, from 1 to 31 January 1795.

<!-- lines 7417-7433 -->
On 1 January, already early at 5 o'clock, the Hottentots came to wish us happiness for the new year, and this went on so [until they had all been. At 10 o'clock was] the meeting [1], in which Brother Kühnel spoke on the words of Paul to the Ephesians, chapter 4, verses 22 to 25, and in a prayer implored our dear Lord for his blessing, not only for this day but for the whole year now begun, and that he himself would open by his Holy Spirit the hearts that are still closed, so that the word of life might find entrance. The meeting was very well attended. In the afternoon we had the adoration with our baptised, and promised our Lord and God anew to be obedient and faithful, at which his peace made itself powerfully felt.

<!-- lines 7433-7463 -->
On the 2nd a Hottentot came from about 15 miles from here. He had long had a great longing to live here, but so much evil had been told him of us that we stood amazed at it; among other things, that we taught the people all bad things, for example stealing, murder and so on; that 40 Hottentots had been hanged at once; and that after New Year we must all go away from here. The farmer he works for asked him whether he too wanted to go to school. Answer: "Yes, Baas, I want to hear God's word too." The farmer told him that if he wanted to go there, he would shoot him dead. The Hottentot said: "I have been at the Cape and asked about the hanging [2], but no one knew anything of it. I had no rest; I must come myself, and see and hear, and speak with my own brother and friend." (He has his brother here, who is one of the candidates for baptism.) "Now I have looked at the matter myself and heard it, spoken with my friends, and found it all lies. Now I will go at once to the farmer and settle accounts with him; then I ask that I may come here again and stay here to live." He thanked us for all that he had heard here of the becoming man of Jesus and of his death.

<!-- lines 7463-7477 -->
In our conference on the 3rd we implored our dear Lord on our knees for his counsel and help, and asked him to let us know his will at all times by his Holy Spirit in what we have to undertake with this people, so as to meet the will of his heart. We also asked him to let us live together in love, as in the past year, and promised him anew childlike obedience, at which he let us feel his peace. We also put before him one for baptism at the Festival of the Heathen, whom he assigned to us.

<!-- lines 7477-7487 -->
On the 4th in the afternoon Brother Marsveld was called to a candidate for baptism who was so weak that he could speak little with her. Of her we are convinced that if she dies, the dear Saviour will take her to himself. Towards evening a son of young Mrs Lind came, from about 3 miles from here. He stayed for supper with us, and there was another with him; they would not stay the night, but rode back at 9 o'clock.

<!-- lines 7487-7517 -->
On the 5th the schools began again, which were very full, for now they are all back from the farmers. Since tomorrow is the Festival of the Heathen, we had the school called off, and specially reminded them of this important festival for them. This caused a great stirring, especially among the children, when Brother Kühnel told them that the wise men from the East had brought the dear Saviour gifts. If they now also wanted to give him a gift that would make him glad, they should give him [their hearts anew as his own] [3], which he had bought so dearly with his blood; and he went on to say that all those whose whole mind it was to renounce Satan and to live only for the Saviour in this world should stand up, fall on their knees with him, and pray the dear Saviour for his mercy. Not one of them stayed sitting, but they all fell on their knees with him, and the greater part began to pray aloud; and it was not long before everything melted in tears, and the prayer had to be closed because of the strong and loud weeping. Even in the eyes of those of whom we have little hope the tears stood. May the dear Saviour have further mercy on us.

<!-- lines 7518-7542 -->
(Since at this time we had thought much, and spoken with one another, about what Baas Teunis made known to us on 7 December last year, we at last took the resolution that Brother Marsveld should travel to the Cape and see how things stand for us with the government. Since we also cannot send our diaries and letters to the Cape at present, because few farmers ride there, being busy now with threshing their corn, and we would still like to let you have news of us often, Brother Marsveld was to take this with him, in the hope that a ship will soon leave for the fatherland. Father Schmidt also promised, when he was with us, to put our cart, which is in a bad state, into order again if we would send it to the Cape; and since we also needed various things from there, we resolved to send the cart along and put on it our necessities that we had to have from the Cape.

<!-- lines 7542-7570 -->
On the 6th, the Festival of the Heathen, we had a meeting early with our baptised, in which we first prayed a part of the litany, and then some letters from the history of Greenland were read. Soon after 8 o'clock was holy baptism, which Brother Marsveld held. The baptised woman was named Christina [4]. The meeting was very well attended. In the afternoon at 2 o'clock there was again a meeting with the baptised. We very much wished that we had had accounts of other heathen stations translated into Dutch, so as to be able to tell them something of them; but since we have nothing of the kind, we filled this meeting with stories of other heathen, and we had a pleasant time together. We also reminded them that on this day, in our fatherland as in all the congregations, they are remembered in prayer before the Saviour, as are all heathen in all places. They declared their thankfulness to the dear Saviour, who had provided for them so that they too have received teachers who make the way to salvation known to them. Our dear Lord made this day a day of blessing for us as well as for the Hottentots.

<!-- lines 7571-7590 -->
In the afternoon our Brother Marsveld set out on his journey to the Cape, accompanied by our blessings. Since Holy Communion falls in this week, it was not a matter of indifference to us to put it off; but we could do nothing else this time, because the journey, if it did not go ahead now, would have to be put off a long time again because of the moonlight. For by day one cannot travel because of the too great heat, since the cattle cannot stand it; and when there is no moonlight at night, then [it is dangerous. On the 8th several came] to speak with us about the sorrow of their hearts [5]. One of them was quite disconsolate over the words that those who would not turn to Jesus will cry out on that day, namely: "Ye mountains, fall on us, and ye hills, cover us!"

<!-- lines 7591-7600 -->
On the 9th the candidate for baptism who had been excluded came and asked for forgiveness, but did not name her transgressions. When we put her behaviour to her, she said that it was not true [6]. We told her that she had grieved the dear Saviour very much and should pray him for forgiveness; and when she could truthfully say and testify that she had received forgiveness from him, then we too would forgive her from our hearts.

<!-- lines 7601-7621 -->
On the 10th our sister Elisabeth came and said with many tears that she thanked the dear Saviour day and night that he had sent teachers and let her receive the great grace of being baptised, and now also of partaking of his body and blood in Holy Communion. This night she had become quite uneasy that she had not yet thanked us for this great love, that we had come over the great water to make known to them the way to salvation; and also our brothers in the fatherland, who have sent us to them. Eva made a like declaration, and said: "Oh, how shall I thank the Saviour for it!" [7]

<!-- lines 7617-7639 -->
On the 12th we picked part of our beans, with which we are richly provided this year. Here the beans are got out of the pods in no other way than by beating them with sticks [8]. The farmers who have many of them have them trodden out by horses, like the corn. Beating them out with sticks takes much time. So we have made some flails to thresh them out with. This was something new for the Hottentots, and pleased them very well, because it goes very fast; but they show little wish for threshing, because the arms get very tired from it, and they would rather leave it to the horses, or take their time and beat the corn out with sticks. It is not at all easy here either, because one has no barns, and must do everything in the heat of the sun, which presses out many a drop of sweat.

<!-- lines 7640-7643 -->
On the 13th we received a letter from Brother Marsveld, from which we learned that he was still well, but that he had been able to achieve nothing with the Edle Heeren [9].

<!-- lines 7644-7674 -->
On the 14th Brother Jacob was at the post at Baas Teunis's. He asked him whether any of the Hottentots had gone away yet. Jacob said: "No!" He declared that he should tell the Hottentots to see to it that they got away; he would soon come, and those who would not obey he would bring to the Cape. How we feel at such threats our dear brothers can well imagine. He also said that Hottentots had run away from the Landdrost at Swellendam who were supposed to be living here (but this is not the truth, for they do not live here), and the Landdrost meant to make a complaint about it at the Cape. It seems as if Baas Teunis and his wife are now ruled by the devil. He has himself been to the Landdrost at Swellendam, and people want to say for certain that it was to carry out something against us with him. [As soon as he came back from there,] he went off again the next day to the Cape, on the very same day that Brother Marsveld travelled there [10]. Now he came with such an order, and we look forward longingly to Brother Marsveld, to learn something more from him. God our Lord and Saviour alone must help, for all help of men seems to be in vain.

<!-- lines 7674-7679 -->
On the 15th Baas Teunis again sent a message to the Hottentots that they should see to it that they got away from here, or else it would not go well. Through such sharp words they do fall into some perplexity [11].

<!-- lines 7680-7689 -->
On the 16th 15 men came and asked what they should do about the order to move away from here. But we cannot give them much advice in these circumstances, and believed that the best was to keep quite still, according to the order of the government; and when they come to us to ask advice, to tell them that we could not advise them in this, but they must do what seemed best to their minds.

<!-- lines 7690-7703 -->
In the evening at 10 o'clock our Brother Marsveld came home again, fairly well. He had got an ailment in his throat, so that he could not swallow well; we hope it will soon get better with him. He brought with him the Daily Texts for this year and 30 copies of the New Testament [12], and also 2 books, the life of Count Zinzendorf and the Wholesome Doctrine, and some weekly and congregation news. Brother Marsveld also writes the following account of his journey and his business:

<!-- lines 7704-7744 -->
"On 6 January at 4 o'clock in the afternoon I set out from here for Cape Town with the best blessings of my dear brothers, and rode through the night to the Bot River, where I arrived at 1 o'clock. After I had unsaddled my horse, I let it go into the grass, and lay down to sleep on the earth under the open sky. But when day began to break, I was quite cold and stiff from the dew and the night air. I now took my flintlock to hunt something; but as soon as I had climbed a small hill, I saw a carriage far off drawn by 6 horses. I went towards it to see who it was, and when I came down the hill again, I found that it was the Frenchman mentioned in our last diary, who has come here because of the troubles of war. His name is Zinkhank [13], and he was very glad to meet me here; and when he saw that I was quite stiff with cold, he asked whether I would not take something. I said yes, whereupon he gave me about 2 thimblefuls to drink, which warmed me greatly. He asked me to get in with him, but I thanked him; but at his request I promised to be at his place, Eckstein, at 12 o'clock and to dine with him. I went to look for my horse, and the Hottentots for their oxen, and at 11 o'clock we arrived at that place, where we stayed until 4 o'clock and then went on together towards the Cape. Since with my horse I got over the rocks faster than he did with the carriage, I rode ahead and waited for him at the foot of Hottentots Holland Kloof. Here I got into the carriage with him, and we drove to Mr Morgel's [14], where we stayed the night.

<!-- lines 7745-7776 -->
"On the 8th in the morning we continued our journey [to the Cape. As we came near the town,] I experienced a special protection [15]: several wagons drawn by oxen met us, at which our horses took fright and sprang in among the oxen. I got out of the carriage in good time, and Mr Zinkhank soon found himself obliged to do the same; but everything passed off without any harm. This gentleman has asked me several times to dine with him at midday, and said that I need only let him know when I would come. I drove with him to his lodging, which belongs to the Emperor's consul and is very fine; the consul himself also welcomed me very kindly. Mr Zinkhank at once told him of our Hottentots, that there was a great difference between them and those who lived further up country. At dusk I came to our old friend Martinus Schmidt, who with his whole family received me very kindly and lodged me with them. He was very thankful for the greetings that I brought him by word of mouth from the Unity Elders' Conference [16], and resolved to write to Brother Reichel himself, which he afterwards did.

<!-- lines 7776-7825 -->
"On the 9th I went first to the Gezaghebber, Mr Reenius [17], and told him that the order had come from the Commissioner, Mr Sluysken, that all Hottentots who had come to us with their cattle should take it back to their former place. I put it to him that these people could not subsist without their cattle, and that several of them would have to fetch their slaughter cattle from as much as 3 days' journey away; and so I wished to ask him for good advice. He answered that I should go to the Commissioner himself and ask him to take back the order mentioned, so that the Hottentots might stay with their cattle in our district, where there was no lack of room. I thanked him for this good advice. Mr Berg, the cashier, was also present, and said: 'This happens only through the stirring up of Baas Teunis at the post.' I now went to the Commissioner, Mr Sluysken, who was quite friendly, and I put my business very modestly, but in vain. He said that he was so overrun by the farmers with complaints that if the Hottentots were given instruction they would get behind their secrets. I asked him politely to allow me to tell him my thoughts about it, and not to take it amiss; to which he gave me the answer: 'You may speak quite freely.' So I went on: 'What secrets have the farmers but ploughing, sowing and reaping? And those they must still learn from the Hottentots; otherwise they have no secret.' I spoke further: 'If the farmers thought that the Hottentots might read their letters, they need only do as Your Honour does and seal them; we too seal our letters and are in no danger.' His answer was: 'That is all very well, dear man; but as to the cattle I cannot change it [18].' So I went away again without having achieved anything; but I was to come to him once more before I left here. Then I visited some good friends, such as Commandant Gordon and Mr Lesueur, the councillor of police [19], by whom I was received very kindly.

<!-- lines 7825-7871 -->
"On the 10th I dined with Commandant Gordon, where much was said of the fatherland and its present circumstances. I also visited the 3 Reformed ministers, who were friendlier than before, and I visited the Lutheran minister Kolver [20], who invited me to a meal, but I had to decline. On the 11th I went to the Reformed church [21]. On the 12th I went to the Commissioner again to take leave, and to try once more whether I could still achieve anything towards taking back the order mentioned, so that the Hottentots could keep their cattle at Baviaanskloof; but it was all in vain. So I took a friendly leave, and commended us to his protection, which he also promised, and assured me that he would take care for our best. Then I went to the Gezaghebber, Mr Reenius, who asked me how I had fared with the Commissioner. On my answer, when I told him that everything must stay as it now was, he shook his head and said that the former [22] would have to answer for it; for his part he could do nothing in it now, as he had helped us as much as possible formerly, when he still held that charge. I assured him that we were fully convinced of it, to which he added: 'Be assured that if I can still do anything in it, I shall not fail to do it.' He wished us God's blessing on the work of God among the Hottentots. Mr Gordon, to whom I also went afterwards, asked me whether we had made a beginning with forging knives. Answer: 'We have no workshop for it yet.' Question: 'How is that?' Answer: 'The Commissioner, Mr Sluysken, has forbidden us to build anything whatever.' Mr Gordon was very surprised at this, and promised me to speak with the Commissioner about the matter.

<!-- lines 7872-7885 -->
"On the 13th I was busy taking leave. On the 14th I left the Cape, ashamed at all the good I had enjoyed with our old friend Martin Schmidt; he treated us as his own children. On the 15th I arrived at Mr Morgel's, where after taking some refreshment I continued my journey. My horse I had to leave behind there, because it had put out its hip and could not go on along the road. On the 16th in the evening I arrived again with my dear brothers at Baviaanskloof, whom I found well and in health, full of praise and thanks to our dear Saviour."

<!-- lines 7886-7888 -->
The diary goes on to say:

<!-- lines 7889-7903 -->
On the 17th in our conference the dear Saviour assigned us one as a candidate for baptism. Since we had put off Holy Communion until Brother Marsveld had come back from the Cape, we were in consultation today whether to hold it together now. But we resolved to put it off altogether this time, because our minds, as well as those of our fellow partakers, were far too distracted in the present circumstances. Today we heard that a Hottentot had been at Baas Teunis's, and that he had been extraordinarily angry, had railed very violently against Baviaanskloof, and had uttered shameful threats against our place.

<!-- lines 7904-7910 -->
Early on the 18th a farmer came (a Frisian by birth [23]) who had lived nearest of all to the Kaffers, but was driven away by them, and his place was set on fire. He was looking here for Hottentots who had run away from him, but did not find them. In the afternoon he rode away again.

<!-- lines 7910-7922 -->
On the 19th many Hottentots went into the forest 7 hours away [to dry peaches,] which are there in great quantity and free for everyone [24]. Farmers drive there and fetch whole wagonloads. The Hottentots stay there for some time so as to dry them at once, and while they are at it they can dry a great deal, for there is no lack of peaches, since they grow wherever a stone or pip of them falls.

<!-- lines 7922-7933 -->
On the 20th the Hottentots came and said that tomorrow they meant to go to the government at the Cape, to see whether they could not obtain by pleading leave to stay here. We told them that they must do as they judged best. Another Hottentot also came, who had served a farmer about 10 miles from here. The farmer had given him a receipt that he had served out his time. Baas Teunis told him that he must leave his cattle where he had been.

<!-- lines 7933-7948 -->
On the 21st, at the break of day, the Hottentots set out on the road to the Cape, and among them were 2 of the candidates for baptism. Over several of them we could rejoice; but taken as a whole we wished them more feeling and life in the heart [25]. In these days we had to exclude a man from the candidates for baptism; he had got drunk at a farmer's on the way. Yet he asked to be allowed to go into the meeting of the candidates for baptism; but it seems as if he were quite dead, so we had no confidence to take him back for the present.

<!-- lines 7949-7991 -->
At 10 o'clock Baas Teunis came, with another man with him. He came as a sergeant in his uniform with his sabre at his side, as he has never shown himself here before. He was very modest and friendly, and we were so to him too, as well as we could. After staying a little while he said that he had come on the Governor's business, to make known to the Hottentots the order that had often been given, and to speak with us about it, to learn our thoughts on how the living and the future of the Hottentots would be. Then he gave us a letter to read, in which he was authorised to carry out the matter: all those who did not follow the order he was to take prisoner with the help of the field corporals and deliver to the Cape, where they would be punished as disobedient; but he was to give notice of it. It also said in the same letter that he was to help us wherever it was fitting. But nothing was said in it about his speaking with us about what we thought of their future living [26]. We told him that we did not concern ourselves with this matter at all, because we had no understanding of it; nor could we say whether this place was too small for the cattle, because we do not understand farming [27]; but this much we could say, that so far not a single head of cattle had had to die of hunger. He answered that that would surely come in the rainy season. Because the Hottentots were not at home now, he could not make this order known. He said: "When they come home again, I shall surely learn what they have achieved; but I believe it will stay as it is." In the afternoon he rode home again.

<!-- lines 7992-8009 -->
On the 22nd, when several Hottentots were busy in our garden [picking] beans, [...] and shouted aloud [28]. When they were asked what that meant, they said that a boomslang was lying on top of a post. This snake cannot be killed without danger except by shooting, which Brother Kühnel did. We have shot more of this kind already. A few days ago a farmer about 5 miles from here was bitten by such a snake in his garden in the evening, and by the next morning he was already dead. So far the dear Saviour has kept us from such accidents.

<!-- lines 8009-8019 -->
On the 23rd our sister Anna came and asked: she had heard that a brother of ours was among the Kaffers, and taught them just as he did us, and proclaimed the Gospel to them, and that they would learn well; was this true? This brother was said to be quite deprived of his sight, and to have come not from the fatherland but from somewhere else. She was told that she should put no faith in it, for if this were so, we too would know something of it.

<!-- lines 8019-8024 -->
On the 24th a Hottentot came who had served a farmer but had run away from him; he wanted to stay here about a month. We told him that this could not be; he should see to it that he got away from here again.

<!-- lines 8025-8026 -->
On the 25th young Mrs Mary came to us with one of her sons.

<!-- lines 8026-8041 -->
On the 26th the same Hottentot who had come here on the 24th of this month to stay a month came again, and said that he had thought it over: he wanted to stay here and live, hear God's word, and let his children go to school to learn. We told him that we were no schoolmasters, and had not come for that either; he should just go where he himself had said. When he would still not let himself be turned away, we told him that he must fetch a receipt from the farmer he had served, that his time of service was over. This man seemed very strange to us.

<!-- lines 8041-8045 -->
On the 27th a young woman was laid in the earth; she is already the fourth [29] to die of the same sickness that raged last year and is now beginning again.

<!-- lines 8045-8046 -->
On the 28th many Hottentots went again to dry peaches.

<!-- lines 8046-8081 -->
On the 29th towards evening the Hottentots who had gone to the Cape on the 21st, 18 in number [30], came home again very sad. They had been twice with the Edle Heer. The first time he said that they must move away again, and the second time that what he had already said must stand, as Baas Teunis also had it in writing: namely, that they must take their cattle back where it had been before, because their cattle took the neighbours' pasture from them, so that these could not subsist; and [he asked] where they would then go, when they had finished [learning], if the farmers had taken their places from them [31]. The Hottentots' answer was that they meant to stay there and not move away again. The Hottentots also have more insight into our cause than the Christians; for these say that when they have been taught and baptised, they must all move back to their places, and others come in their stead, and so it must go on. Against this the Hottentots say that they will not go away from here, but stay here, where the word of God is proclaimed, and not go back into the wilderness. So say those who are not yet in closer union with us. [Of the baptised several have said that they] will not let go of the word of God, and will not go away from us either, let it go where it will; for if they did this, they would regard it as if they denied our dear Lord [32]. The suffering of this time lasts only a short time [33].

<!-- lines 8082-8127 -->
These Hottentots had also been with the Fiscal, Mr Reinveld [34]. He said that he would gladly do his best that they could keep their cattle, but the Edle Heer insisted all too firmly that they must take it away. They also spoke with the captain of the Hottentots, named Stoffel, who is on guard at the Cape. He said that Baas Teunis and he had brought the matter so far with the Edle Heer that all the cattle must go away. Baas Teunis had given him drink to swill, and 2 shillings on top of it, to confirm to the Edle Heer that the cattle of the surrounding farmers were quite crowded out by the cattle of the Hottentots and the farmers could not subsist; which the captain, as he himself says, did, because he had got plenty to swill and 2 shillings on top of it. This captain of the Hottentots lives on the place, an hour from here, that Baas Teunis's son had, which old Teunis now has again [35]. The other Hottentots also complain much about this captain, that he was to blame for Baas Teunis's son getting this place: had he taken thought for the good of the Hottentots, this would not have happened; but because he likes to drink, he consented over a bottle of wine. That the Hottentots' cattle take the farmers' pasture is quite untrue, for their cattle come on no farmer's land except the place of Baas Teunis, which he has got back from his son, who did not dare to drive the Hottentots' cattle away. But since old Teunis has this place, they dare not go there any more. Yet everything that is said by the Hottentots is taken for lies.

<!-- lines 8127-8142 -->
On the 30th we spoke with one of our candidates for baptism who had also been at the Cape. He agreed with what is reported above, and said further that they had wanted to speak with the Edle Heer about our dear Father, but he had forbidden them, saying that they must be silent, and told them to see to it that they got away from here, or he would have them beaten away. The Fiscal, Mr Reinveld, asked them whether they believed in God. Answer: "Yes, and in Jesus Christ too." Question: whether they also knew where Jesus Christ had come from? Answer: "He was born of the Virgin Mary." At this the Fiscal said: "That is true! In him you should believe; for there is no other salvation to be found than in Jesus Christ."

<!-- lines 8143-8178 -->
On the 31st. Since the Hottentots mentioned have come back from the Cape and told how things look, the others are very sad about it, and come to ask what they are to do, since they would not leave the word of God. We can advise them nothing but to obey the authorities. But how we feel about it, dear brothers and sisters, no one will be able to believe. Against people who are anxious about their welfare and salvation, and who gladly hear of their Creator and Redeemer, other people are bought with money and wine to bear false witness before the authorities! To know all this, and to have to sit still by it, [if one does not want to make the fire ten times worse, is] not easy for us [36]. Yet God, who holds the government, has also taken his power from the devil. He will know how to carry out his work, so that in the end it serves after all to the honour of his name, although we cannot see it at once. Perhaps in this way he means to draw some more of these people to himself, since there are still many of them there who themselves do not know why [37]. Yet there are many who mean it sincerely with all their hearts and who must suffer under it, and this grieves us very painfully. Our prayer to the dear Saviour is that in these circumstances he would keep us with himself and his wounds, and give us the needful insight into how we are to conduct ourselves in them; to which end we commend ourselves to all our dear brothers and sisters in the congregations, in their prayers before our dear Lord.

<!-- lines 8179-8181 -->
Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn and Joh. Chrt. Kühnel

## Notes

1. Line 7420 is missing. The transcription runs *en dat duurde zo voort* / *de Vergadering, waarin Br. Kühnel*. The German diary has "and this went on until they had all been. At 10 o'clock was the meeting". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
2. *heb my naar het ophang ondervraagd*, "asked about the hanging". The German diary has "asked about the arrangements", probably a misreading.
3. Line 7502 is missing. The transcription runs *zo zouden zy Hem van* / *geven, dewelke Hy met Zynen Bloede zo duur gekogt had*. The German diary has "they should give him their hearts anew, wholly as his own, all of which he had bought with his precious blood". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
4. The German diary has Christina, and adds that Brother Marsveld spoke on Acts 8, verses 26 to 40.
5. Line 7584 is missing. The transcription runs *als er by Nacht geen Maanlicht is, dan* / *scheiden met ons te spreeken*. The German diary has "and when there is no moonlight at night, it is dangerous", followed by a long entry for the 7th about Bedgen and her husband, and then "On the 8th several came who spoke with us about the distress of their hearts". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. The Dutch does not have the entry for the 7th; one line of the transcription could not hold it.
6. *zeide zÿ, dat het niet waar was*; the German diary has "she said that it was no more".
7. The German diary goes on with a visit on the 11th from young Mrs Grünewald and her family; the Dutch does not have it.
8. *met ſtokken uit de ſchil geſlaagen*. The German diary has "to beat them out with horses", which does not fit the next sentence; the Dutch reading is the right one.
9. The German diary adds that Brother Marsveld had heard that Neuwied had been burned, and that a farmer from 16 days' journey up country stayed the night; the Dutch has neither.
10. Line 7666 is missing. The transcription runs *om met hem tegen ons iets uitte-* / *ging hy 's anderendaags weer weg naar de Kaap*. The German diary has "to hatch something against us with him. As soon as he came back from there, he went off again the next day to the Cape". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. Where the German diary has "Brother Jacob was out hunting at Baas Teunis's" on the 14th, the Dutch has "at the post"; where it has "5 Hottentots", the Dutch has no number.
11. *Door zulke ſcherpe uitdrukkingen komen zy wel wat in verlegenheid*; the German diary is garbled here (its note 14).
12. *30 ſtuks Nieuwe Teſtamente boeken*. The German diary has "the 30 rixdollars maintenance money". The 30 Dutch Testaments were promised in Brother Schmidt's letter from Holland (entry 29 of A363595, 12 December 1794), so the Dutch reading is probably the right one. *de Levensbeſchryving des Graaven van Zinzendorf, en der heilzaamen Leer*: the German diary names the same two books (its note 15). The Dutch does not have the German diary's passage about books that Domine Miller took on to Batavia.
13. *Linksank*; the German diary has Zinkhank. He is the gentleman from Flanders of 21 December 1794 (entry 29 of A363595), whom the Dutch here calls a Frenchman. *zyn plaats Eckſtein*: not identified.
14. *den Heer Morgel*. The German diary has *Br.* before this and the other names in Brother Marsveld's report (its note 20); the Dutch has *Heer* throughout.
15. Line 7748, the first on page 129b, is missing. The transcription runs *Den 8 's Morgens Zetten wy onze reiſe verder voort* / *men, ondervond ik eene byzondere bewaaring*. The German diary has "we continued our journey to the Cape. As we came near the town I experienced God's protection". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
16. *van de Uniteits Ouderlingen Conferentie*. The German diary is garbled here (its note 21).
17. Johan Isaac Rhenius and Abraham Josias Sluysken; see the glossary.
18. *maar wegens het Vee kan ik het niet veranderen*, "as to the cattle". The German diary has "because of the war", probably a misreading of *Vieh*.
19. *den Heer.* / *Polieziynaad Lesieur*: probably *Polieziyraad*, "councillor of police"; the German diary has *Polizeymſtr*. See Lesueur (1794) in the glossary.
20. *Colſer*; the German diary has Colver (its note 24). Probably the Lutheran minister Kolver; see Kolver in the glossary.
21. The German diary adds the text of the sermon (Acts 9, verses 10 to 12) and a letter to Brother Rothe at Zeist; the Dutch has neither. It also has "even a goat shed" in Brother Marsveld's answer to Gordon below.
22. *de eerſtgenoemde*, "the first-named", that is, the Commissioner.
23. *van geboorte een Prieſs*: probably *Vries*, a Frisian. The German diary is garbled here (its note 26).
24. Line 7912 is missing. The transcription runs *in 't boſch 7 Uuren ver* / *dewelke daar in groote menigte voor ieder een vry Zyn*. The German diary has "into a forest 7 hours from here to dry peaches, which are there in great quantity and free for everyone". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
25. The German diary has "Today we also had the speaking of the candidates for baptism" before these words; the Dutch does not, and the words seem to refer to the candidates who went to the Cape.
26. *Doch wierd er niets in hetzelve gemeld, dat hy met ons Zoude ſpreeken*. The German diary is garbled here (its note 28), and goes on to say that the brethren took the question as a temptation; the Dutch does not have this.
27. *om dat wy geen Boerdery verſtaan*, "because we do not understand farming". The German diary has "rivers" (its note 29).
28. Line 7994 is missing. The transcription runs *bezig waren met boonen te* / *denzelven en ſchreeuwden over luid*. The German diary has only "several Hottentots raised an alarm in our garden". The word in square brackets is supplied from the sense and from the bean harvest on the 12th; the rest of the lost line is not supplied.
29. *de vierde*; the German diary has "the third". The farmer bitten by the boomslang lived 5 miles away in the Dutch and 3 in the German.
30. *18 in getal*; the German diary has 16.
31. *en waar zy alsdan als zy uitgeteerd hadden heen wilde*: *uitgeteerd*, "used up, eaten up", may be a misreading of *uitgeleerd*, "finished learning", which the German diary has. The words in square brackets are supplied from the sense.
32. Line 8076 is missing. The transcription runs *Zoo zeggen die geene, welke met ons nog in geen nadere Verbindenis ſtaan.* / *het Woord Gods niet willen aflaaten*. The German diary has "Of the baptised, several have said that they will not let go of the word of our dear Lord". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
33. *Het lyden deezes tyds duurt maar een korten tyd*. The German diary's garbled sentence (its note 35) is the same.
34. The Fiscal Willem Stephanus van Ryneveld; see Fiscal van Ryneveld in the glossary.
35. *dewelke tegenwoordig de Oude Teunis weder heeft*; the German diary is garbled here (its note 38).
36. Line 8158 is missing. The transcription runs *daarby ſtil zitten moeten, als men het Vuur* / *voor ons niet ligt*. The German diary has "if one does not want to make the fire ten times worse, is no easy thing". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
37. *Moglyk wil hy van deeze Menſchen op dien aart nog eenige tot Zig trekken*; the German diary is garbled here (its note 41).
