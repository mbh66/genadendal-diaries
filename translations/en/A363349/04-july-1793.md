<!-- lines 1554-1587 -->
On 1 July they came back from Baas Teunis with the message that he had promised to write to the Cape. It is so sad that all who live here, without exception, are so poor. For if there were only some who had something, the need would not be so great, since the Hottentots are very sociable and generous in giving food and drink. Old Lena has nothing at all but what she receives from the others. She goes round the kraals, and where she finds something, there she stays. For those who have something give gladly as long as there is anything.

On 2 July a Hottentot woman who came to visit us said that some time ago she had [become very sad. When we asked whether] some harm had come to her [1], she answered no; but she thought much about God and about what she heard in the meeting, and did not know whether it might perhaps be owing to that. She had not been able to put off any longer coming to tell us. We pointed her to the Saviour and advised her to lament her sadness to him, who is as willing as he is mighty to comfort the sorrowful. She thanked us and promised to follow the advice we had given her.

On the 3rd it rained so hard that we could hold neither the children's meeting nor the evening meeting.

<!-- lines 1588-1632 -->
On the 4th the Bastard Hottentot who had undertaken to plough on the far side of the river came to tell us that the farmer named Gildenhuysen [2], who lives an hour from there, had come to forbid him to plough and sow, threatening to have him soundly thrashed if he refused, and alleging that this piece of land belonged to him; which, however, is not true, since according to the Hottentots it belongs to the Sergeants River. We advised him to go to Baas Teunis, which he did at once, and on 5 July he came back with a letter from Baas Teunis to the Landdrost at Swellendam, two days' journey from here on horseback. As soon as the rivers have fallen further, he will ride there.

One of our candidates for baptism came today to tell us that her heart's desire was to share in the great grace of holy baptism. Some days ago she was asked whether she thought much about baptism; to which she then answered: "Not so much about baptism as about the great grace that has come to me, that I can now believe that the Saviour has forgiven me all my sins."

On 6 July this and more such utterances of our candidates for baptism gave us occasion in our conference to think before the face of the dear Saviour about several of them with regard to holy baptism, and it pleased the Saviour to indicate one to us to be baptised, and another to be added to the candidates for baptism.

<!-- lines 1633-1691 -->
On the 7th we came to hear that none of our Hottentots, not even those for whose leave Baas Teunis had asked, would be allowed to come home. The cause of this is said to be that they must march against the Kaffers [3], who are said to have already pushed far into the land inhabited by the Christians, next to the colony under the administration of Graaff-Reinet, murdering in fury and burning everything that belongs to the Christians. Their number is said to be so great that they cannot be opposed by a couple of hundred men. When Brother Kühnel was last at the post, Baas Teunis had received a letter from the Landdrost at Swellendam, giving him news, sad to say [4], of the cruel murdering and burning by this nation. Baas Teunis said on that occasion that he had long foretold that this nation [would] come and avenge the merciless treatment by the Christians, and that the Christians had richly deserved it; for they have driven the Kaffers out of their land and taken it for themselves; they have shot them dead like wild animals, and when a farmer had shot a Kaffer dead, no more fuss was made about it than if he had shot a reebok or a dog. "For the rest," said Baas Teunis, "the Kaffers are a good-natured nation, as I have come to know them in my frequent dealings with them."

The prospect, my dear brethren, of what we have to expect is truly most worrying: on the one side the French are expected, on the other side the Kaffers are coming, murdering and setting fires. It is remarkable that the latter have chosen a time when the government is at war with an enemy against whom it needs all its strength to defend itself. From here one must travel a month to come near the land of the Kaffers. So far, by God's goodness, we still live in rest and peace. God keep it for us, and in his grace turn the threatening danger away from us.

On 8 July the Bastard Hottentot rode with the letter from Baas Teunis to the Landdrost at Swellendam [5].

<!-- lines 1692-1728 -->
On the 9th many Hottentot women came to tell us that they no longer wanted to go on learning to read, because they could not see.

On the 10th an old Hottentot woman came and said that, having heard singing early in the morning, she had thought she had been shut out, which grieved her very much, since she had come alone from far away to hear the word of God. She was told in reply that this meeting, in which she had heard singing, was set apart only for those who no longer wished to live for themselves but only for the Lord, and for that reason had renounced the world and all sinful desires, so as to be pleasing to their Redeemer and Saviour in all that they did and left undone. She answered: "That is also my heart's wish and longing, to give myself wholly to the Lord Jesus, soul and body." This made us tell her further that if she was so minded, and could declare it with her whole heart (God knew best whether it agreed with her words), she should earnestly beg God for the forgiveness of her sins, and he would bring the time when she too might attend this meeting.

On 11 July another Hottentot woman came to live here and, as she said, to hear the word of God.

<!-- lines 1729-1760 -->
On 12 July we began to hold a singing meeting with the children, for the following reason. For some evenings in a row they came begging us to hold a singing meeting with them, in which they take great pleasure and are very keen. They have already learned 7 verses by heart, which they sing very prettily, being gifted by nature with fine [voices] [6]; [these verses] are sung both before and after school. But they would rather come together every evening to sing, if we had time for it. Now, to please them, we have set apart two evenings a week for it, namely Tuesdays and Fridays. Formerly they gathered in our kitchen before the singing meeting began; but as it has become too small for it, we have told them to stay in their kraals until we gave them the sign to come with our bell, for this singing meeting is held only after our evening meeting. Now they watch carefully for this sign and gather beforehand in the kraal nearest our house.

<!-- lines 1760-1789 -->
In the singing meeting the flute or the violin is played, which they like to hear.

On 13 July we asked the woman to be baptised what they said to one another in their kraals about holy baptism. She answered that this matter was a chief subject of their conversations. Among other things she told us that her husband had once dreamed that 3 brothers would come to proclaim the word of God to them and to teach them. "That is us," we told her; "for although we are not brothers by birth, we call one another brothers, because God is our Father, and so we are children of one Father and consequently brothers among ourselves." We take this woman who is to be baptised alone every day, to instil in her a right idea of holy baptism and to explain to her the few questions in our baptismal liturgy. She is just the one of our candidates for baptism who can understand least, while the others understand and learn much better; and yet the Saviour has indicated this simplest among them to us to be the firstfruits of the Hottentots baptised by us.

<!-- lines 1790-1831 -->
On 14 July a quite special emotion was noticed among the hearers during the sermon on the Gospel, Mark 8. After midday a Hottentot woman came to complain to us that when she came into the meeting now, she felt much more unrest and anguish in her heart than before. We pointed her with this unrest to the Saviour, to beg him for the forgiveness of her sins.

On 15 July two Hottentot women were brought to bed, each of a son. We wondered whether it might perhaps go according to their old custom on this occasion, that is, with dancing and leaping. To look into this thoroughly, we went into their kraals in the evening, since until now they have kept their heathen customs very secret from us; but behold, we found nothing of it, and all of them sitting quietly together in the kraals. Otherwise the Hottentots are very great lovers of dancing, and hardly do a couple of Hottentots come together at a fire before they begin to dance at once; seeing it, one cannot think otherwise than that they had lost their senses. For this they use an instrument called a calabash [7], which is a plant like a gourd, to which a small board is fastened and strung with 3 or 4 strings. They make these give a sound with their fingers, which is always the same [8], [and they are] very merry with it. Among our Hottentots who live with us we have (God be praised) seen and discovered nothing of this.

<!-- lines 1831-1870 -->
Today we received a letter from the Cape from Mr Heuser [9], who passes on to us the latest war news from Europe, brought by a packet boat from England; at which we remembered our dear brothers and sisters. According to this news the French General Dumouriez [10] wanted to dine in public in Amsterdam on 17 February and in London on 20 June; but that is rather too high and too broad a claim. Meanwhile we do not know how matters stand, and we look out with the utmost longing for letters from Zeist, which, however, we cannot promise ourselves at this time. It looks dark everywhere; God be our helper.

On 16 July the Hottentot who had been to the Landdrost came back. Because of the high water he had not been able to get over the river, and had therefore stayed away so long. The matter for which he had gone there has thus been decided: at a distance of half an hour from the river a Hottentot can and may plough and sow, without being hindered in it by the farmer. Now our piece of land lies only a quarter of an hour from the river, and the farmer who would not let the Hottentot plough there must allow it at a distance of another quarter of an hour further.

<!-- lines 1867-1929 -->
On 17 July, early, before the meeting of the candidates for baptism began, we first let the woman to be baptised know that this grace would come to her next Friday, the 19th. We had kept it from her until today, thinking that it would not have been good for her to have known it long beforehand, being slow of understanding, while we still wished to give her the necessary instruction about it. After this we made it known to all the candidates for baptism in their meeting, asking them to say nothing of it to the other Hottentots, since we intended to do so ourselves in the evening meeting, which was done. The sighs sent up to God on this occasion by the Hottentot women present, and especially by the candidates for baptism, could not be heard without deep emotion.

On 18 July we received from Mrs Teunis the answer to our letter written to her yesterday, in which we told her what we intended to do tomorrow, and reminded her of her offer to be godmother [11] to the first Hottentot woman baptised, which she had made known even before our house was built. At the time we listened to it in silence, not knowing how things are done in such a case at our other mission stations among the heathen, and having as yet no prospect of whether we would live to see this grace of administering holy baptism to a Hottentot. Now that the dear Saviour counts us worthy of this grace, we were truly at a loss, and it cost us much deliberation whether [to accept it] or not. We were not free to perform the baptism without informing Baas Teunis of it, since he has been charged by the commandant to keep an eye on us and to report to him everything that happens with us. We therefore resolved to inform Baas Teunis of our coming baptism and at the same time to remind his wife of her kind offer, all the more because the woman to be baptised was a Hottentot woman and ought to have a woman as godmother. She answered that she and her husband heard with great pleasure that God had let the proclaiming of his word become so fruitful that a Hottentot woman could now be baptised, and that she would gladly come to share in this blessing.

<!-- lines 1930-1950 -->
On 19 July was the appointed day of baptism, of which we can truly say that it was a day that the Lord has made [12], and on which what is said in our Old and New Testament texts for this day came true. The first reads: "They kept the feast with joy: for the Lord had made them joyful." Ezra 6:22 [13]. "O what a great blessedness / one here experiences, / that he pastures us with his peace / and loves us faithfully and tenderly." The second reads thus: "Your sorrow shall be turned into joy." John 16:20. "Then let me weep for sorrow sometimes, / if only even in weeping I can hold fast to thee."

<!-- lines 1951-1986 -->
The reason why we held this baptism on a weekday and not on a Sunday is this: we feared that on a Sunday far too many farmers would come uninvited [14], for whom we had no benches to sit on, and not enough food and drink to give them, being very poorly provided with these. At 9 o'clock, however, a farmer named Plaisir came, whom we had let know of it because he is a special friend of ours. He was followed at 11 o'clock by Baas Teunis and his wife, his son and his young wife, and 4 more children. Mrs Teunis was asked whether she would like to give the Hottentot woman who was to be baptised a name, and it was noticed that it would please her if she received her own name. Hence this Hottentot woman received at holy baptism, in place of her former name Hanna, the name Anna Maria [15]. The baptism was performed outside in front of our house, because there was not room enough in the house for all the people who had come together, whose number, as someone who knew [16] said, was about 80. The feeling at this solemn act cannot be described with a pen, and we only wish from the bottom of our hearts that its impression may stay for ever with all who were present. As the woman baptised had no clothes of her own, clothes were lent her by others who have some, so that she received holy baptism dressed almost entirely in white.

<!-- lines 1986-2028 -->
And her strings of beads and pearls [17], with which the Hottentot women in general adorn their necks and bodies and to which they are much attached, she had willingly put away, like all the candidates for baptism, after we had made her understand that this ornament was not pleasing in the eyes of Jesus, who [hung] naked and bare for her on the cross. [It was also remarkable that] [18] the husband of the baptised Hottentot woman, having twice asked for leave in vain himself, had at last obtained it through Baas Teunis's intercession, though only for a time of 3 weeks, and had arrived 3 hours before the baptism. Driven by a strong inward urge, they had made all possible haste to get home, and on his arrival he was exceedingly glad to hear that his wife was to be baptised, and even declared a great longing to share in this grace himself.

To this came another remarkable circumstance. Some days ago we asked the sister of the baptised Hottentot woman, who is also a candidate for baptism, whether she knew where the children of those whom Brother Schmidt had baptised were now living, and whether they were still alive. She answered that she herself, together with the baptised Hottentot woman and two other candidates for baptism, were children of a Hottentot baptised by Brother Schmidt with the name Jonas, whose name had formerly been Cupido [19]. It truly deserves to be noted with wonder that these 4 sisters, children of a Hottentot baptised by Brother Schmidt, have become the first candidates for baptism without our having had any knowledge of it.

<!-- lines 2029-2070 -->
On 20 July 3 more Hottentots came from the Cape, who had left there together with the 3 who arrived yesterday, but had made less haste. They told us that Dominee van Lier [20] (NB: in this country the schoolmasters are called Dominees, and the teachers [of the church] Predikanten) had sought out the Hottentots who belong to us, and when he had found them and taken them on their own, had handed out ABC books to them (which they had to leave behind when they left), and had visited them every Saturday to tell them something about Jesus, without letting himself be held back by the great distance from the Cape, their post being at least an hour's walk from the Cape. Mrs Teunis had brought us apple, pear, peach, fig and lemon trees yesterday, which we were busy planting today [21].

On 21 July the last of the Hottentots who had been given leave came. He complained with great remorse that the farmer Gildenhuyser had given him so much wine to drink that he had become drunk on it and had had to stay lying on the way. It is a lamentable thing in this country that the farmers try to make the Hottentots drunk with wine, so as to practise shameful things with them. With our Hottentots this happens very seldom, and when it does happen they come and lament it with remorse, and we admonish them as much as we can to let themselves be set free from this evil thing by God's grace and help. Going out of the door this evening we heard a candidate for baptism praying and pleading on her knees; we heard the very same from the Hottentot who serves us, and from another young man of 14 years.

<!-- lines 2071-2099 -->
On 23 July we began to hold school again for the Hottentots who had come back from the Cape, during their short stay.

On 24 July we had the first meeting [with the baptised Anna Maria, which we had] old Lena attend [22], since she does not fit well into the meeting of the candidates for baptism and does not like to miss a meeting. We told these two baptised women what we now expected, and could expect, of them, and admonished them to deal openly with us and to say how things were with them inwardly, since we were very ready and willing to share joy and sorrow with them: to thank the Saviour with them when things went well with them, and in the opposite case to call on the Saviour more intimately to make the feeling of his suffering and death truly alive in their hearts. Old Lena was especially admonished to deal with us just as frankly and sincerely as she had done with Brother Schmidt, having poured out into his bosom both what was pleasant and gladdening and what was sad and oppressive.

<!-- lines 2100-2136 -->
On 25 July we had 7 oak trees fetched from Mrs Grunewald [23], who lives 2 hours from here, to set 4 of them in front of our house and 3 behind it, partly to protect our house from the attacks of a violent wind, partly to use them as a shade against the burning heat of the sun, as is commonly done in this country.

On 26 July we began to fence in a piece of land meant for a burial ground. We mean to use for it some young oak trees that have sprung up from the acorns of three old oak trees standing at the end, in front of our garden, and so to close in our burial ground.

On 27 July our candidates for baptism were again the special subject of our conference, when we asked the Saviour whether it might please him to let the grace of holy baptism come to some of our candidates for baptism on 13 August [24]. The Saviour indicated 3 to us for it, namely the sister of the baptised Anna Maria, and also a mother with her daughter.

At the first baptism two Bastard Hottentots who were present had declared roundly to the other Hottentots that they would never do what the baptised Anna Maria had done, namely fall on her knees; and they kept their word and stayed standing, although all the others who were present knelt.

<!-- lines 2136-2184 -->
We did not judge it fruitful to speak to these two Hottentots about it; but in the sermon on 28 July, being Sunday, which was heard by a numerous crowd with great emotion, we took the opportunity to speak of how we poor human beings had abundant reason to bow down in the dust before God the Almighty, and that we could not thank our dear Saviour Jesus Christ enough on our knees for his becoming man, his suffering and dying. The discontent of the two Bastard Hottentots mentioned seems to have arisen from their imagining themselves better than the other Hottentots and having thought that they would be the first to be baptised; now they are envious that this has happened to others sooner than to them. We hope, however, that they will not run away from the Saviour. They have ability and understanding, even more than many a Christian; but that they must come to Jesus as poor sinners and plead with him, that they cannot understand, being rich in good works and in kindnesses shown to others. One of them told us not long after our arrival here that we needed someone to keep an eye on the Hottentots who came to us and report to us how they behaved, and offered himself for it.

Today we heard something about our most recently baptised Anna Maria that seemed remarkable to us. Her sister told us that this Anna Maria, before she knew that she would be baptised, had dreamed that she saw a white sheet lying in front of our house, with a person lying on it on her face, worshipping; which agreed exactly with what happened to her herself at the baptism, without her having heard anything of it beforehand.

<!-- lines 2185-2228 -->
On 29 July Brother Marsveld went with a couple of Hottentots into a wood an hour's walk from here to cut wood, of which we were beginning to run short. We had worked so diligently that a cart could be loaded 3 times with cut wood, which we had brought home on 30 July. One is astonished, and it seems almost impossible, that one can drive up and down the mountains that have to be crossed with a cart or wagon. On foot the Hottentots are especially quick at climbing the mountains and finding the way. Going with them and asking them, "Is there a road there?", they answer: "Yes, a footpath." Coming there, a European hardly sees a trace of a road, and the Hottentot says: "That road is very good." They think just as differently about the distance from one place to another. Where one has to walk two hours to get there, they call that close by, and by this we have been led up the garden path a few times. But this way of thinking serves them well, since several of their kraals lie an hour away from our meeting place, which they consider near.

On 31 July we finished the hedge round our garden, which has cost us much trouble. For it we used small stumps of two kinds of trees, which we stick into the ground, and whose leaves, when they have shot out, will help to make the hedge thicker, so as to keep the cattle from breaking through into the garden; of which we had an unpleasant experience on 1 August.

1. Line 1573 is missing. The transcription runs *dat zÿ, eenigen tÿd geleeden, zich* / *eenige schade overgekomen was? antwoordde zÿ van, Neen*. The German diary has "for some time she had been so sad. Question: whether some harm had come to her? She said no". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
2. *Gildenhuÿsen*, later *Gildenhuÿser*: Wouter Gildenheuser in the German diary. See the glossary.
3. *Cafferen*, *Caffers*: the diarists' words for Xhosa people. See the glossary. The report concerns the fighting on the eastern frontier in 1793.
4. *reeds Ledier tÿd Narigt*: perhaps *[tot] leedwezen*, "to [our] sorrow"; the German column has "already long since". To be checked against the image.
5. The transcription reads *naar Swellenbosch tot den Landdrost*. The letter was addressed to the Landdrost at Swellendam (lines 1605 to 1606 and 1648), so *Swellenbosch* is probably a slip or misreading for Swellendam.
6. Line 1743 is missing. The transcription runs *van Natuur met eene ſchoone* / *worden zo voor als na de ſchoole gezongen*. The German diary has "for they have fine voices. These verses are sung both before and after school". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
7. *Callabaſch*: the German diary names the instrument *Pottebaß*. It is probably the ramkie; see *pottebas* in the glossary.
8. Line 1828 is missing. The transcription runs *die altoos den* / *bÿ zeer vrolÿk*; the German diary has "it always sounds the same, and that is their whole music, and they are very merry with it".
9. *Den Heer Heuser*: see Heuser in the glossary.
10. *Du Mortier*: Charles François Dumouriez, commander of the French army that invaded the Dutch Republic in February 1793.
11. *Peet*: godmother. See *Pathe* in the glossary.
12. After Psalm 118:24.
13. The transcription reads *Eſra 6, 23*; the verse quoted is Ezra 6:22, the last verse of the chapter. The two texts are those of the Moravian Daily Texts for the day, each followed by a hymn verse, translated here without rhyme.
14. *zonder hunnen komen*: perhaps *[on]genoodigd zouden komen*, "come uninvited"; the German column has "without their coming". The sense is plain from what follows.
15. See Anna Maria in the glossary: called Hanna before her baptism on 19 July 1793, the first person baptised at the renewed mission.
16. *welker getal ſtaat kennis zeide in de 80 geweest te zÿn*: the words *ſtaat kennis* are unclear; perhaps *[iemand van] kennis*, "someone who knew". To be checked against the image.
17. *Kraalen en Paerlen*: *kralen*, glass beads, and *paerlen*, pearls or beads. *Kraalen* here is the Dutch word for beads, not the kraal (settlement).
18. Line 1995 is missing. The transcription runs *die naakt en bloot voor haar aan het Kruis* / *de Man van de gedoopte Hottentottin*. The words in square brackets are supplied from the sense; the German diary has "This too was very remarkable to us: her husband was at the Cape".
19. See Cupido in the glossary.
20. *Dominé van Lier*. Helperus Ritzema van Lier, the Cape minister who befriended the mission, died on 21 March 1793 (see the glossary), before the Khoekhoe men were called to the Cape in May. Since the diarists add that schoolmasters are called Dominees at the Cape, this may be a schoolmaster of the same name, or a misreading. The German diary gives the name as unreadable (A363595 entry 10, note 11). To be checked against the image.
21. The Dutch lists five kinds of tree: *Appel, Peeren, Persiken Vÿgen en Citroen boomen*. The German diary has peach and lemon twice (A363595 entry 10, note 14); the Dutch suggests that the German list should read apple, pear, peach, fig and lemon.
22. Line 2079 is missing. The transcription runs *hadden wÿ de eerste bÿeenkomste* / *de oude Lena deeden bÿwoonen*. The German diary has "the first meeting with the baptised Anna Maria. We had ... resolved to take Lena ... into it". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
23. *Vrouw Grunewaldin*: see Stoffel Grunewald in the glossary, and note 9 of entry 03.
24. 13 August, the Moravian festival commemorating the communion at Berthelsdorf in 1727.
