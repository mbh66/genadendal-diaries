Diary of the 3 Brothers Marsveld, Schwinn and Kühnel at the Cape, from 1 to 28 February 1795.

<!-- lines 8299-8305 -->
Early on 1 February the farmer Harme came with his wife, who is a Hottentot woman; they live together as man and wife. There were 2 more Christians with them. They came to attend the meeting, in which Brother Marsveld spoke on the Gospel. In the afternoon they rode away again.

<!-- lines 8306-8343 -->
On the 2nd one of the candidates for baptism came and said that he had sold a cow and 2 calves; now he still had 3 oxen, and if he could not keep them here, he would sell them too; for he could not move away from here and leave God's word. Baas Teunis has again sent word that they should see to it that they got away from here. Two have already made a beginning and taken their cattle away. But it is a pity that they did not live together; otherwise a few could go along and look after the cattle there; but since they live scattered here and there, this cannot go well. Baas Teunis says [that if they want to come to the meeting on Sundays] [1] they can do it, except that they will have it a little further; how is it then for the Christians, who have three days' journey on horseback, and even further, to the Cape? That is true; but if one then inquires how often they come to church, it happens only, first, when they are baptised; afterwards, when they themselves have children baptised, and with that they even wait until several can be baptised together, because then it is more worth the trouble to go to church. There are many Hottentots here who have 30 miles and more to the place where they were settled [2]. The farmers grudge the Hottentots having God's word so near. The moving away from here goes very slowly, because, as they say, they will not leave God's word. So we must wait and see how it will go with this.

<!-- lines 8343-8378 -->
On the 3rd. Today and in these days several came to speak with us because of the perplexity of their hearts, and declared their great longing to be saved. On the 4th the speaking was held with the baptised, and we could rejoice over the greater part of them. Some were very distracted in their thoughts, partly because of the moving away from here, and partly because of the bad conduct of their husbands. In the communicants' meeting the 3 confirmands, Jacob, Eva and Rahel, were blessed for it with the laying on of hands, after they had once more declared their mind to us beforehand, and afterwards in the meeting, to give themselves to Jesus Christ with soul and body and to want nothing more in this world than what their Redeemer and Saviour desires of them; and the presence of God was powerfully to be felt. On the 5th several came with tearful eyes to lament their lost state, and asked whether there was still grace to be found for them too with Jesus Christ; for they had committed all the sins that a person can commit, and were not worthy to live on the earth. They were directed to the dear Saviour, and told that they should fall down before him, who thrusts away none who acknowledge themselves from the heart as sinners and pray for grace at his feet.

<!-- lines 8378-8414 -->
On the 6th a woman came weeping very bitterly, and said that she must now move away again with her 5 children to the Bok River, 7 hours from here [3], where she had lived before, to take her sheep back there and herd them there. The children had all gone to school. She said that she had had no wish to move here, but Baas Teunis had given her no peace all the same (she lived 2 hours from here), and had always said that she could see now that everyone was going to the school; did she not have a wish to be converted too? Or did she want to be left alone with her children? Since he gave her no peace, she finally made the decision to move here too. Here she has now heard and found something different from what she had thought, and was very glad that she was here. The dear Saviour has not left himself without witness in her heart either; and now Baas Teunis has sent her word, and even told her, that she could not stay here with her sheep but must move back to where she came from. She thought that this would pass over again, because she had been given no peace then until she had moved here, and she did not want to obey this order at once. She said: "It is as if he were now wholly possessed by the devil. I must go away from here; but because I cannot manage alone, I must take my children with me; but as soon as I can, I will send some of them back here."

<!-- lines 8415-8440 -->
On the 7th in the evening we held Holy Communion, with the dear Saviour owning us poor ones; and at it we had partakers for the first time [4], after we had asked them once more beforehand about their mind, and they had declared that it was their mind to want to live for the dear Saviour alone in this world. On Sunday the 8th Brother Marsveld spoke on the Gospel. Our Susanna also came and said that she had no rest; she had to come and declare her thankfulness to us for the grace she had received yesterday. She said: "For joy and thankfulness I do not know what I shall do; oh, I cannot thank the dear Saviour enough! For some time I have felt especially well; this night I could do nothing but weep and give thanks, and so it is this day too." She is a poor widow; her husband, when he was on guard at the Cape, fell from a rock and was killed [5]. She has 2 children.

<!-- lines 8436-8457 -->
On the 9th. For some time the heat has been so great that it was almost unbearable for the Hottentots; we too did not know where to put ourselves for it, by day or by night [6]. Yesterday it began to rain, which gave some coolness and refreshed us greatly; but today it began to grow very hot again. On the 10th the candidate for baptism who was lately excluded came, and asked with many tears that we would forgive her her bad doings, for she saw herself as shut out of the kingdom of heaven. She said: "I pray the dear Saviour day and night for forgiveness." She has been with us several times already and asked us to forgive her. The testimony of others about her conduct is good. We forgave her, for which she was very thankful. A man who was excluded also came, and asked to be allowed to go to the little meeting again; but his conduct was not such that we could allow it.

<!-- lines 8457-8502 -->
On the 11th a servant came of Mr Clüde, to whom Constantia belongs; he lives on one of his places, 4 days' journey from here. He was looking for a Hottentot who had run away from him with his wife and 4 children, but had not come to us. The said Mr Clüde is a German by birth, but has already been 18 years here in the country [7]. On the 12th, when we were having our house made tight where the rain had washed it away, the Hottentots were digging clay not far from our house, and found a nest with 11 snake's eggs. When we opened them, there were already young ones inside, which were all alive and 8 inches long. The eggs were as white as birds' eggs, and one could well have taken them for such, but the shells were not as hard as those of birds. The nest was a good half ell deep in the earth, in a mouse's hole. On the 13th another of our Hottentots took his cattle from here to the Bok River; when he arrived there, Dimeni's servant drove him away again with his cattle. The Hottentot went at once to Baas Teunis, who sent him back again and told him that he must stay there. This Dimeni got a place (which always belonged to the Hottentots) last year, [on condition that he would not drive the Hot]tentots away from there [8]. He is still busy building his house and does not even live there yet; and yet he chases the poor people away. That is how it goes with the poor Hottentots. On the 14th Brother Kühnel was called to a sick man, who asked him to tell him some words about God, because he had not yet been able to come to the meeting and hear something there of our dear Lord. This man was brought here sick; he is the one of whom we have already made mention, and who believes of himself that he is bewitched [9]. Baas Teunis has also sent word to our Hanna that she must take hers [10] away.

<!-- lines 8502-8548 -->
On the 15th, as the entry into the Passion season, Brother Marsveld spoke in the meeting on the Gospel, Luke 18, verse 31 to the end. On the 16th most of the Hottentots who had gone out to dry peaches came home again. They have not been able to dry many, because it has rained so often in this time, and many have also rotted [11]. On the 17th our Sara, who had been at a farmer's yesterday, came home full of fear and sorrow. The farmer told her that the Landdrost of Stellenbosch would come to us in these days and bring a written order from the Edle Heer that we must leave our place within 3 days, and go so far that no Christians live there; and if we did not comply with it, we would be sent to Batavia. We had much comforting to do with her and several others. They said: "If only our teachers can stay in peace, they may do with us what they like; we are not going away from here anyway." We had much comforting to do, and to direct them to our dear Saviour: without his will nothing can happen, and what he allows, and what seems hard to us, he also gives us the strength to bear, if we ask him for it; and in the end we shall still bring him praise and thanks for it. We have much comforting to do, but we ourselves need comfort too, since the rumours and prospects grow ever sadder; and if it were allowed to the enemy of God, all the Hottentots, and we with them, would have to leave here. Today Baas Teunis sent word to us as well as to the Hottentots that they should see to it that they got away from here; he would not like to do them the disgrace of having them taken away by field corporals. He also added that they might not keep any cattle for slaughter here, but everything must go away; they could slaughter it where it grazes and bring the meat here [12].

<!-- lines 8549-8599 -->
Early on the 18th, at 9 o'clock, we saw a carriage drawn by 8 horses, and 2 riders on horseback, coming towards us across the Sonderend River. When we saw this, we did not feel very easy, for we could see that it was no farmer. When they had come to our river, the carriage stopped, and one of the gentlemen got out, and those on horseback dismounted too, and came towards us on foot. We thought: that is no sign of friendship. Afterwards they said that this was done only because the sickness was still among the Hottentots, and so that the gentlemen who are with the wagon should not be infected by it [13]. When they came nearer we went out to meet them and welcome them. They seemed very friendly at once, and one of the gentlemen asked whether we still knew him. Answer: "Yes. You are Mr Clüde" (namely the Mr Clüde to whom Constantia be[longs, who had sent for] us when we had arrived at the Cape, on the recommendation of Mr Schwellengräbel in Holland, who lives at Doorn, 2 hours from Zeist) [14]. He said yes. The other was a gentleman from Stellenbosch named Hoffman, who had been a privy councillor. The third, named Andreas Otto, is a field corporal; he lives about 3 miles from here. Mr Clüde now lives at Stellenbosch too. He brought us greetings from Mr Schwellengräbel, and said that this gentleman had complained to him that we did not write to him at all, and for that reason he had put several questions to him, which he was to answer. He asked us to tell him: 1) How many Hottentots had been here when we came here? 2) How many had moved here since? 3) How many had died of sicknesses? 4) How many went to school? But we could not answer all these questions exactly; the next one, though, all the more precisely: 5) How many oxen, cows, sheep, goats and how many horses did we have? Answer: "We have 5 oxen, 5 cows, 2 calves, 100 goats, 1 horse and 2 sheep" [15]. It made him laugh that we had so little of everything. At this he said: "I have heard that the Hottentots are to take their cattle away from here; what is the reason for it?"

<!-- lines 8600-8640 -->
We told him that we knew nothing except that Baas Teunis says that the place is too small and the cattle must die of hunger. He said that this could not be, for in this stretch alone, which runs from our house to the river, a good 100 oxen could stand, without counting the other cattle [16]. The Edle Heer had not been rightly told the truth. Otto also said that he could not imagine what kind of Christians these were who wanted to keep these people from God's word and drive them away from here. It was a loss to all the burghers if the Hottentots had to go away from here; for now one could still get people for it when one has work, but if these were gone, where would one get any from? But Baas Teunis was to blame for it all, with his avarice [17]. Most of the poor Hottentots gathered in front of our house, in the hope that these gentlemen might perhaps help them; for Mr Hoffman had already spoken with the Hottentots who were at the Cape to hand in a petition to the Edle Heeren, and told them that he would come himself and look at the place and the cattle. He went out in front of the house and spoke with the Hottentots. Now they told him that Baas Teunis had impounded some of their cattle, and they had had to pay 1 shilling for each head. Mr Hoffman spoke with Mr Clüde about it, and both stood very amazed, and said that they would like to know all their names. We had them come one after another, and Mr Clüde wrote down all the names; 50 came in all whose cattle had been impounded. At 10 o'clock, after a friendly leave-taking, they rode from us to Baas Teunis's place, and took a Hottentot with them as guide.

<!-- lines 8641-8669 -->
They looked at this place very carefully and asked about everything, because it belonged to the Hottentots, who have now been driven from it entirely. In front of our house Mr Hoffman asked the Hottentots why they had moved here. Answer: "To hear and learn God's word." At this he said: "That is good, if you do this. You must not think that these people [have been put here by the government or by other men to teach you] [18]; they are not from men, but sent by God himself; and you must follow everything they tell you, and if you do this, you will become blessed people." They said this also to several who met them on the way. Mr Clüde said that he had known this place very well since the year 1738, when he had been with Georg Schmidt. (He went to school, that is, with Brother Schmidt.) [19] We believe that the visit of these gentlemen had some well-founded cause, for they inquired very carefully about everything. But that Mr Schwellengräbel should have put these questions to him for him to answer, we cannot believe. God let it turn out for the best for his cause.

<!-- lines 8669-8711 -->
On the 19th Brother Marsveld was called to our sister Lena [20]; she was ill. He found her in a contented state of heart, and expecting that the dear Saviour would soon take her to himself. She said: "I have no pain, but I am very weak, so that I cannot walk." On the 20th a Hottentot came who had been with a farmer about 15 miles from here, with a discharge saying that he had served out his time honestly and faithfully and was now free. The farmer with whom he served was a smith, and the Hottentot learned this trade from him. When he wanted to leave, the farmer would not let him go. At this the Hottentot said to him: "If Baas will stand good for my soul, that it shall not be lost, then I will stay with Baas all my life; but for that Baas must be my surety, and Baas will have to answer for it too if he does not let me go." (This Hottentot had been here before, to stay here; but we told him then that he must bring a discharge from the farmer that he was free.) At the Hottentot's answer given above the farmer stood speechless, and said: "I cannot answer for my own soul, much less for others'"; then he gave him a discharge and let him go. On the 21st Brother Schwinn was called to the man mentioned more than once, who believed he was bewitched. He told him that when he was serving a farmer he had shot dead a Hottentot Bastard; had he perhaps got his illness from that, and had he done wrong in it? He had first wounded him, and afterwards, at the farmer's word, shot him dead. That is how it goes here: when a Hottentot is shot dead, no one asks about it. On the 22nd a carpenter came who had not long been free of the Company, and now lives with a farmer 2 hours from here. He is a German.

<!-- lines 8711-8724 -->
Our Hanna also came again, who had been at Baas Teunis's and asked him to let her stay here with her oxen and cows. But she got the answer that she could not keep her cattle here, but must take it all away. She said: "I cannot go away, and I am not going away, let it go as it will." So say many more. We cannot know what will yet come of it, for they will not bring themselves to move away from here, although the order for it is very sharp.

<!-- lines 8724-8746 -->
On the 23rd our sister Rahel gave birth to a little son. On the 24th his baptism took place in the meeting. Beforehand we spoke with her about there having to be baptismal witnesses too, and for these she named Brother Schwinn, Brother Jacob and Sister Sara. Brother Marsveld performed the baptism, and Brother Kühnel held the child at it [; it received the name Daniel] [21]. We had the godparents come beforehand, and told them how great and weighty a thing it is to stand as witnesses at a baptism. They had not only to commend this child to the dear Saviour in prayer at holy baptism; it must also be their concern that it should be brought up for him, as if it were their own. Afterwards several of our sisters came and said that they could not thank the dear Saviour enough that he had given them the great grace of seeing a child baptised into his death.

<!-- lines 8746-8767 -->
On the 25th [22]. The basin [23] by our house had so far been dug out only a little. Early today, when the children's meeting was over, we had the children carry stones to set it round with, and taking some Hottentots to work, we had the basin dug out. Setting the stones, which we did ourselves, was heavy work for us because of the heat of that day. On the 26th several Hottentots came who had lived on the Breede River, about 20 miles from here, and who are now to take their cattle away from here too. They had been to Baas Teunis together, asking that they might keep them here after all; but they got the answer that they should not leave a single head of it here; they could slaughter it at their places and bring the meat here. Some of them said that they would not go away from here, and one woman said: "Even if my husband goes away from here, I will not go with him; I will stay here."

<!-- lines 8767-8804 -->
On the 27th we began to make a lean-to roof against our little house, to put our calabashes [24] under it and keep them from the rain. These calabashes are good food for the Hottentots. We also have a great many of them, which will come in very handy for the Hottentots whom we have in our service, especially in the rainy season; for of the garden produce (except beans) we have very little, and so in the rainy season we must keep to the beans [25]. For want of room we are often in great perplexity and at our wits' end. We could build ourselves something at little cost, if it had not been forbidden us. Now the dear brothers and sisters can easily imagine: in the house we have nothing but a little chamber where we sleep; the front room and the kitchen we cannot use, because of the school and the meetings that are held in them. Our loft is so poor that we can store nothing on it but light things [26]; and yet we must provide ourselves sufficiently with food, especially in the rainy season, because we cannot get across the river. Where, for example, shall we get corn and flour from? If we want to go to the mill, we must cross the river. Here someone might well ask: How did you manage it last year? Answer: Then Baas Teunis was still our friend, and he helped us; but how he is disposed towards us now, our dear brothers and sisters will be able to see well enough from the diary.

<!-- lines 8805-8839 -->
Our longing also grows greater daily to get a place where we can hold meetings and schools. We did once wish to have a proper, roomy meeting house; but now we would only wish to be allowed to build a hut, like the Hottentots, to be sheltered from the sun as well as from rain and wind, and would thank God for it from our hearts. [Until now we have held the schools mostly] in the garden under the pear tree [27]; but time and the weather will soon forbid this. When one of us holds school, whether the children's in the morning or the women's in the afternoon, it is always so full that not an apple could fall to the ground, and neither of the two other brothers can get into or out of the chamber, since necessity requires one to go through the front room. When we hold a meeting we set the door and window open, and let as many Hottentots go into the kitchen as it can hold, and still there is far from room enough for all. But what shall we do when the strong wind and rain set in? The dear Saviour, who sees everything, is our only help, and we pray to him for it daily. Today 4 more women and 3 children came who wanted to stay here. Since until now we had read the 4 Evangelists to the end in our meetings, today we began the Acts of the Apostles, and Brother Schwinn read the first chapter of it.

<!-- lines 8839-8865 -->
On the 28th. Today being 2 years since we moved into our house, in our evening meeting we brought our dear Lord and Saviour our most heartfelt thanks in a prayer on our knees, for his gracious protection and help through these 2 years, and also for the blessing which he has visibly laid on the stammering of his 3 poor brothers [28]; and we asked him for forgiveness where we had not done, thought and acted according to the mind of his heart, and that he would go on with his mercy and help us through in the time to come, and give us all that we shall need as faithful servants [29]. For some time the Spirit of God has been especially at work in the hearts of the Hottentots, chiefly among the women. Hardly a day passes, indeed on many a day very many come, mostly new people, who want to speak with us one by one about the unrest of their hearts, and say that they have no rest day or night, cannot sleep, and pray that they may obtain forgiveness of their sins.

<!-- lines 8866-8889 -->
With this we close our diary for this month again, wishing that you may soon receive it. It is, dear brothers and sisters, almost as if we were quite cut off from you; and not only from you, but from the Cape too. We have various things at the Cape which Brother Marsveld ordered and arranged there to be sent to us at the first opportunity; and although it is already 2 months since, we still do not have them, and do not know the reason either, where the fault lies, unless it be that no farmer will take them along [30]; nor have we received a letter from there since that time. Now, we are all 3 still well and in health, and live in peace and love with one another, and commend ourselves to the prayers and remembrance of all our dear brothers and sisters in the congregations.

Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn, Joh. Chr. Kühnel

## Notes

1. Line 8322 is missing. The transcription runs *Baas Teunis zegt:* / *men, dan kunnen zy het doen*. The German diary has "Baas Teunis says that if they want to come to the meeting on Sundays they can, only it will be a little further". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. The Dutch *hoe hebben het dan de Chriſtenen, welke drie Dagreiſen te paard, en wel nog verder naar de Kaap hebben*, "how is it then for the Christians", settles the German diary's garbled reading here (its note 1).
2. *tot aan die plaats waar Zy geruſt Zyn*, "to the place where they were at rest"; the German diary has "to the places where they were before".
3. The German diary has "with her 3 children, to the Bok River (2 hours from here)", and says in the next sentence, as the Dutch does, that she lived 2 hours away. The German diary has a page number 7 at this point (its note 4); the Dutch "7 hours" probably comes from that number, which suggests that the Dutch was translated from the German manuscript.
4. *waarby wy Eerſtmaalige Deelgenooten hadden*, "at which we had first-time partakers". The German diary has "3 truly blessed fellow partakers", probably a misreading. The Dutch does not have the German diary's note that in the conference that day Susanna was assigned as a candidate for Communion and one as a candidate for baptism; Susanna's words on the 8th show that she was one of the new partakers.
5. The number 24 at the start of line 8432 is a page number and is left out.
6. *wy wiſten ons ook zowel by den Dag als ook by den Nacht geen verblyf voor dezelve*; this settles the German diary's garbled reading (its note 5).
7. *Genoemde Heer Clude is een Duitſcher van Geboorte, doch reeds 18 Jaaren hier in 't land*. The German diary says this of the servant, which is more likely: Mr Clüde had known the place since 1738 (18th).
8. Line 8486 is missing. The transcription runs *verleden Jaar* / *totten daar niet te verdryven*. The German diary has "only last year, on condition that he would not drive the Hottentots away". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. The Dutch *verleden Jaar* settles the German diary's *erſt weniges Jahr* (its note 8).
9. *die van zich gelooft, dat hy betooverd is*, "bewitched". This settles the German diary's readings "possessed" and "converted" (its notes 9 and 21).
10. *dat zy haa moeſt wegbrengen*: probably *haar Vee*, "her cattle", as in the German diary.
11. *welke om Perſiken te droogen uitgegaan waren*, "who had gone out to dry peaches", and *er oo veele verrot zyn*, "many have also rotted". The German diary has "who had gone to drive plough oxen" and "many are hateful to them" (its note 10); the Dutch readings are the right ones. Peach drying is mentioned in January 1795 (entry 19, note 24).
12. The German diary goes on with a passage on the impossibility of this demand for people living 30 miles away, since meat spoils in a day and the Khoekhoe have no salt, and a wish that the brethren could speak openly with someone; the Dutch does not have it.
13. *om dat de Heeren dewelke by den Wagen zyn, daarvan niet mogten aangeſtoken worden*, "the gentlemen who are with the wagon". The German diary has "the slaves who were with the wagon", which fits better.
14. Line 8568 is missing. The transcription runs *welken Conſtantia to* / *ons, toen wy op de Kaap waren aangekomen*. The German diary has "to whom Constantia belongs ... He had sent for us when we arrived at the Cape". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. *Schwellengrbel*, probably *Schwellengräbel*; the German diary has Schwellengräver. See Swellengrebel and Hendrik Cloete in the glossary.
15. The German diary has "3 oxen, 3 cows". To be checked against the image.
16. *alleen in deze Streek, die van ons huis in de Rivier gaat, zouden wel 100 Oſſen ſtaan kunnen, zonder het andere Vee gerekent*. The German diary has "1000 sheep could live" in the field "from your house to the Sonderend River, without the other land".
17. *doch was Baas Teunis aan allen de Schuld met zyne Gierigheid*, "avarice". This settles the German diary's *Opritz* (its note 18).
18. Line 8650 is missing. The transcription runs *dat deeze lieden van de Regeering of van andere* / *zy zyn niet van Menſchen*. The German diary has "that these people are put here by the government or by other men to teach you; they are not from men". The words in square brackets are supplied from there.
19. The Dutch states as a fact what the German diary reports as hearsay ("he is said to have gone to school with Brother Schmidt"). See Hendrik Cloete in the glossary and the German diary's note 19.
20. Probably old Lena, baptised by Georg Schmidt (see the German diary's note 20).

The German diary has "Brother Kühnel held the child at it; he received the name Daniel". The words in square brackets are supplied from there. *De peters*, "the godparents"; the Dutch has *te vooren*, "beforehand", where the German diary has "afterwards".

The Dutch "in the garden under the pear tree" is probably the right reading, and settles the German diary's note 26.
28. *voor dien Zegen, welke Hy op het Stammelen Zyner 3 arme Broederen oogenſchynlyk gelegd heeft*. This settles the German diary's *das Wörtlein Seines Namens Bruder* (its note 28): the German probably read *das Stammeln Seiner 3 armen Brüder*.
29. The German diary begins the 28th with "in the conference the dear Saviour approved four as candidates for baptism"; the Dutch does not have it.
30. *het zy dan, dat het geen Boer wil mede neemen*. The German diary has "and whether his brother means to bring them" (its note 29), probably a misreading.
