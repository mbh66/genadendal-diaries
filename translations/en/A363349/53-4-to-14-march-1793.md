On 4 March, in the morning, 25 old people came to school. Some others came to tell us that they could not come to school, because they had to go and look for food to live on. In general the Hottentots had believed that they would get their food from us; but we told them plainly that they [had nothing of the kind to expect from us, but that we also] would ask nothing of them for our trouble [1]. About midday Brother Marsveld rode to Baas Teunis's, to go with him to the town tomorrow. We commended him to God's guidance and protection.

<!-- lines 14805-14859 -->
On 5 March 27 came to school. A Hottentot whose child, only 2 days old, had died came to tell us so [2]. We asked him to let us know also when it was to be buried, because we would like to attend this burial. He did so towards evening, and we went there. When we arrived, all the Hottentots who live in the surrounding kraals or houses were standing assembled in front of the kraal of the dead child. The women sat beside it smoking a pipe of tobacco. Then the one who was to carry the child went into the house of the dead and brought the child out; it had been wrapped in a sheepskin and then tied up, so that nothing of it could be seen. He went with it to the burial place, which lay about 200 paces from the kraal. Another Hottentot walked before him, and behind him followed the father. When we asked what the one who walked before the body stood for, the answer was: "He stands for the precentor" [3]. The father was followed by several Hottentots carrying spades on their backs. The grave was more than two ells deep, and bigger than for a grown person. Then two Hottentots went into the grave to place the child in a hollow that had been dug in one side of the grave [4]. When this was done, they took stakes and fixed them in front of this hollow. Then they laid brushwood between these stakes, and that so close that the earth which was laid in front of it, to cover the stakes and brushwood, could not get through. To the question what this was for, the answer was that it was even better than burial in a coffin, for a coffin soon presses the earth down and makes it sink, whereas in their manner of doing it this could not so easily happen. After all this, each of those present threw a couple of spadefuls of earth into the grave; and when that was done, all hands were busy filling up the grave with earth to a great height, after which a certain plant was strewn on it, which looks very like a weed and is also taken for a weed here, and quickly sprouts, and bears a fruit that is called figs here and also tastes of figs [5]. When this fruit becomes ripe, the Hottentots pick it and are content to keep themselves alive with it. Last of all they lay stones on the grave, covered and heaped up with earth, so that the wolf may not come and dig up the earth [6]; and then they all go home again. When they had come home, a small wooden trough with water had been set outside in front of the house, which they use to wash their hands in [7]. The women had not gone with them to the burial, but had stayed sitting by the house of the dead, smoking their pipe of tobacco with relish and watching the procession from a distance. Meanwhile the father of the dead child had bought a goat and had it slaughtered to be eaten [8]. I forgot to mention that when we went away from the grave, now quite set in order, the Hottentot who represented the precentor thanked those who had followed the body for the love shown to it. We received special thanks, and were asked whether the child had been well buried. Close behind our house there are many burial places of this kind.

<!-- lines 14860-14881 -->
On 6 March our pupils came punctually to school again. Brother Kühnel had got a boil on his right knee, which was very painful and kept him from going out of the house. This came at a very bad time [9], since we are busy: we must see to getting our garden into such order that it can be sown, so that we have something to eat; for now is the best time to sow and plant lettuce, turnips and cabbage, which does not succeed well in summer because of the too great heat. To be able to do this, the garden must first be put in order, which costs much trouble and would be too costly to have done by others. In our house, too, there is still much to do. The windows are still without glass, which Brother Marsveld must first bring from the town. The most necessary things cost an astonishing amount of money, and many things one cannot even get made, though one would and could pay three times over for them. Thus there is not one carpenter in our whole neighbourhood, and it would suit us very well if one of us were a carpenter.

<!-- lines 14883-14912 -->
[On the 7th our pupils all came again. It looks] charming to see a number of people whose clothing consists mostly of a sheepskin [10]. Some of the men have breeches on; the rest of their clothing consists of a skin. Of the female sex there are only a few who have any clothes on, and these, by the custom of this country, go in front when they go out, and are followed by the common ones who are not clothed [11]. The worst of it is that the Hottentots have so many fleas and lice in their skins, which are particularly large; and what wonder that we too sometimes get a few of them. In future we shall try to persuade them to rid themselves of this vermin by diligently beating out their skins [12]. Now to say something also of their kraals or huts, which they can build in one day. This is done in the following way. They fetch slender shrubs from the bushes, and stick these into the ground in a round circle, leaving inside a space of about 6 ells from one side to the other, according as the household is more or less numerous [13]. These rods are bound together all round; the greatest height, which is in the middle of these rods, is about 2½ ells. Then the ground below is laid with skins, and the rods are covered from above, and that is the roof of their kraals. On one side an opening is left, which is the door, but it is so small and low that one has to crawl in almost on hands and feet. In front of this opening they hang a skin or a mat, and so they all lie together, man, wife and children. The women are not at all used to working, but lie together and refresh themselves with a pipe of tobacco, which they begin to learn already in their youth.

<!-- lines 14913-14918 -->
On 8 March our pupils gathered punctually again. The old Lena, baptised by Brother Schmidt, comes with them every time; she, as well as the woman mentioned above who can read [14], helps the others on, and in these few days they have already learned to know so many letters that it goes beyond our expectation.

<!-- lines 14920-14958 -->
On 9 March we held no school, as we intend not to hold it on Saturdays and Wednesdays. A Hottentot came to tell us today that he wanted to bring his kraal here, so that he too could learn. On the 10th we two brethren prayed our Church Litany with a deeply felt emotion [15]. Several Hottentots came to tell us that on the other side of the mountains there were many who wanted to come and live with us to learn. Many of them serve the farmers, who will not let them go, and the Hottentots will not stay; this makes much stir in that neighbourhood, and we do not see how it will end. The Hottentots who live with us are also dismayed at it, for they do not see either where they are all to find their living here. We have no insight into it either; but God knows, though it is hidden from our human eye. Yet we cannot keep out those who want to come and live here, or say to them, "You cannot live here", for this would be as much as to say: "You may not come here to learn and to hear the word of God"; since if we refused it, the Hottentots who live on the far side of the mountains and of the river mentioned above could come to us only seldom or not at all, because of the great distance behind the mountains and the flooding of the river in the winter season. We can do nothing in this but call on the Lord to make the way for it himself, since it has pleased him to put into the hearts of these people the desire to hear the Gospel, which makes us hope and believe that the hour for the salvation of the Hottentots has come. Curiosity has also already moved several farmers to visit us, who behaved very kindly towards us and were met by us in the same way. Thus on 7 March a farmer came from a distance of 3 hours' riding on horseback, which is a long way from us, since they ride very fast; and on 9 March another came again (from a good hour away, who was said to be a worthy man) [16]. We had to promise to visit him, and he promised that he would be a good neighbour to us.

<!-- lines 14959-14975 -->
On 11 March our pupils came together again. One finds in the Hottentots a very good and praiseworthy quality, namely that they are very honest, and one can leave everything open and lying about before them without fearing that they will spirit anything away, unless they were driven and tempted by hunger to take some tobacco, food or drink; and even that happens very rarely. But as for money and money's worth, one need not lock it up at all: it lies as safe outside the house as inside. If one loses anything, the one who finds it brings it to the owner at once, of which we have already had some examples [17]. Today a Hottentot came to tell us that he meant to bring his house here, and wanted to make a start on it today [18].

<!-- lines 14976-14985 -->
On the 12th a young Hottentot woman came to us [19] and said that when she had told the farmer she had served for two years that she wanted to go to us to learn, he had beaten her, with the added threat that if she carried out her intention he would soon come and fetch her back again. We told her that if she had done nothing else, she should just stay here in a kraal, and the farmer would probably not come and fetch her. Three others had come with her, wanting to learn too. These 4 women were all properly dressed.

<!-- lines 14986-15008 -->
On the 13th we heard that a Hottentot who comes to our school had beaten two holes in his wife's head [20]. So on the 14th, when all the pupils had come to school, we asked him why he had done it. His answer was: because she would not mend his breeches [21]. We replied that we could not and would not suffer in our midst pupils who had promised us that they wanted to become children of God, and came to school with us to learn for that reason, if they behaved as he had done. He could therefore go wherever he pleased, provided he gave us back his book; and both were done. On this occasion we declared to the others that all who came to us to learn must beware of doing such things, or else we would have nothing to do with them. Today a Hottentot woman came to complain to us that a farmer she served had kept back her child of two months, because she wanted to go to us to learn, which he much disliked. We sent her with a letter to Baas Teunis, who has written to the farmer about it, and today we heard that she has her child back [22].

## Notes

1. Line 14800 is missing. The transcription runs *wy hebben hun duidelyk gezegd, dat zy* / *niets voor onze Moeite van hun zouden begeeren*. The German diary has "we told them that they would get nothing from us, but that we did not want anything from them either"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there.
2. The transcription reads *Een Hottent Hottn Kind maar 2 daagen oud zynde, geſtorven was, kwam ons dit boodSchappen*, with the Hottentot written twice and a word such as *wiens*, "whose", missing. The German diary tells more: the father had come when the child was born to say that it was ill.
3. *Voorzanger*: the precentor, who leads the singing in a Dutch Reformed church. The German diary has "cantor" here and "clerk" below; the Dutch has *Voorzanger* both times.
4. This settles the German *mit ſohlen ins Grab* (A363595, entry 04, note 6): two of the men went down into the grave to set the child in a hollow dug in its side.
5. *vrugt draagt die hier Vygen genaamd worden*: the Cape succulent called Hottentot fig or sour fig (*Carpobrotus*), as in the German diary.
6. *de Wolf*: the settlers' name for the hyena.
7. *een houten bakje met water*, "a small wooden trough with water". This settles the German *Bat* (A363595, entry 04, note 9), which is the Dutch *bak*.
8. *een Bok*: a goat, or a buck.
9. The transcription reads *'t welk juiſt niet zeer te onpas kwam*, literally "which came just not very inopportunely", which contradicts the sense of the sentence and the German diary ("It is bad that it comes just now"). *niet* is probably out of place. To be checked against the image.
10. Line 14882 is missing. The transcription runs *Timmerman was.* / *aardig uit, een getal Menſchen te zien*. The German diary begins the passage "On the 7th our pupils were all there again. It looks quite charming to see a crowd of people"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there.
11. The transcription reads *als zy uitgaan, in 't geinzen voor aan*; *geinzen* is not a word, and may be a misreading of *gaan*. The sense agrees with the German diary.
12. The Dutch leaves out the German diary's story of the kaross full of lice left in front of the door, and the owner's answer.
13. The German diary has "thin poles from the river".
14. The woman of about 30 who read Matthew 2 at the Sergeants River on 24 December (entry 50). The transcription has *die Leeren kan*, "who can learn", for *leezen*, "read".
15. *ons kerken gebed*: the Church Litany of the Brethren. *wy twee Broeders*: Brothers Schwinn and Kühnel, while Brother Marsveld was at the Cape.
16. The transcription reads *(op denzelfden afſtand van een groot Uur*, "at the same distance of a good hour", which contradicts the 3 hours just given. The German diary has "from a good hour away"; *denzelfden* is probably a slip.
17. The Dutch leaves out the German diary's note that Brother Kühnel had recovered from his bad knee. Line 14964 is missing, between *leggen laaten* and *laten, zonder te vreezen*; the repeated word is a catchword, and the missing line was probably the marker of page 250b, which is not in the transcription. No text seems to be lost.
18. *zyn Huis*, "his house". This settles the German *ſein Pferd*, "his horse" (A363595, entry 04, note 11), which is a misreading of *Haus*.
19. The transcription reads *eene jonge Hottn Vrouw, wier zoon tot ons*, "whose son", which has no sense here: the woman comes alone, and the German diary mentions no son. To be checked against the image.
20. *twee gaten in het Hoofd van zyne Vrouw geſlagen had*. This settles the German *ſeinen Herrn zwingher in den Kopf geſchlagen* (A363595, entry 04, note 12): *ſeinen Herrn* is a misreading of *ſeiner Frau*, and *zwingher* probably of *zwey Löcher*, "two holes".
21. *om dat zy zyne Broeken niet had willen verhelpen*, "because she would not mend his breeches". The German diary has "she would not put up with the teaching for him"; the German transcription is probably a misreading of words about mending (*flicken*) his breeches (*Hoſen*). To be checked against the image of the German manuscript.
22. The transcription adds *en heden een Wagen heeft*, "and today has a wagon", which has no sense here; the copyist's eye probably slipped to the next line, *door eenen Hottn die een Wagen heeft* (line 15009).
