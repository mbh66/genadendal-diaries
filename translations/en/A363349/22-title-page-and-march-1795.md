Cape reports of the month [of March] 1795 [1].

<!-- lines 8900-8902 -->
Diary of the 3 Brothers Marsveld, Schwinn and Kühnel at the Cape, of the month of March 1795.

<!-- lines 8903-8944 -->
On 1 March we were visited by a shoemaker who had once spent 14 days in Germany, namely in the Brethren's congregation at Neudietendorf, but had gone away from there again, which he now showed that he regretted [2]. Afterwards Juffrouw Teunis came with 3 children and her sister. We were much surprised at this visit, because she is so hostile towards us. She attended all the meetings. Afterwards she went to our Sister Martha and began to quarrel violently with her. The cause of it was that when Martha was lately at the post, one of Teunis's slaves said to Martha that at times things were said in their house, terribly, to our harm. Martha told us this again, and Juffrouw Teunis came to know of it; she was so angry about it that she fell on our Martha very harshly, saying among other things that Martha ought to have told it to her (instead of to us); then she would have punished her slave woman for it. Juffrouw Teunis denied everything that she was supposed to have said to our harm, and declared it to be pure lies. It is in general very much this woman's way that what she can say today she will deny entirely tomorrow, and that with the greatest stubbornness. As she said, she had now come here for this purpose alone [3], to shake out her spirit properly for once. As soon as she had finished with this business of hers, she drove back again, and did not even stay to eat with us.

<!-- lines 8945-8968 -->
The shoemaker left too, but in the evening he came back again with his companions. It had namely rained heavily in these days, so that the river they had to cross was so swollen that the oxen had to swim through it. They had taken a Hottentot with them, who swam beside the oxen and guided them; while the company went over a tree that had been laid across the stream, to call another Hottentot to help, the oxen meanwhile got so deep into the mud with the wagon that they stuck fast [4]. If they did not want to let the oxen drown, all the traces had to be cut loose. As soon as this was done, the oxen swam to land; and they had to leave the wagon stuck until they had called more people to help. The next day, the wagon being free, they went on their way.

<!-- lines 8968-9016 -->
That same evening Juffrouw Teunis sent to ask that one of us might come to her. So Brother Kühnel rode off early on the 2nd. This was what had happened: in that night 19 head of cattle belonging to the Hottentots had got into her garden and ruined it entirely, and so she had now had them driv[en into the kraal. When she asked what she should] do about it [5], Brother Kühnel answered that we could do nothing about it, and she must just do what she thought good. While she was speaking about this, 30 more head of the Hottentots' cattle came running towards the garden. "Look!" she said, "there you see it! What am I to do now? There is nobody with them. I will just have them all driven to the post again; then they can wait and see what my husband will do with them." Brother Kühnel said that she must deal with them as she pleased. So she had her slaves drive the cattle to the post. Brother Kühnel assured her that we admonish the Hottentots often enough to take better care of their cattle, but that it was hard to bring these people into good order. "I cannot understand that," she said, "that the Hottentots should not do what you bid them do; for I have often seen and heard myself that when they are told to do this or that, they answer: 'We do not do that any more, for the gentlemen, or our teachers, have said that it was not good, and that we must not do it any more'; or: 'We will first ask our teachers whether it is good.' Now, should they not show you obedience, then, if you bade them take better care of their cattle?"

<!-- lines 9017-9046 -->
On the 6th our Sister Susanna came home again. She had been to the Landdrost at Stellenbosch and complained about the farmer Stoffel Grunewald, with whom her son is, and whom she wants back again. But the farmer will not let him go. His time was already up a year ago; but because he had let the farmer's oxen, which he was herding, run into the corn, by which great damage was done to him, he forced her son to serve another year for it; and now this year is over again too; but since one of the farmer's horses has now died, it is said: "Your boy is to blame for it; if he had watched better, it would not have happened; so he must also make good this damage to me by serving." In this way the farmers seek, by all kinds of empty pretexts, to keep the poor Hottentots in their service all their lives. The Landdrost gave her a letter to take and hand to the farmer.

<!-- lines 9047-9097 -->
On the 8th 2 farmers came to look for Hottentots who were to help him cart manure. On the 9th 2 farmers came, Molman and Grunewald. The first lives 8 and the other 3 miles from here. Molman wanted to speak to a Hottentot to whom he had lent money because he once (so he said) wanted a proper drink [6]; and he had promised the farmer to earn this money, and had indeed begun to do so for some days; but then [he asked leave to go and] visit [his mother], who lay sick [7], and since then had not come back to the farmer. Grunewald, who does not look like much (as if he could not count three), said: "Baas Teunis has issued an ordinance by which every farmer who has a Hottentot in his service is free, if he is not obedient, to go into the kraal after him and beat the Hottentot to life and death." He said further: "I have one here too who owes me something as well; when I have to plough he must come; if he does not, I shall come with a fellow and punish him as he deserves." Now, to this we said that we did not know that the farmers had such freedom. But they stuck to their point and said that it had not been so formerly, but that now it was so in fact. We could do nothing about it but keep silent, because the authorities give the farmers freedom for it; for we must take great care to say nothing against the command of the authorities. You can easily imagine, my dear brothers and sisters, that such circumstances move us deeply. We pray and cry to God for mercy for this poor people.

<!-- lines 9098-9132 -->
On the 10th Brother Schwinn spoke on Mark 16:15. Brother Kühnel held the singing meeting [8] outside in front of the door. On the 11th we heard that the farmers and Hottentots must go under arms to the Cape again, and that things look sad for our country. On the 12th two Hottentots fetched back their cattle, which Juffrouw Teunis had lately had driven away. They had to pay 2 shillings for each head. Baas Teunis sent word to the others that they could fetch theirs back too; but they might not drive them here any more, but to such parts where they would be safe from them. To the first 2, because they belong here, he gave freedom to drive them here; but as for the others, he will take care that they are not driven here any more. On the 13th 2 Hottentots came who live [14] days' journey from here on the [Olifants] River [9]. A farmer had driven them away from there and taken all their cattle. They had indeed been to the Landdrost to complain about it, but the answer was that he could not help them. Now they had been at the Cape with the Fiscal, who had given them a letter to the farmer.

<!-- lines 9133-9182 -->
On the 16th we again had to have a kraal made on the Sergeant's River for our goats and billy goats, because on this side of the Sonderend River it is unhealthy, and many cattle die there [10]. It seems that we are not to become farmers. It goes the same with us with the oxen: we have already had to slaughter 3, because they became sick. We would gladly [do away with all the cattle, if we saw a poss]ibility of managing without it [11]. But cattle we must have, especially oxen. How else should we get firewood, corn or flour here? With the flour we have a hard time, for in the whole country there is no mill that can grind in summer. There is one a mile and a half from us; but since there is no other within a circuit of 10 miles, everything goes there. Our cart with corn has now been standing there 4 days already, and we do not yet know when it will come back. Then we must also give board and wages there to 1 or 2 people, who see to it that we too get a turn; otherwise no one would give our flour a thought. In the rainy season this mill does not have much to do, for most of the farmers, having mills themselves, grind for themselves, but not for others. As soon as the rainy season is over, the grinding stops too; and now we must provide ourselves with flour, for in the rainy season one cannot cross the river. That so few mills grind in summer comes, then, from the want of water. If only we knew whether we had a lasting place here, we could build ourselves a mill here, for with us there is water enough for a mill even in summer; then we would be freed from this need. From our 5 cows we have not been able to get a drop of milk for our coffee for a long time, and we have no prospect either of where we might get any.

<!-- lines 9182-9206 -->
On the 18th Brother Schwinn had his 45th birthday [12]. At the break of day 4 of our sisters came to wake him with sung verses of blessing, which we accompanied on the flute. Afterwards the others came to wish him blessing. On the 23rd 3 persons were again added to the number of the candidates for baptism [13]. Today some of Baas Teunis's people came, from whom we heard that what the farmer Grunewald said here, namely about the freedom to punish the Hottentots at will, was untrue. On the 25th [14] a farmer named Herz came here to hire a couple of Hottentots for some weeks, but got none. He is a Jew by birth, and was baptised only 2 years ago. He has already lived here in the country 10 years. His wife was a slave, but is now free, and baptised. He lives close to Swellendam.

<!-- lines 9207-9237 -->
On the 24th we had a fine rain, for which there had been great longing, for the drought was very great, it having been very hot for 2 months and not rained at all. The cattle suffered want everywhere, and this drought, as one hears, has been everywhere. Since tomorrow the festival of all the choirs is to be celebrated in our congregations, we put off the solemnity of the baptism to that day, and made known the order of the meetings for tomorrow [15]. So today, the 25th, many people came together. The solemnity of the baptism was performed by Brother Schwinn. [The baptised woman] received the name Louise [16]. The peace of God made itself felt at it. Indeed, we must confess in general that God's Spirit showed himself powerfully in all the meetings in the hearts of those present, and that at the solemnity of the baptism of adults who come out of heathenism there is always something special to be perceived. This day, then, was an excellent day of blessing, for us as well as for our people.

<!-- lines 9237-9277 -->
But since everything here is only in part, and changeable, our joy was damped by hearing of cases that always grieve us. Some women namely came (who are not yet baptised, but do go to school) who had worked for some time with farmers; some of them accused themselves to us of their bad conduct, and asked for forgiveness. Others confessed that they had drunk too much and fought with one another. Others again accused themselves of fornication, saying: "This man and that, with whom we kept company, did promise to marry us, but did not keep their word." So we had to tell them that they might not come to school any more; and that if they wanted to serve the devil and remain his children, we would have nothing more to do with them; and that it was better for them not to hear God's word at all, since they would not accept God's great grace, but trampled it underfoot. It is sad: as long as these poor people, who are still unbaptised and heathen, must stay with the farmers, the so-called Christians, they get their fill of drink, and hear and see all bad things, and the rest of the talk is nothing but cursing and scolding. But when these poor souls are with us, one can often perceive the powerful work of the Holy Spirit in them; but when they are with the farmers again for a time, they lose everything again.

<!-- lines 9278-9311 -->
On the 26th Brother Kühnel went with 7 Hottentots into the bush, which lies 5 hours from here, to fell wood. We want to see about making another little room onto our little house, because at times we are at our wits' end for want of space. Brother Kühnel also means to see about making all kinds of implements from the wood. The reason why one of us went with them is this: if one lets the Hottentots work alone, nothing comes out of their hands, for they spend most of the time smoking tobacco and resting. If one wants them to work diligently, one must be there. On the 27th we slaughtered another sick cow. On the 28th we received a letter from Baas Teunis; he now wrote as friendly as he has never done before. We think that means something. In the evening Brother Kühnel came back from the bush with the Hottentots. Because of the heavy rain they have not been able to cut enough wood, so we must go at it once more, as then happened on the 30th. On the 31st we bought a pig to fatten. In our conference we again resolved [to read] something from the story of Jesus' sufferings, [divided and] assigned to the [evening] meetings [17], beginning it today after a prayer beforehand to enjoy God's blessing in it. Because of the strong wind we now had to hold the singing meeting in the house.

<!-- lines 9312-9334 -->
With this we now close this diary too, commend ourselves and our people to the remembrance of all brothers, sisters and friends, both in and outside our Brethren's congregations, and ask them to entreat the Saviour humbly with us that he may go on convincing the poor heathen further of their misery, so that they may seek from the heart him who accomplished salvation for them too on the cross. In this month few days have passed on which some did not come to us who spoke with us about the unrest of their hearts and declared their longing to be saved. At times the number of these has been great. Praise God, we are all 3 still well, live in love and peace, and remain your 3 poor brothers, H. Marsveld, D. Schwinn and Joh. Chr. Kühnel.
