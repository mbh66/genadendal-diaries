<!-- lines 14598-14617 -->
We took turns with one another at the supervision of the building of our house until 22 February, when a room in it was ready to live in [1]. Brother Schwinn and Brother Kühnel moved into this new dwelling with the word that fell on that day: "With thy blessing let the house of thy servant be blessed for ever." 2 Samuel 7:29. "Lift up both thy pierced hands over us, with thy heart stirred within thee; let us wholly enjoy the blessing thou hast won for us!" [2] The text of the same day was: "He that overcometh shall inherit all things." Revelation 21:7 [3]. "Lord, thy bitter weeping, thy bloody struggle, thy death on the cross, yes, all thy suffering has earned it for me." Meanwhile Brother Marsveld stayed on at Baas Teunis's until all our things could be brought over.

<!-- lines 14618-14633 -->
On 25 February a captain of the Hottentots from Graaff-Reinet came to visit us on his way to Cape Town [4]. His dwelling place borders on the country of the Kaffers [5], at a distance of 200 hours from us, so that one needs at least a month to get there. A church for Christians has recently been built there and supplied with a minister. Today we had a great fright: a Hottentot who was helping a mason as his labourer with the plastering of our chimney fell 4 ells from a scaffold onto a post, and hurt himself so badly that he could work no more [6]. He was the most diligent and orderly of them all, and is very eager to learn. It is sad that in such cases there is no one here who knows medicine. On 26 February Brother Marsveld arrived here with the rest of our goods, so that we now live together in our house.

<!-- lines 14634-14676 -->
On 2 March Baas Teunis also came with his wife to visit us, which was very welcome to us, also because [they brought us some vegetables] [7]. On this occasion we will report something of how things stand with food, as regards the country and as regards ourselves. In general there is an abundance of it in this country. A farmer whose household is in order is well off, and has meat in plenty, having many cattle, which graze and keep themselves in the field without the farmer having any trouble with them, and increase so much that enough can be slaughtered to feed the farmer. Besides this he sows as much corn as he needs for bread. The vegetables grow in his garden, and precious fruits too. As for us, we fared very well at Baas Teunis's during our stay of two months, and ate at his table; since we have been here at our place, we can buy bread and meat, but nothing else. Butter is not to be had, because the farmers keep no more cows than they need just for their household. When a farmer has 30 or 50 oxen, one will seldom find that he has more than 10 or 12 cows [8], which give hardly as much milk as 2 cows in the fatherland. When the cows are milked, the calf must first suck and stay by the cow, otherwise she will not let herself be milked; and so, because of its scarcity, one cannot get butter to buy. The farmers will not let us have vegetables, because they are not well disposed towards us, and the nearest of them lives at a distance of 1½ hours from us. At Baas Teunis's we could get the vegetables we need, but he lives 5 hours from us [9]; so we must learn to eat dry bread, and that is only crushed and not quite cleaned of the grains, which comes from there being no mills in the country. Our dear brothers and sisters can well imagine that to eat this unground dry bread one must be really hungry. Mutton must be salted quickly, or it goes bad, and one can eat fresh meat of a sheep only once. The rest must be kept pickled. So we have no other meat to eat than salted. The vegetables have to be stewed with sheep's fat, which is taken from the long tails of the sheep, which are full of fat.

<!-- lines 14677-14704 -->
Baas Teunis told us that he meant to drive to the town on 1 March [10]; if any of us wished to drive with him, he should get ready for it. To that end we held our first conference on 3 March, to consider which of us should go there, since our circumstances required it, partly to consult with the government about one thing and another concerning our dwelling place and the Hottentots who had joined us, partly to buy various necessary things for ourselves; and Brother Marsveld was named for it. In this country nothing of such necessities is to be had, and so they must be fetched from Cape Town. Everything one needs, except food, which is still reasonably cheap, is exceedingly dear. If we had had to pay for our house and the materials needed for it in the usual way of this country, it would have cost us very dear; but Baas Teunis has let us have the materials for a very small price, or quite free. By the established rate of this country one must pay a craftsman from whom one has something made or built one rixdollar [11], and give him food and drink besides. And even so they make difficulties about doing it, and prefer to take the work by contract. The work itself goes forward very slowly, and they take good care not to overwork. There are also only a few craftsmen in this country.

<!-- lines 14705-14724 -->
On the same day, 3 March, we celebrated the festival of our moving into our finished house [12]. Until now we had not yet been alone, because the master mason had been sleeping with us all this time; but now that he had finished, he went away. The house is not quite finished yet, but what is still to be done to it can be done by the Hottentots. So we consecrated this house to our Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ as a dwelling, in which he and his Holy Spirit might be pleased to come and live, imploring him with our whole heart to grant us of his grace everything we need for his service and the fulfilment of our calling, and to let our testimony of his becoming man, his suffering and dying, find a blessed entrance into the hearts of these poor heathen. We promised him [new faithfulness and obedience, and he let] us feel his lovely and comforting nearness blessedly in this [13]. The word of that day was: "Hezekiah trusted in the Lord God of Israel; he clave to the Lord, and departed not from following him. So the Lord was with him." 2 Kings 18:5 to 7 [14]. The verse below it read: "I will stay with thee, my Jesus. Hold me fast, be near me!"

<!-- lines 14725-14794 -->
Today we had the elderly among the Hottentots who live with us come together, and made known to them that for those among them who felt the inclination and had the ability to learn we would begin the school tomorrow; but that with the children we would put off until after our Brother Marsveld's return from the town. Several of the old people made it known that they wished to get ahead of the children in learning, saying: "When our children come home, and we ask them, 'What have you learned at school?', and we still know nothing of it, they can tell us whatever they like; but if we are better taught in it, that cannot happen." We told them: "As soon as Brother Marsveld has come back from the town, we mean to make a start, on Sunday evenings after sunset, with reading the Bible aloud and explaining to you what has been read" [15]. For the present we two German brethren were not able to do it, not yet being sufficiently master of the Dutch language [16]. To that end we wrote down the names of the old people, as well as of the children whom the parents want to send to school [17]. The old people are all very eager to learn, so that they may be able to read the Bible. Now although many of them are so old that one can hardly expect that they could learn to read, we could not advise them against it, so as not to make them believe that we love them less than the young. They may at least make the trial. Accordingly we gave each of them an A B C book, on their promising to give the book back to us if they lost the wish to go on with it. The school of the old people was to begin at 8 o'clock in the morning, and then that of the children. In the afternoon there was to be school for the children first, and then towards evening the instruction for the old people. There are now already 8 houses or kraals (for that is what they call their houses) inhabited by Hottentots who have come to us to learn. One is moved to sadness on seeing that so many Hottentots are eager to hear the word of God, but live so scattered that they will seldom be able to come; and to settle here altogether will be still less possible, because this is a very barren stretch of country for cattle. It is said that the cattle, and especially the sheep, on this side of the river nearly all die, because it is much colder here than on the other side of the river (called the Sonderend) [18]. So the farmers who live on this side bring their cattle over to the other side in winter. The river is also so swollen in winter that one cannot get over it; so for the winter one must provide oneself with necessities for 2 or 3 months. The swelling of the river comes chiefly from the many heavy rains that fall in the winter time. This river lies an hour away from us. How it will go when we are able to provide ourselves with cattle may well give us cause for thought. The cold of this side is chiefly to be ascribed to the many high mountains, very close to which we live [19]. The mountain near our house is higher than any we have yet seen. These are many difficulties and discomforts, to be sure; but what is this compared with what our brethren had to undergo at the first posts among the heathen, where they had to go far and wide to seek out souls who would hear a word of Jesus; and here they come to seek us out, to hear and to learn and to live with us! Oh, God grant that it may be lasting and constant, and that we his poor servants may be counted worthy to receive from him what we need, so as not to harm his work in any way! Then we took counsel together on how best to arrange the Hottentots' future meetings for divine service, and agreed on a time to be fixed.

## Notes

1. *tot dat daar in eene Kamer om te woonen, klaar was*, "until a room in it was ready to live in". This settles the German *Römer* (A363595, entry 03, note 16), which is to be read *Kammer*, "room".
2. The transcription reads *over ons met rommelnde ingewanden*, literally "over us with rumbling bowels", the old biblical figure for a heart moved with pity; the German diary has "fulfil all the blessings that stir in thy heart for us". The English gives the sense.
3. *Openb. 25, 17*: the verse is Revelation 21:7, as in the German diary. The Bible verses are given in the words of the King James Version.
4. *Graf Renet*: Graaff-Reinet, the frontier drostdy founded in 1786; see `glossary.csv`. Its first church was built in 1792.
5. *'t Land der Kafferen*: the diarists' word for the Xhosa people and their country; see `glossary.csv`. The German diary has "close to the Kaffers". The "200 hours" is not in the German.
6. *viel van een Stellagie 4 Ellen hoog neder op een Paal*, "fell from a scaffold 4 ells high onto a post". This settles the German *Stienge* or *Stiege* (A363595, entry 03, note 19): *Stange*, "a pole" or "post", is probably meant there. *als Opperman*: as a labourer serving the mason.
7. Line 14636 is missing. The transcription runs *door⸗* / *kwamen*. The German diary has "also because they brought us some vegetables, which we badly need"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there. The German also says that they came with their 5 children.
8. The German diary has "If a farmer has 100 oxen".
9. The German diary has "that is 3 hours away"; on 3 January the Dutch gives the journey to the post as 4 hours by ox wagon (entry 51).
10. *d. 1 Maart*: the German diary has "on the 5th", and Brother Marsveld left with Baas Teunis on the 6th (entry 55). *1* is probably a misreading of *5*.
11. The German diary has "1 rixdollar a day".
12. The German diary has *ſegneten wir unſern Einzug b- ſtätigt*, which its translation found garbled (A363595, entry 04, note 1). The Dutch *vierden wy het feeſt, van onzen Intogt*, "we celebrated the festival of our moving in", gives the sense.
13. Line 14718 is missing. The transcription runs *Geloovende Hem* / *ons daarby Zyne lieflyke en vertrooſtende nabyheid zalig ge⸗voelen*. *Geloovende* is probably *Beloovende*, "promising". The German diary has "Then we shared the cup of praise and covenant among ourselves and promised the Saviour new faithfulness and obedience. He graciously acknowledged us poor ones"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there, and the lost line probably also mentioned the cup of covenant.
14. The transcription reads *200 was de Heere met hem Jeſ: 18, 5 g*: *200* is a misreading of *Zoo*, "so", and the reference, given as Isaiah (*Jeſ:*), is 2 Kings 18:5 to 7. The Dutch *Hizkias*, "Hezekiah", settles the German *Blicke vertrauſte dem HErrn* (A363595, entry 04, note 3): *Blicke* is a misreading of *Hiskia*.
15. The German diary has "in the evening before sunset", with no mention of Sundays.
16. *wy, twee Duitſche Broeders*: Brother Marsveld was Dutch, and Brothers Schwinn and Kühnel German; with Marsveld away, the two Germans could not yet preach in Dutch.
17. The German diary adds that "the number came to 60 altogether".
18. The Sonderend River; see `glossary.csv`.
19. The transcription reads *De koude, van deeze tyde*, "the cold of this time"; *tyde* is probably *zyde*, "side", as the sense requires. The German diary has "our house stands so close to the mountains that one could reach them with a musket ball".
