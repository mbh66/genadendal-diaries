<!-- lines 14253-14263 -->
On 1 January 1793, after the guests at the festival had already wished one another a happy new year in the night after 12 o'clock, a flag was hoisted, which flew the whole day. This may be done nowhere except where there is a Company post. In these festival days we saw with astonishment how far pride goes among the female sex of this country. We have seen farmers' daughters so adorned and with their hair so dressed as we have hardly seen on a lady in the fatherland [1].

<!-- lines 14264-14273 -->
On 2 January we received a letter from Brother Haensel from Cape Town, where he had arrived on his return journey from Tranquebar to Europe [2]. We would very much have liked to speak with him in person; but since that could not be, we wrote him a letter, and at the same time one to Europe. On 3 January our Baas Teunis told us that he meant to drive to Baviaanskloof tomorrow, to peg out the place for the house to be built for us, and to see whether he could get people who were willing to work.

<!-- lines 14274-14299 -->
On the 4th we drove off at 9 o'clock in the morning and took our way by his son's house, to supply ourselves there with the necessary tools, and to take with us the mason who is working on his son's house. When we arrived at our place at 3 o'clock, the house was pegged out. The space inside took up 36 feet in length and 15 in breadth, to make two rooms and a kitchen in it. The height up to the roof is 7 feet. First a tent was put up, under which we and the mason were to lodge meanwhile. We had hardly got there when some 10 Hottentot men and 10 Hottentot women came to welcome us [3], to whom we proclaimed the Saviour under the pear tree that Brother Schmidt planted, which had grown fairly big [4]. As soon as the place for the house had been pegged out, we began to carry together stones from Brother Schmidt's former house to the new dwelling place, which lies only 15 paces from the first house. The walls of Brother Schmidt's house were of stone, but ours will be built more of clay, like those of Baas Teunis's house, which have stood undamaged for all of 50 years. The gable and inner walls will be built of unburnt bricks. The Hottentots helped us carry the stones as long as it was day. Baas Teunis had spoken to them and encouraged them to help us for a moderate wage and at their own cost for food, adding that the work must be finished before the winter, because at that time it rains hard and so no building can be done.

<!-- lines 14300-14353 -->
They all promised to help and to provide their own food; but this is a foolish thing for them, since they have nothing. When they work for the farmers, they go to the kraal and eat and drink [as long as there is anything there. Many have] no wish to work, and lie in their huts the whole time [5]. When they grow hungry, they look for roots and fruits and are content with them. When they have something, they dance and are merry and in good spirits. Among those who promised to help us were two quite respectable people, who have cattle and fine gardens. These are born of a Christian father and a Hottentot mother. They are called Bastards, and there are several of this kind in this country, to whom also belong those who have for a father a Negro, of those who are slaves here. So these two named above were able to provide for their own food. They are also our two nearest neighbours, lying about 2 hours from us, which the Hottentots call close by [6]. They have promised us besides to help us with vegetables, which is of great importance to us, since at the beginning we would not know where to get anything. The farmers would bring us nothing, for they look with disfavour on our having come into the country; and even if it were not so, we live too far from them, the nearest living at a distance of 2 hours from us. To get to the Company post, where Baas Teunis lives, we must drive 4 hours with an ox wagon. How much time one needs to go there on foot I do not know, for in this country one cannot well go on foot, because the brooks and rivers in this country cannot well be crossed on foot [7]: the oxen sometimes have to wade through them up to their bellies, and there are no bridges or footbridges anywhere. If one asks how far it is to travel to this place or that, the answer is: with an ox wagon so many hours, and on horseback so many, which takes fully half the time of a wagon. Yet in this country one gets as far with such a wagon as in Germany with the ordinary post, for the oxen must go hard, and often do so for 12 hours without resting once, because there are no inns at which one can stable them. On horseback it goes twice as fast, since one rides at a gallop all the time if the hill is not too high; otherwise one rides up and down it equally fast. In general there are no stables in this country, neither for horses nor for oxen and sheep. The cattle come under no roof the whole year through, and get no fodder but what they look for themselves in the pastures; so one sometimes has to go an hour's distance when one wants to use them, since they can stray so far.

<!-- lines 14354-14393 -->
When the first preparations for building our house had been made, Baas Teunis went back home, and made difficulties about leaving us here. He said that we could not help with the building itself anyway, since Europeans were not as skilled in the way of building in this country as the Hottentots; and besides, he feared we might fall ill, since we were not used to working in this country, where the great heat by day (and it was just at its highest) was followed by cool nights, which might become very harmful to our health, as we had to sleep in a tent. But we could not bring ourselves to that, and we also wanted to make a start on working our garden, and thought it necessary to be present at the building; so he had a sheep and some vegetables given us for our food. On 5 January, after we had slept very little in our tent, we had the sheep slaughtered by a Hottentot. This day was now fixed for laying the foundation of the house. The Hottentots who had undertaken to work at it had been told to come at 5 o'clock. But they did not come until about 9, giving as their excuse that they had first had to look for food, and adding that if they were provided with it, they would gladly come and work. One of the two mentioned above, who are not so needy, said that it was rather hard for the other Hottentots; when they work for the farmers they are fed, and on that condition, of working diligently, the farmers were quite willing to meet their wishes, but not otherwise [8]. He advised us to put into his hands the money they earn for their work, and he would get flour for bread for them; for if they received it themselves, they would squander the money and still have nothing to eat [9]. We were very well content with this proposal and will follow it. On 5 January the first stone of our house was laid. The word for the day was: "The Lord thy God turned the curse into a blessing unto thee, because the Lord thy God loved thee." Deuteronomy 23:5. "If God is my friend, what reason have I to be afraid? No power shall tread me down; I fear no violence nor pain." [...] [10] "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which persecute you; that" [11]

<!-- lines 14394-14412 -->
Today old Lena, who was baptised by Brother Schmidt, also came to welcome us. She lives an hour from here, and had needed 3 hours to come [12]. She told us that she had been the first to come to Georg Schmidt, and had cooked for all who worked on his house. We asked her where Schmidt had got anything to cook, and whether many had worked on his house. Answer: he had cooked [bought] meal and groats, and had had many people at work [13]. But at that time there was not so much poverty among the Hottentots as now; they still had cattle then, and so had meat and milk in plenty; and many Hottentots lived here then. Question: why had they moved away from here, then, and where were they now? Answer: after Schmidt's departure they had gone to the farmers, and many had died, and this place was quite deserted. "When your house is built, I mean to come again to learn." When one spoke with her of Jesus and his merit, she seemed to be as ignorant as the others. It is a wonder: here they all understand the Dutch language, but if one speaks of spiritual things, it is like a foreign language to them, for they have never yet heard such words.

<!-- lines 14413-14438 -->
On Sunday 9 January we visited the Hottentot who means to buy the flour for the others [14]. Here the household looks neat, as does the clothing of his wife and children [15]. We drank milk here, and he also gave us some cucumbers to take home. The most respectable Hottentots go clothed, and the women too. Otherwise the clothing of most women consists only of a sheepskin, which they call a kaross [16] and hang over their shoulders. Many of them will not work, and lie in their huts all day. In the afternoon eleven Hottentot women came again with little children to visit us. They carry the children on their backs, with the sheepskin over them so that only the head can be seen. Round the child's back they tie a thong to hold it fast. Every Hottentot, man as well as woman, carries on the back a bag of steenbok skin, which they strip off without cutting it open, so that it does not need to be sewn, and in it they put their tobacco, pipes and so on. When they come to visit, they sit down on the ground, and one of them lights a pipe, takes a couple of puffs, and then gives the pipe to another, and so it goes round. When the pipe is smoked out, she fills another, and in this way they sometimes stay sitting a whole half day. This is done by the women as well as the men. On 7 January the work on the house was begun by 14 persons. The first thing was that they made clay ready.

<!-- lines 14439-14451 -->
This is done as follows. One of them took a plough, put 6 oxen to it, and ploughed up a piece of land. Then a harrow was taken and hitched on, and the oxen were driven round in the clay until it was finely trodden. Next a hide was taken, the trodden clay put on it, and dragged with 6 oxen to the place where it was needed. Others formed bricks from it, and others again began to build the walls [17], a foot high every day, and then it had to dry for a day. Brother Kühnel was not quite well that day, probably from the great heat by day and the heavy dew at night, by which even our mattresses had become wet through and through.

<!-- lines 14452-14473 -->
On the 9th those who work for us came with their wives and children and pitched their tents on the old walls close by our house, to live there until our house is finished [18]. [That] day a baboon came into our garden and climbed a peach tree to fetch peaches. One of our Hottentots fetched our gun, but it did not go off, and so the baboon escaped. Whether we shall keep anything in our garden we do not know. The pear tree that Brother Schmidt planted bore much fruit, but the baboons have stolen the pears before they were ripe; and these animals are here in great numbers. They come over the mountains in whole troops and make a fearful howling; one must marvel that these animals can scream so. Today old Lena visited us again. We now found too that it was as Baas Teunis had told us: that we could do nothing here, and must be afraid of illness, of which we were already noticing signs. So we went back to Baas Teunis's house for the time being. He, as well as his wife, is glad that we decided to do this and so took care of our health. Meanwhile the supervision of the building is in the hands of the [mason], who [...] and is a Dutchman by birth [19].

<!-- lines 14474-14510 -->
12 January being Communion day in our congregation, we considered this together; but we had to forgo it until we could move into our own dwelling [20]. We do have a room of our own at Baas Teunis's, but we cannot be undisturbed for half an hour. It is hardly to be believed how lively it is here, partly because of the many slaves with their children who are here, partly because of the constant visits of strangers, from which not one day has been free as long as we have been here. They stay one or two days eating and drinking, and hardly give thanks for it, for that is the custom of the country. On these occasions we get to know many farmers, and they us. Towards us they still show themselves friendly, but not so behind our backs. In Baas Teunis's presence, though, they dare not let anything be seen. He says: "You people have few friends, but many enemies." And that is no wonder either, for many Hottentots are already saying that they no longer want to work for the farmers, but will go to us to learn. Who in that case is to do the farmers' work? They cannot buy many slaves, for at one time they have much to do, at another little. For this they hire the Hottentots by the day and the week, and when the work is done they send them away again; that cannot be done with slaves. (Indeed, many farmers are also forbidden to buy slaves, because they treat them so unmercifully, and so they must have their work done by the Hottentots.) It is said that there are farmers living in the country who have 20 to 30 Hottentots at work.

<!-- lines 14511-14534 -->
On 19 January a Hottentot came who lives 10 days' journey from here, and had been at Cape Town with the commander to ask permission to go to us and learn [21]. The gentleman sent him here to Baas Teunis, telling him that he would find us here. He has run away from his master, whom he served for 7 years, and has 5 children besides, whom he would also gladly have here to learn; but he did not know how he could bring it about, for his children served the same master, and if he went back there to fetch them, he ran the risk of being shot dead by the farmer [22]. Baas Teunis advised him to stay at Baviaanskloof for a time, for our house had to be built first before a start could be made with the teaching. It must be noted here that when a Hottentot wants to move from one place to another, he must report it to the Landdrost under whose authority he belongs, or to the field corporal, and give the reason why he wants to leave. This is done so that no one who has done wrong may escape punishment by living in another place.

<!-- lines 14535-14562 -->
On 20 January we received a letter from Brother Haensel from the town, in which he gave us to understand that he would have liked to receive a fuller letter from us; which we would indeed have written, if we had known that he would stay there so long. He had written to us in his first letter that he was to leave the Cape again in 8 or 10 days, and the messenger, who arrived late in the evening, wanted to go away again early the next morning. Now a Hottentot who is a good runner needs 3 days to make the journey, and this made us write only a short letter [23]. We would most have wished that Brother Haensel could have come to us, to see us in our occupations at the beginning and be able to give the brethren a report by word of mouth of how things look with us. Brother Kühnel and Brother Schwinn were not quite well in these days, but it is better again now [24]. We believe it would be good if one of us were present at the building of our house; and since Baas Teunis's son is building at his house most of the time, whichever of us is [there can eat and sleep at his place and every] day go over to see to the building [25]. Brother Schwinn is busy putting up sauerkraut and beans, and also drying peaches for the winter, all of which we get free from Baas Teunis. Brother Kühnel is busy writing; and so Brother Marsveld is going to Baviaanskloof for the purpose mentioned, until one of us has time to relieve him.

<!-- lines 14563-14582 -->
On the 26th the Landdrost of Stellenbosch came to visit us. He said that an order had come from the government in the town to all landdrosts and field corporals that they were to see to it that no farmer might keep back a Hottentot who wanted to learn from going to us; and if this happened, they were to punish it severely and report it; much less were they to allow the least obstacle to be put in our way. Accordingly he told us that if anything of the kind should happen, we should not fail to report it to him at once, for our place is under his command [26]. On the 28th Baas Teunis received a letter from Mr J., the minister at Swellendam [27], in which he seriously expressed his displeasure that we had not yet visited him. But in the first place we did not know that a minister lives there; and now that we have learned it, we do not know how we are to get there, since the place lies two days' journey from us, and a wagon is needed for it, which would cost us much money.

<!-- lines 14583-14597 -->
During the two months that we stayed at Baas Teunis's, we got to hear many sayings of farmers, which mostly came to this, that they do not like to see the Hottentots going to us to learn. Among other things they spoke thus to two Hottentots who showed a wish to go to us [28]: "Just wait, you will get a good beating, for I have heard that they have brought a whole chest full of canes with them to beat you with, and they beat cruelly." These two Hottentots answered: "We have seen at the farmers' that when the children will not learn, they have been beaten; and so we must put up with that too." This answer made the farmer keep quiet. From this one sees that the Hottentots have a great longing to learn. Oh, may it please God that it lasts so!

## Notes

1. In the German diary these words end the account of the New Year festivities (A363595, entry 02, last paragraph), after the dancing, the meal at 11 o'clock at night, the shooting at midnight and the soldiers' quarrel, all of which the Dutch leaves out. The German says the women were "Germans" in their pride, and speaks of their great headdresses (*Kopfzügen*, note 23 there); the Dutch *gekapt*, "with their hair dressed", supports that reading.
2. Johann Gottfried Haensel, a Moravian missionary on the Nicobar Islands; see `glossary.csv`. Tranquebar, the Danish settlement on the Coromandel coast of India, was the base of the Moravian mission to the Nicobars. The German diary has "from the East Indies".
3. *aan 10 Hottentotten, en 10 Hottentottinnen*: the German diary has 9 men, and "soon afterwards" 10 women.
4. *welken wy onder den Peereboom ... den Heiland verkondigden*, "to whom we proclaimed the Saviour under the pear tree". This settles the German *Wir wieſen ihnen den lieben Heiland unter dem Birnbaum an* (A363595, entry 03, note 2).
5. Line 14308 is missing. The transcription runs *gaan zy in de kraal en eeten en drinken* / *geen lust tot werken*. The German diary has "as long as there is anything there. Many of them will do nothing at all"; the words in square brackets are supplied from there.
6. *omtrent 2 Uur van ons af gelegen, 't welk de Hottents digt by noemen*. The German diary has "about half an hour away from us. Half an hour away they dug [?], close by", which its translation could not explain (A363595, entry 03, note 5). The Dutch shows the sense: the German *Griſſen ſie* is probably a misreading of *heißen ſie*, "they call [it] close by". The two copies differ on the distance.
7. *de Breeken en Rivieren*: *Breeken* is probably a misreading of *Beeken*, "brooks". The German diary has "the many rivers where water is always running".
8. The sentence is garbled in the transcription (*om onder beding dat wanneer zy by de Boeren werken, om gespysd worden, zo waren de Boeren op die voorwaarde van vlytig te arbeiden heel gewillig ...*). The English gives the sense, which agrees with the German diary: the farmers feed those who work for them.
9. The German diary says that he wanted to be paid out of the Khoekhoe's wages for the flour he would buy for them, "but if we gave the money to the Hottentots, he would get nothing for his flour". The Dutch gives a different reason: that they would squander the money.
10. Line 14390 is missing, at the foot of page 243b. It probably held the words introducing the doctrinal text (*De Leertekst was*); the German diary has "And the doctrinal text". The first text is Deuteronomy 23:5, as given; its hymn verse differs from the German ("If I have the Head for my friend and am beloved of God").
11. The doctrinal text, Matthew 5:44 to 45, breaks off after *opdat*, "that", and the diary goes on with old Lena at the next line. The German diary continues: "ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven. Truly, that we may forgive our enemies from the bottom of our hearts." *[u]* in *die [u] vervolgen* is the transcriber's addition. The Bible verses are given in the words of the King James Version.
12. *en had 3 Uuren daartoe nodig gehad*; the German diary has "she had brought three more with her". One copy has misread the other.
13. *Meel en Gort had hy gekookt*, "he had cooked meal and groats". The German diary has "he had bought flour and barley for the people", which fits the question; *gekookt* is probably a misreading of *gekocht*, "bought".
14. *Zondag d. 9 Jan*: the German diary has "Sunday the 6th". 6 January 1793 was a Sunday; the Dutch *9* is a slip, and the entry for 7 January follows.
15. *Hier ziet het doen niet, gelyk ook met de Kleeding van Vrouw en Kinderen ordentlyk uit*. *niet*, "not", contradicts the sense and the German diary ("Things look orderly with him"); it is probably a misreading of *net*, "neat".
16. *een Krots*: the kaross; see `glossary.csv`. The Dutch leaves out the German diary's sentence on the small skin worn in front.
17. *te metzelen*, "to lay [walls]". This supports the German diary's normalised *mauern* for the transcription's *zu Klieben* (A363595, entry 03, note 9).
18. The German diary puts this on the 8th and the baboon on the 9th. In the Dutch, line 14455 begins *Dag kwam een Baviaan*; a word such as *Deezen* is missing at the start of the line.
19. Line 14472 is missing. The transcription runs *neemt intusſchen den* / *gen is, en een Hollander van geboorte is*. The German diary has "Baas Teunis has handed over the building and its supervision to his mason, who is a Dutchman"; the word in square brackets is supplied from there. The German also says that the brethren drove back with Baas Teunis's son on the 10th.

22. The German diary has "he is in danger of the farmer punishing him"; the Dutch *doodgeschooten*, "shot dead".
23. This settles the garbled German *und wenn es einer in drey Tagen gehat* (A363595, entry 03, note 12): a good Khoekhoe runner needed three days for the journey to the Cape, so the brethren had to write in haste.
