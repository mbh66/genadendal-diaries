<!-- lines 10252-10271 -->
On 3 July we held the Holy Communion that had been put off 8 days before, which Sister Bedgen partook of with us for the first time [1]. Today we received a letter from Mr Heuser at the Cape. He reports in it that the English were still lying before the town, but that the burghers meant to defend themselves to the utmost; adding further that the ship which had things for us on board had been seized by the English. "It is also said," he writes further, "that the whole of Holland is in the hands of the French, and that the Prince of Orange is in England." By this our correspondence with Holland is now cut off entirely, and we see no possibility of hearing anything from Holland, however great our longing is to learn something of our dear brothers and sisters at Zeist and Neuwied.

<!-- lines 10272-10302 -->
On 5 July a messenger came from Baas Teunis to tell us that, having heard that Hottentots had come to Baviaanskloof with many cattle, he commanded the Hottentots to go to the Cape, and their cattle must be sent back. So one order comes after another, to send to the Cape all the Hottentots that can be brought together. The government has sent word to the rebels to come to the Cape and hand in their complaints there, which would be heard, provided that they worked to help defend the country. Their answer was that they would not come, and they stood firm in their purpose. Baas Teunis's messenger did not find here the Hottentots he had been looking for, so he rode back again and took the road on which he thought to meet them. Whether he succeeded in this [we do not know. Amid all these confus]ed circumstances we notice a powerful work of the Holy Spirit in the hearts of our Hottentots, and especially among those who arrived most recently [2]. But not knowing what lot still awaits them and us, we had no freedom of heart at present to think of a baptism. The poor Hottentots are seized with fear, and the farmers make them believe ten times more than is true.

<!-- lines 10303-10339 -->
On 9 July 7 of the Hottentots whom Baas Teunis had had searched for here arrived. We had them come to us, to ask them whether Baas Teunis had spoken to them and told them what he wanted done by them. When they answered yes, we gave them to understand that they could not stay here, but must go back to Baas Teunis, and gave them a letter written by us to him to take with them. On the 10th some who had been with the farmers told us that they had heard from them that a commando of the rebels would come here that very day, on the way to the Cape, with 300 wagons [3]. We did not let ourselves be disturbed by this, and it soon turned out that it was not true. On the 11th a Hottentot who had come yesterday told us that he could no longer keep silent, but must say what he had heard. One of the farmers who have deposed the Landdrost of Swellendam had asked him whether he too meant to go to school. "Yes, Baas!" was his answer. At this the farmer replied that he might just go; but the school would soon come to an end. The Hottentots must not be made wise, but stay as ignorant as they were. After all, so many children of Christians run about who learn nothing; why should the Hottentots learn anything [4]? "We shall soon do away with the schools; for we are the authorities now; do you know that?" This very same thing was also said to him by another farmer on his way here. We also came to hear that the rebels meant to hold a meeting at Swellendam on 8 August [5].

<!-- lines 10340-10362 -->
On 12 July we received a letter from Father Schmidt at the Cape, who lamented greatly the present sad circumstances of the country, and wished to be with us for a time. But here too, even those who had shown the most steadfastness are seized with fear by the growing threatening rumours. They speak much of fleeing, and ask us what we would do, wanting to follow our example in it [6]. We ourselves must admit that we are not at ease about it, and so cannot give others good advice with freedom of heart; but we only tell them so much, that if they had a mind to flee they might always do it; we, meanwhile, would not do it yet. When they then tell us that they too would wait with fleeing until we went, we answer them that in that case we would not be in a position to do them any helpful service.

<!-- lines 10363-10418 -->
David, who had been away from here a couple of days, came home again on 14 July, telling us that he had heard that the rebels were at Swellendam again, expecting reinforcement, and having 300 Hottentots with them. When the said reinforcement had arrived, they would come to Baviaanskloof and shoot us and our Hottentots dead. 300 Hottentots had joined them. From every side [we heard such news, and were] very much at a loss what we should decide [7]. At last we decided that the Brothers Marsveld and Kühnel should ride out the next morning, the first to Pieter Dohi, and the second to Geldenhuis and Mary, to learn the truth or untruth of it. This happened on the 15th. Towards evening Brother Kühnel came home again. From both he learned sad news, as much about the Cape as about the disturbances in the countryside. Yesterday the fourth messenger of the government to the rebels had come back again with nothing achieved; and they were making ready to push on to the Zonder river, 2 hours' walk from the Cape, and only there to enter into negotiation [8]. On 16 July Brother Marsveld came back too, having been able to learn little from Pieter Dohi. But he has promised in case of need to hide our cattle at his place, and to come to our help with his horses if we should have to flee. We had noticed that many Hottentots would gladly have fled, but no one wanted to make the beginning, and they could not bring themselves to leave us, since after all we had come to them out of an urge of love. So we told them in the next meeting that in this they should act entirely as their hearts bade them, without waiting to see how we would behave in these circumstances, and that no one would be blamed if they left us now. Our Martha, who is in service with us, we had seen by her tears for several days already to be very afraid [9]. This moved us to tell her in particular that she might act freely as her heart bade her. Late in the evening Anna, Marie, Elisabeth, Rahel and Magdalena came with an old Hottentot to tell us that, being very frightened, they would hide in the mountains this night, and now came to take leave of us [10]. Our hearts were moved with the most inward compassion; for it was so dark that the eye could not see the hand.

<!-- lines 10419-10461 -->
On 17 July Brother Kühnel rode to Baas Teunis to take counsel with him. His first word was: "The prospect is as bad as it can ever be"; and he told how he had spoken with several rebels, who now call themselves Nationals, and had found that they were extremely angry at the school, and for this reason, that the Hottentots were drawn away by it from service with them, saying: "From whom shall we now get our work done, since we have no slaves? The Hottentots have lived so long among the farmers [11] and have not been taught; why then does it happen now? You who live on the Sonderend River can get as many Hottentots as you want; but where shall we get them from? Why do these people not come into our parts, where they will have it just as good as anywhere in other places?" His advice accordingly was that we should not accept any of the Hottentots who come to us from the district of Swellendam, but make them go back to their former places. This would satisfy the farmers; but if it did not happen, a great misfortune could come of it. He went on to say: "For a time I have held back, as well as I could, the Hottentots coming from those parts; and yet there are 53 of them on this side of the river, [whom I have forbidden to go to you. I] cannot get them back from there [12]; but if you told them, they would go; for one word from you has more force with them than ten from me. The Hottentots of another district they will probably do no harm, and then leave you in undisturbed peace too." In the evening Brother Kühnel came back again.

<!-- lines 10461-10499 -->
Baas Teunis's advice put us in great perplexity; and weighing it before the face of the Saviour [13], we judged it most advisable at this time, for our poor people and for ourselves, to follow this advice. But you can easily imagine, dear brothers, how we must feel at it: to tell souls seeking their salvation, in whom one perceives the most powerful work of the Holy Spirit, who never grow tired of hearing the Gospel of Jesus' suffering and death proclaimed, who steadfastly keep saying, "Let it go with us as it will, we will not give up God's word"; to tell such people to go away from us. And yet we judged it most necessary in these threatening circumstances, commending them to the unchangeable mercy of God, who as the ruler of the universe is mighty enough to change these circumstances. So we decided to let the school stop for this time, and to have our Hottentots come together the next day to make known to them our decision and the reason for it. During the night most of the women and children had hidden in the mountains, for fear of being attacked in the night. In the morning they could be seen coming out of the mountains on every side. We had them called, and in haste they were all together, when we made known to them our decision mentioned above. The stir it caused among them cannot be described. No eye stayed dry. Hardly an hour afterwards 13 came [14]. A heart of stone would have been softened, seeing the women and children shed the hottest tears.

<!-- lines 10500-10533 -->
Most pitiful of all was the sight of little children tied quite naked on their mothers' backs, while it was very cold and raining hard, and the rivers were so swollen that in our neighbourhood, for example, the water in the river covered the lower half of the body, and further on the rivers were deeper still. Others came and said: "How shall I get on, being unwell, and having little children who cannot walk, and whom I shall hardly be able to carry, since I myself am scarcely able to walk?" These, and still others who complained of yet other difficulties, came to ask our counsel; and we ourselves were at a loss. Counting how many there were who left us at our word, we found that their number came to 30, whom we counted, besides those whom we could not count any further, struck as we were with grief and sorrow; so that we cannot give the true number exactly, since the leave-taking lasted until late in the evening. What a day this was for us we cannot describe: a day that we shall not forget all our lives. Being so moved, we were not in a state to hold a meeting, and put it off [15]. In the evening we closed this sad day with a prayer on our knees, calling on the Saviour's help with the greatest urgency, and commending our poor Hottentot sheep to his merciful heart.

<!-- lines 10533-10552 -->
We had lately read together, with great compassion and sym[pathy, the diary of the wandering congregation of the North American Indians; but the circumstances in] which we now find ourselves [seem to us] still more dismal [16]. When the Indians must flee, they have at least a place to which they can flee; the Hottentots are deprived of this. The Indians can stay together and keep their teachers with them; but the Hottentots cannot. These, wherever they turn, come among farmers who try to make slaves of them. The Indians can have the little they have carried over to other places in boats; but the Hottentots must carry their goods and children away on their backs.

<!-- lines 10553-10615 -->
On 19 July our baptised came in the morning to the church prayer. Not one of them has gone away yet, although some of them belong at home at Swellendam. We thought we would have room to spare at the sermon, after so many had moved away yesterday; but the room, the front room and the kitchen were filled with hearers. More people have lived here than we had known. We did see that the house was full every time; but those who stood outside we could not see. On the 20th we received a letter from Mrs Teunis, who reported to us that yesterday a commando of the Nationals had been at Mrs Lind's. If we would ride there, we might perhaps come to know something concerning the school; for a paper had been read out there, which was to be handed in to the government, in which mention is also made of the school. On the 21st Brother Kühnel rode there, and found that the article concerning us was of roughly this content: "There must be no Herrnhuters here to teach the Hottentots. After all, there are so many Christians in this country who are not taught; why should that happen with the Hottentots? Well then, they must stay ignorant, as they were before. The Hottentots who are born on a place belonging to the farmers must stay there, and serve the farmers for nothing until they have reached their 25th year. The Hottentots must live among the farmers, and not together, as at Baviaanskloof. The Bushmen whom we catch must remain slaves for their lifetime. Among these, and somewhat among the Hottentots in our parts, one would have found the Herrnhuters [17]." These and more such things have been laid down in the said paper under the name of Liberty and Equality. Indeed, the people say: "What was read out does not contain a third part of their resolutions; and nevertheless we have had to sign the whole paper, if we did not want to put our lives in danger." In this way 3000 people have already signed. These men who call themselves Nationals would know nothing of the Company as their rulers, and mean to oppose the English with force. On the way back Brother Kühnel visited Baas Teunis, and stayed the night at his house. He told him that lately a commando of 25 men had been on the way to put an end to our school; but meeting Holzhuizen with a letter from the government to the rebels, they turned home again [18]. But they said to him that the school must not go on existing any longer, because rogues and thieves lived there. On 22 July towards evening Brother Kühnel came home again, and brought no gladdening news.

<!-- lines 10616-10646 -->
On the 24th in the morning we received a letter from Pieter Dohi, in which he reports that he wished to be able to speak with [one of us], because one could not entrust everything to the pen [19]. This moved Brother Schwinn to go there. At his place too some Nationals had been, who had at once begun to speak of the Herrnhuters, saying "that they wondered very much why the Company had sent teachers for the Hottentots, and no schoolmasters for their children?" Pieter Dohi tried to quieten them, telling them: "The Company did not send the Herrnhuters; that was done by their brethren at their own cost, for the Company gives nothing towards it." They also made mention of more other grievances, which, however, are too long-winded to bring in here. For the rest, Pieter Dohi thought that there were still decent people among them, although still much prejudiced against the Herrnhuters. So many things could yet change, and become more bearable for us than we could now foresee. On 25 July we thought it good to partake of Holy Communion among us 3 alone. Today we wrote several letters to our good friends at the Cape, having heard that some of them are acquainted with the Commandant of the Nationals. Pieter Dohi undertook to see to these letters.

<!-- lines 10647-10709 -->
On the 29th we saw two riders coming towards us; it was Holzhuizer and Hendrik Weltsel, who are our good friends, and relieved us of all anxiety. When they came into the house they were quite silent at first, and to the question "What news is going about?" they answered: "Not much good. We come to bring you the order of Commandant Pisani, that within 3 days you are to leave your place and go either to the Cape or to another burgher's place, if you want to escape bad consequences." How deeply this news struck our hearts we cannot describe. We asked: why? and what harm had we done? They answered that they knew nothing further than that a commando of 8 men had come to them with orders to deliver this order to us. Commandant Pisani is marching with 800 men on the far side of the mountains to the Cape [20]. Not knowing what we could do in this haste, or where we should put our goods, the rivers being very swollen too, Brother Kühnel rode that same night to Baas Teunis to take counsel with him. He knew no other advice than to obey the order, since force goes above right. He himself was not sure that the same lot would not befall him any day. "But as for your goods, I will send a wagon tomorrow to take them into safe keeping at my place. I do not know what may happen to me; but where my goods stay, there yours shall stay too." This message soon became known among the Hottentots, who came to us, great and small, with tear-stained cheeks, and helped us pack. On the far side of the water we had two wagons standing, which the Hottentots were busy pulling across to this side of the water with thongs [21]. Whoever had an ox brought it at once. Brother Kühnel came back in the night from Baas Teunis, whose promised wagon came very opportunely next morning, 30 July, since not much could be loaded on our small wagons. So now we had three. The Hottentots lay round our house all day, lamenting and crying without stopping. Towards evening we had them come together outside in front of our house; for in the house there was not room enough for them. The sorrow was too great for much to be said [beyond admonishing them] to think over diligently what they had heard from us. At the end of the address Brother Kühnel offered one more prayer on his knees [22], and so for the last time we lay with these sheep at the feet of Jesus, imploring him fervently not to leave them or forsake them, but to have mercy on them and to deliver them from this present distress at the time he has appointed. The tears that were shed on this occasion God will surely have counted; for to a man they were numberless.

<!-- lines 10710-10739 -->
On 31 July our 3 wagons drove to Baas Teunis. For our journey to the Cape, and to take with us what we could not do without of our things, we made use of our two carts. The Hottentots provided us with 20 oxen, of which 10 were inspanned and 10 sent ahead to change over on the way. Early in the morning the Hottentots were already gathered round our house. Many of them wanted at all costs to go with us, and we had a great deal of work to talk them out of it. They said: "We are to blame that this is happening to our teachers; we have not been thankful and obedient enough; that is why they are now being taken from us. O God, forgive us our sins!" We charged some of the baptised to stay in our house and look after the garden. So we left our Hottentot congregation and many hopeful sheep, amid the shedding of a thousand tears on both sides. Many ran after us for the distance of an hour's walk, with a constant cry of lament [23]. That was a day for us that remains beyond description. Yet with all this painful feeling we must say that our hearts were filled with a trusting faith that the Saviour will complete his work begun, and carry away praise and honour for his help through.

<!-- lines 10740-10759 -->
When we received the order to leave our place on 29 July, the watchword was: "He hath made his wonderful works to be remembered: the Lord is gracious and full of compassion"; and the text: "I am the Christ, the Son of the Blessed, and ye shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven." On 30 July, when we took leave of our people, the watchword was: "I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken, and will strengthen that which was sick, and I will feed them with judgment"; and the text: "Sit thou on my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool." On the day of our departure, 31 July, the watchword was: "I will give thee rest from all thine enemies"; and the text: "I am Jesus, whom thou persecutest" [24].

<!-- lines 10759-10767 -->
Today we drove as far as Pieter Dohi's, who stood quite amazed at this event, and offered to take our cattle to his place [25]. A Hottentot woman offered to give us a wagon in place of our cart, which we accepted with thankfulness.
