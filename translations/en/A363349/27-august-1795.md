<!-- lines 10767-10789 -->
In the night a great crowd of Hottentots came after us, and when we continued our journey on 1 August, many tears were shed again at the leave-taking. Pieter Dohi, who saw this, said: "That must be a hard post for you, to leave these poor people." He rode on horseback with us to bring us across the river, and advised us to spend the night with his brother-in-law Jan Dohi, which we also did. We had real conversations with him, since he is truly concerned to be saved [1]. On 2 August we crossed the Palmiet River, not without danger, for it was much swollen; and since the next river we had to cross was still [more dangerous, we had to spend the night at Mr Morgel's] [2]. On 3 August the water in the rivers had gone down, and late in the evening we arrived at our friend Martin Schmidt's at the Cape. This family was filled with astonishment when they saw all three of us arrive, and asked at once how this came about, and were not a little dismayed when we made the cause of it known to them.

<!-- lines 10790-10814 -->
On 4 August we went to the Edle Heer Commissioner [3], to tell him what had happened to us and ask him for good advice. He wondered exceedingly at the presumption of Bisani; but he said that we had done well to obey his order. For these people were filled with fury, and were committing harmful violence everywhere; we should for the present just stay here a while and wait for what followed. After that we visited more gentlemen of the government, who all showed much sympathy with what had befallen us. They could not wonder enough that this Bisani had found so many followers, being in his own person a scoundrel who had deserved the gallows more than once. The matter soon became known among our friends, and all showed great sympathy, but also a good confidence that everything would order and change itself to the glory of God. Since Mr Schmidt had a heavy billeting of soldiers, Mr Schönberg took us into his house this time.

<!-- lines 10814-10846 -->
When we had gone out on 5 August, a slave came running after us with a message from his master that we might come to him at his house. We went, and found that it was one of the Nationals, indeed, to all appearances, a commander among them, named Van der Wad, who was charged to enter into negotiation with the gentlemen of the government [4]. He wanted to hear and learn from ourselves from whom we had received the order to leave. When we named Bisani to him, he said: "We do not acknowledge this Bisani as a commander among us. Now we are spoken to here at the Cape by so many, and taken for men who had driven you from Baviaanskloof; but we are not guilty of it at all. So this very morning we held a meeting about your case first thing, and I am charged to ride today to the Landdrost at Swellendam, and to hand over letters concerning you. I assure you on my word of honour that you will be able to go back to your place without having anything to fear." This gentleman is really going to Swellendam to fetch a couple of hundred more men from there. For the real Commandant of the Nationals, named La Bort, stands with his people close to the Cape, having been persuaded first to help drive away the enemy, after they had been promised that attention could then be paid to their proposals and interests [5].

<!-- lines 10847-10868 -->
On 6 August we told the Commissioner what the Nationals' commander had said and promised us, and he said to us that we might stay here a little longer, while he meant to inform himself more closely about it and send for us again. This happened on the 7th, in the evening [6]. He said that we could go back home in peace, and he would give us a letter to take with us. That was indescribably joyful news for us. But now we were at a loss how to make our journey back, since we had sent our wagon back directly after our arrival at the Cape. But behold, 3 wagons of Pieter Dohi's laden with barley [had just arrived, and were to leave again this] evening [7]. This fine opportunity was indeed rather sudden for us, since we still had much to see to; but we decided to make use of it.

<!-- lines 10868-10889 -->
Until now everything had stayed quiet at the Cape. But on Monday at 3 o'clock a signal shot was heard, and shortly after it 4 more shots [8]. These made known that the English and the burghers were in battle, and that the latter were in distress and needed help. The action took place at Muizenberg, 3 long hours from the Cape. The consternation in the town was indescribable. The drums beat the march; the blood flag flew on the Castle; the burghers took up arms, and the women fled into the country. After 4 o'clock the cavalry rode out of the town towards Muizenberg, followed by the soldiers in the Castle. The burghers went to the batteries outside the town, with the Edle Heer on horseback at their head. In the midst of this uproar we drove out of the town with our wagon [9]. On 8 August we heard heavy firing at Muizenberg still.

<!-- lines 10890-10921 -->
On the 9th we came to Jan Dohi's, and on the 10th to Pieter Dohi's, to whom the 3 wagons belonged [10]. From here the Brothers Marsveld and Kühnel rode to Baviaanskloof, where Brother Schwinn also arrived on the 12th with the goods loaded on them. The joy of the Hottentots at seeing us again was indescribable. They said: "How shall we ever thank the Saviour enough that he has given us back our teachers!" They offered at once to have our things fetched from Baas Teunis's with their oxen. This happened on 13 August, which important day we spent entirely in quiet, partly because many of our baptised and communicants were not at home, and partly because we first wanted to find out how our Hottentots had conducted themselves in our absence. On the whole our hearts were filled with praise and thanks that we were here again, and we could not wonder enough at the mighty hand of God that had led us so graciously. No one could have thought or believed that within 3 days we would get our freedom back to make our journey home [11], and still less that there would be a possibility of making that journey, since all oxen and wagons had been seized to carry cannon and ammunition; and what shall one say to this, that in spite of all this our wagons were let go on their way free and unhindered?

<!-- lines 10922-10941 -->
On 16 August we began to hold our usual meetings again. We saw that many were missing; but they came back again little by little. We had been away only 12 days, and yet the enemy has succeeded in leading some of our Hottentots astray, so that we were obliged to exclude 2 baptised persons and one candidate for baptism. On 17 August we learned that our communicant sister Sara [12], who had taken work with a farmer, was very ill. Brother Marsveld rode there, and found her in a gladdening state of heart. She said: "I can believe that the Saviour will forgive me my sins and take me to himself into eternity." On the 19th we received the news of her blessed passing into eternity. Her daughter Martha saw to it that her body was brought here, and we helped her son, who was not unskilled in working wood, to make a coffin.

<!-- lines 10942-10967 -->
On the 20th this Sara was buried. She was the first who came to us, when we were still living at the post with Baas Teunis and had no house of our own, and showed a longing to be saved. [She was received among the candidates for bapt]ism [13], was baptised on 13 August of the same year, and on 17 May 1794 was made a partaker of Holy Communion. She often said: "How shall I ever thank the Saviour enough for all the grace that he shows me!" Over the conversion of her daughter Martha she felt a deep joy, and often uttered the wish that her other 3 children might be as happy too. Our flight went deep into her heart, and she said: "Oh, if only no harm may be done to our teachers!" [14] After the meeting for the funeral we arranged our Hottentots behind the body, which was carried by 6 men. The children of the departed were first in the procession; then came the baptised, and behind them the candidates for baptism, two by two. We went in front, and held the burial liturgy shown as No. 2 in our liturgy book [15] at the place where the body was committed to the earth.

<!-- lines 10968-10980 -->
On 25 August we heard that Commandant Bisani, who had driven us from Baviaanskloof, is sitting in prison at the Cape with his secretary and adjutant. On the 29th we celebrated our choir festival in a blessed way, with a solemn morning blessing, the reading of a choir address, and the partaking of Holy Communion [16]. We were in spirit in the congregation, and wished very much to receive news from their midst, for which, however, there seems to be no possibility at present.
