<!-- lines 16454-16463 -->
On 1 Sept. a woman and a young girl received the bath of Holy Baptism. The 6th was again a day of praise and thanks for us, because we received letters once more, namely from Brothers Reichel, Latrobe and Wollin, together with some reports [1]. By the fatherly and loving encouragement of our dear Brother Reichel we were strengthened and fired anew to carry on the work of the Lord, comforted by his grace.

<!-- lines 16464-16475 -->
For a good while we had had a carpenter from the Cape at work, who is a slave; now we had to see to it that he could get home again. Since nobody would take him along, we hired a wagon for him, and decided that Brother Schwinn should travel with him to buy one thing and another that we cannot get here. He left on the 12th, and took our diary with him, in the hope of finding an opportunity to send it on to Europe.

<!-- lines 16476-16482 -->
Our little congregation gathered from among the Hottentots consists in this year 1796 of 12 communicants; 8 who are being prepared for Communion; 22 baptised who do not yet go to Communion; 8 baptised children; 26 who are being prepared for baptism; so of 76 souls [2].

<!-- lines 16483-16483 -->
The diary goes on as follows [3].

<!-- lines 16484-16497 -->
Taking leave of us cost the carpenter many tears. He had always been much moved in the meetings; he also often spoke with us about the state of his heart, declaring that he wished for nothing more than to enjoy the great good fortune that had fallen to the Hottentots, namely: to be allowed to hear God's word. He was gifted with a good memory, and during his stay among us of 8 months had learned many hymns and their tunes well.

<!-- lines 16498-16499 -->
On the 20th Brother Schwinn came back from the Cape, and reported the following of his journey.

<!-- lines 16500-16520 -->
"On the 14th I arrived safely at our friend Schmidt's, who gave me loving hospitality. On the 15th and 16th I did my business, visiting among others the Fiscal Reinveld [4]. This gentleman asked at once how things went with us and our Hottentots? I answered that we could not thank the Saviour enough for the grace he showed to us and our people: but that on the other hand it grieved us very much that this poor people were so oppressed by the farmers over their grazing. He advised me to go to the General; and he himself would try to find an opportunity to speak with that gentleman about it too. I followed his advice. But as the General understands neither German nor Dutch, and makes use of an interpreter who, as I have heard from many and found myself, is very hostile to us, I am not sure whether he translated all my words honestly. [...] [5]

<!-- lines 16523-16552 -->
"[I] put it to him that the farmers had taken the land from the Hottentots, and that their cattle now could not possibly find enough grazing in the small area that had been left to them. The General's answer was that the Hottentots must not go too far, nor think that they must get back everything that their forefathers had possessed. I had not said a word about forefathers; so I gave him to understand that the Hottentots now living were the owners of the land, and that the government had indeed taken the land for itself, but on condition that the Hottentots with their cattle might not be driven off. The General answered to this that he had learned just the opposite from the many complaints, and that it was the Hottentots who oppressed the farmers. I answered this by saying that I well believed there would be no lack of complaints; but if the matter were once examined closely, the truth of what I said would come to light. The General said to this that the Landdrost of Stellenbosch would shortly come to us and examine the matter; and charged me to tell my brothers that he was glad to have spoken with one of us, and that we had every protection to expect from him.

<!-- lines 16553-16561 -->
"From here I went back to the Fiscal, to whom I gave an account of what had been said. Upon this he asked us to let him know at once if anyone should want to do us any harm, and assured me that he would help us as far as it lay in his power.

<!-- lines 16562-16581 -->
"On the 17th I travelled back, and in the evening arrived at our friend Mr Däß's in Stellenbosch, who received me lovingly and, with his family, entered into pleasant, heartfelt conversations [6]. Here I also visited the minister, who was formerly a great enemy of us and our cause, but had been brought to other thoughts by reading the Greenland history and the *Idea fidei Fratrum*, which Mr Däß had lent him [7]. He began to speak of it at once, and declared: 'I have been a still greater persecutor of you than you could have thought; because I had read the old libels written against you, and had never examined the matter itself.' He asked very much for forgiveness, declaring his joy over the work of God among the Hottentots, and at the same time offering his help as far as it lay in his power [8].

<!-- lines 16582-16584 -->
"After a hard journey, especially because of cold and rain, I arrived on the 20th with my dear brothers."

<!-- lines 16585-16621 -->
On the 21st, early in the morning, a mounted orderly came to us from the Landdrost with the order that we should let all the Hottentots come to him at once: for he wanted to examine the many complaints between the farmer Gildenhuijs and the Hottentots [9]. So we sent them all; and when they had come back, they told us that the Landdrost had treated them very kindly. They were ordered to put forward their case in the presence of that farmer, and he wrote it down in the main points. Our people held up before the farmer his dreadful treatment of them; how he had beaten the children, and threatened that if he found them in that place again they would see how the bullets would fly, and all the cattle would be shot dead [...] that he had said: "one had reason to thank God that the French would soon come": for which he got a sharp rebuke from the Landdrost [10]. At first the farmer wanted to deny everything; but he was convicted, so that at last he admitted that it had been done in a temper. Meanwhile he was strictly forbidden to commit any acts of violence from now on; and at the same time the boundaries were fixed, how far the Hottentots might graze with their cattle, and it was settled that after the harvest they should get another piece of veld besides. With this they are now helped somewhat, but not yet fully: for it is the truth, after all, that the whole of the farmer's place belongs entirely to the Hottentots, and that it has been taken from them against all right.

<!-- lines 16622-16653 -->
On the 23rd the Landdrost visited us, together with Mr Morgel, and showed us much kindness. He said that he had examined the known disputes on the General's orders, and had also put in order the matter of Mr Clüde's place [11]. The Hottentots had now got more veld, and boundary stones had now been set everywhere. He read us the document about the boundaries, and asked whether we had anything more to remark on it. We answered that it would now be too late if we had anything to bring against it; that the Hottentots had been given a fairly large but quite barren veld, which consists of nothing but mountains and rocks, and the best grazing had been kept back. He admitted that this was so; but, he added, one could not after all take it away again from those who had bought the places and paid taxes on them every year. Enough, they had given something of it up all the same, and the Hottentots now had more than before. He asked us to call the Hottentots together, so that the document could be read to them too. On taking leave he assured us of his readiness to help us wherever he could, and declared that he would gladly have attended a Sunday meeting, if his business had allowed him.

<!-- lines 16654-16672 -->
On the 25th, just as the meeting was about to begin, a slave woman of Mr Morgel's came, asking that some Hottentots be sent to help her master. Some at once did as she asked, and found the party, 6 in number, in great distress. They had meant to come here to church, and not far from here the horses had bolted, the wagon had overturned and then been dragged a stretch further; so that everything is in pieces. They all arrived here on foot. Mr Morgel was badly hurt and went to bed, but the others attended the service. Then, as we still had the wagon that we had used on our journey to the Cape, we gave it to them for the journey home.

## Notes

1. *Br: Reichel, Latrobe, en Wollin*: see Johann Friedrich Reichel and Latrobe in the glossary; Brother Wollin is not identified.
2. 12 + 8 + 22 + 8 + 26 = 76, which agrees. These figures were written for the diary that Brother Schwinn took to the Cape on 12 September; the figures at the end of the year (entry 58) are higher.
3. *Verder luidt het Dagverhaal, als volgt*: the copyist's words. The diary was evidently sent off in two parts, the first ending with Brother Schwinn's departure on 12 September and the figures above. Line 16485 is empty in the transcription and loses no text.
4. *den Fiscaal Reinfeld*: the Fiscal Reinveld (see Fiscal van Ryneveld in the glossary).
5. Line 16520 is the catchword *ons* at the foot of page 274a, and line 16522, the first line of page 274b, is missing. The next line begins *ſtelde hem voor*, "put it to him". The lost line probably began with *ons* and told how Brother Schwinn spoke to the General on the mission's behalf. The word in square brackets at the start of the next paragraph is supplied from the grammar only.
6. *d' H.r Däß in Stellenboſch*: a friend of the mission at Stellenbosch. Not identified.
7. *de Groenlandſche Hiſtorie en de Idea fidei Fratrum*: David Cranz's history of Greenland and its Moravian mission (1765), and Spangenberg's *Idea fidei fratrum* (see the glossary). The minister of Stellenbosch is not named; in 1796 this was probably Meent Borcherds, minister there from 1786. Not certain.
8. The minister's words have an opening quotation mark but no closing one in the transcription.
9. *den boer Gildenhuijs*: a farmer bordering the mission's grazing. Not identified.
10. Line 16604 is missing. The transcription runs *en al het vee dood geſchooten* / *gen, dat hij gezegd had*. The lost line probably finished the threat (*zou worden*, "would be") and said that the Khoekhoe also accused the farmer (*betuig-den*, *beschuldig-den*) of having said the words that follow. The words in the translation are only those that survive.
11. *den H.r Morgel*: see Morgel in the glossary. *de plaats van den H.r Clude*: Mr Clüde (see entry 65, 8 July).
