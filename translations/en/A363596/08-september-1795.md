<!-- lines 2381-2406 -->
On 1 September we were visited by a Hottentot [1] who had got leave from the Cape to serve with a farmer. He said that all our Hottentots there are still alive. Of these, 4 men arrived here on the 7th, among them our Jacob, having got leave to visit their wives and children for a couple of days. When they left [the Cape], it was known that 15 English ships had arrived off Muizenberg, and the fleet was now said to be 35 ships strong. On the 8th Baas Teunis sent us a letter which he had received from the Edle Heer. It contained the instruction to provide food for all the wives and children of those now serving at the Cape, and to consult with us about it, because we would know best what was needed; and the report was to be made by him and us together. At the same time he had us deliver a letter to Andreas Otto, asking him to bring us a load of flour, which we were to distribute among the people.

<!-- lines 2406-2421 -->
On the 9th we had a pleasant conversation with our Jacob about our people at the Cape. He said that they all come together morning and evening, and he sings a few verses with them. At first [2] the others asked him why he did this. He answered: "In the evening we thank our God that he has kept us from all harm through the day, and at the same time we ask him to keep us through the night as well; in the morning we thank him for keeping us through the night, and commend ourselves to him for the day." To our people he also said: "It is our duty to pray for our teachers, that God will keep them at home, for they pray for us every day."

<!-- lines 2422-2441 -->
On the 10th we had all those called together to whom flour is to be distributed on the instruction of the Edle Heer, and wrote down their names. We counted 42 women and 74 children, 116 heads in all. These are only the dependants of those who are serving at the Cape. Besides these there are many other women here who have no husbands, and many children who have no father; also many [old] and sick men, whose wives and children do not come under the above number. In addition, those who live at Baas Teunis's are also to receive food. Among them are many of those who moved away from here on 18 July; their case, however, will be treated in the same way as that of the people here [3]. On the 11th Brother Kühnel was at Baas Teunis's to arrange the distribution of what the Edle Heer has assigned for this purpose.

<!-- lines 2441-2455 -->
Since 3 July we had not had Communion with our Hottentot communicants. Since many of them had long shown a great longing for it, and our Jacob, whose walk gives us much joy, cannot wait for the proper [Communion day] [4], because he must soon go back to the Cape, we decided to celebrate this sacrament on the 12th. All hearts were in tears when this was announced to them, and Jacob especially was very thankful. A special peace of God accompanied this partaking.

<!-- lines 2455-2482 -->
On the 14th Baas Teunis came to us. He brought the written order from the Edle Heer to send to the Cape every Hottentot who could be found. Those here all had to come together, and so another 10 were picked out, and also the 2 who had got leave to stay here. Among them was our Abraham. Now of our baptised only David, who was ill, was left at home. We were, however, allowed to keep our farmhand. Mounted messengers have been sent everywhere to raise men; for the Cape is to be contested against the English with all possible force. On the 15th those who had been ordered left here, among them our 2 brothers Jacob and Abraham. We gave them proper admonitions for the road. Now everyone who can fight has gone from here. Baas Teunis sent us a copy of the summons of the English commander-in-chief, in which he announces the [reinforcement] [5] he has received and demands the peaceful surrender of the town; and at the same time the answer, that the town would not be surrendered, and that no letter from him would be accepted.

<!-- lines 2482-2513 -->
All the more unexpected was it for us, early on the 18th, to see 4 Hottentots arrive from the Cape, who told us that the town had surrendered to the English, that all the Hottentots had been discharged, and that each had been paid a rixdollar. At midday 18 men came all at once, and then always 2, 3 or 4 together. Among them was our Isaac too. All agreed that none of our people had been killed in the fighting. By their account the English advanced from Muizenberg towards the Cape on the 14th; after some cannon shots the burghers fell back close to the Cape; on the 15th the Hottentots marched into Cape Town, the farmers for the most part went home, and the Hottentots were discharged. Now, as far as we know, we are under English rule. A messenger from the Edle Heer to the Landdrost of Swellendam [6], who passed through here on the 19th, brought us the same news. Today many more Hottentots came home, and on the 20th [very many] came [7], so that there were more in the [meeting] than we had ever yet seen here. For many others came too, who had not lived here before, and asked leave to move here with their families. We told them to wait a little, because we had only just got new authorities and must first learn how they were disposed.

<!-- lines 2514-2531 -->
The meeting had to be held in front of the house, because not a quarter of the people would have had room inside. There was a great stir among them. Many said that they could not thank God enough for keeping them safe: often in a fight it had been as if it were raining bullets, and they had fallen among them like sand and yet done them no harm. A cannon ball had passed between one man's legs. For the rest, the Hottentots are praised on all sides as faithful soldiers. They had to go in front everywhere, and the burghers and farmers followed behind at a well-chosen distance. On the 22nd the little son of our [Mr] Bedgen [8] was baptised with the name Christian. On the 23rd our Jacob came back from the Cape too; he had not been able to come sooner because of a commission from Baas Teunis.

<!-- lines 2531-2557 -->
On the 24th we received through Baas Teunis a letter from the English commander, in which the inhabitants were released from their duty to the Company, with the assurance that their welfare would be cared for and that everyone would be heard with his complaints. This letter was signed by Major-General Craig [9]. At the same time we received the 13 articles of capitulation, among which was this one, that all religious services shall remain undisturbed. They were signed by General Clarke. In the following days many farmers were with us to hire Hottentots. We closed the month with the heartfelt wish that a way of communication with our dear Brethren might soon be opened for us; for we have much to report and would like to hear much too. For some time now we have had peace from the [party of] war, or the Nationals; for they were quite dismayed at the changes that had taken place and seem to have given up their cause. Yet one still hears a threat here and there.

## Notes

1. The transcription reads *Gottentott*, a misreading of *Hottentott*, as in the eight lines of A363595 mentioned in the README.
2. The transcription reads *Im Auftrag*, "on commission", which makes no sense here; probably *Im Anfang*, "at first".
3. The transcription reads *d. ihre Sache wird aber ſo grade ſeyn als der hieſigen*; the normalised column has "gerecht". The sense is probably that they will be treated the same as those at the mission.
4. The transcription reads *den eigentlichen Charfreitag*, "the proper Good Friday", which makes no sense in September; probably a misreading of a word for the regular Communion day. To be checked against the image.
5. The transcription reads *Einſchließung*, "encirclement", but the relative clause (*die er erhalten*, "which he has received") suggests *Verſtärkung*, "reinforcement". The British reinforcements under General Alured Clarke arrived in early September 1795. To be checked against the image.
6. The transcription reads *Ein Bote vom Edlen Herrn, dem Landdroſt von Schwellendam*; the normalised column makes the Landdrost the sender. The English reads the messenger as going to Swellendam. To be checked against the image.
7. The transcription reads *am 20ten ſtellten nur noch ſehr wenige, ſo daß ſoviele in der Gnad. waren, als wir noch nicht hier geſehen hatten*: "on the 20th only very few presented themselves, so that as many were in grace as we had not yet seen here". This contradicts itself; the English follows the probable sense. The 20th was a Sunday. To be checked against the image.
8. *unſers Hrn. Bedgen*: perhaps a misreading. See entry 06, note 2, on Sister Bodgen.
9. Major-General James Henry Craig, commander of the British land forces, who became commandant of the Cape; General Alured Clarke commanded the reinforcements. The Cape surrendered on 16 September 1795.
