<!-- lines 3177-3198 -->
On 6 February we had Holy Communion, at which Louise looked on as a confirmand. On the 8th one half of the roof rafters was put up, and everything went well; but on the 11th a great accident could easily have happened. While walling at the gables, one of the masons stepped too far out onto the end of a plank, and it was just about to tip over with him when he was seized by the other mason and a Hottentot and saved. We thanked the Saviour from our hearts for this protection. On the 13th we finished the brick-making, since we now thought we had enough. On the 14th Rosina, who had several times shown herself very repentant of her offence [1], was readmitted, and this was announced in the meeting as a warning.

<!-- lines 3198-3249 -->
Today a Hottentot told us that Baas Teunis had told him that the farmers of the Unterland would come this very week and make an end of Baviaanskloof. We have often heard such talk in recent times and taken no notice of it. It is certain that Satan is raging anew, and Baas Teunis is one of his tools. Not long ago, with many curses, he told the Hottentots that not one of them should set foot in the new house. We meant to pay no further attention to such talk this time either; but at midday an express messenger came to us from Baas Teunis with the spoken message that a commando of farmers would come to us tomorrow or the day after; he believed they would come in the night; whether they meant to destroy Baviaanskloof or not, he did not know. How we felt at this message we cannot describe. The poor Hottentots too were seized with great terror. Last month they had been with the English General at the Cape, who gave them a letter to the Landdrost at Stellenbosch; and the Landdrost in turn gave them a letter which they were to show to the farmers if they ever came. We have this letter in safe keeping, and it is favourable enough to us; but since these farmers are in revolt against the English government and intend to starve out Cape Town, we cannot count on it any further. Not long ago the Landdrost had told the Hottentots that as soon as they heard the farmers were really on the way, they should send him word quickly. So today most of them set off, some on horseback, some on foot, for Stellenbosch, and some of them to go on from there to the Cape. Many asked us what they should do, but we did not know what to advise them. As for ourselves, we had nothing to do but look to the Saviour's help, for it is his work after all.

<!-- lines 3249-3271 -->
The 15th and 16th passed quietly, and no farmers appeared. On the 17th we even heard that Baas Teunis had told a Hottentot that the whole thing was not true, and that he would very much wish the Hottentots had not run off at once to the Landdrost and to the Cape [2]. On the 19th young Teunis came and said that today a mounted orderly had come to his father with a sharp letter from the Landdrost about the Hottentots' complaints: the General had ordered that every act of violence done to the Hottentots should be severely punished; and his father had also received a sharp reprimand for not having reported the farmers' plan. He now asked that one or two of us should come to him tomorrow, because he must speak with us urgently.

<!-- lines 3271-3326 -->
So on the 20th the Brethren Marsveld and Kühnel rode over to him. He had also sent for the field corporal Otto. When we arrived, he took us and the corporal into a separate room, asked us what confusion the Hottentots' running to Stellenbosch and the Cape had caused, read us the letters he had received, and said there was no truth in the whole matter. He entirely denied that he had sent us that message; he would send for the same Hottentot and have him taken bound to Stellenbosch. We answered that not only this one but several Hottentots had heard such words from him, which he could not deny, but tried to excuse. He said the General meant to send a strong commando of soldiers to Stellenbosch, but he had written back that it was all lies. Then he said: "It is indeed true that a commando of 100 farmers means to come to you, and I expect them here today; but they will do you no harm; they only want to seek out the Hottentots who are in their debt and must work for them for it, and drive them along before them." We answered: "So it is the truth after all, that they mean to come; and is it not violence if they seize the Hottentots and drive them along in front of their horses?" He promised to do his utmost to keep them away from us; and we believe he will do it, for he has to answer for it to the government. The Hottentots' debts, which are now taken as a pretext for driving them off, come mostly from this: the men have had to do military service at the Cape for so long, and during that time their wives have had to borrow from the farmers to keep themselves and their children alive. The Landdrost has also written to us that we should urge the Hottentots to work off their debts with the farmers; we do not fail to admonish them, and many do what they can, but it cannot all be done at once. The two brothers came home again the same day.

<!-- lines 3326-3368 -->
On the 21st a Hottentot came running from the post and said that the commando would come tomorrow. He had seen 18 out hunting, and 30 were at another place an hour away, all armed. At another place our mason had spoken with 12 men. Until now people had always spoken of farmers of the Unterland; but now it turns out that it is the farmers of this district, and among them some who live not far from us. Not many were at the meeting today, because most of the women had fled into the mountains with their children for fear of the farmers. When the Brethren Schwinn and Kühnel visited in the kraals in the afternoon, they found at a Bastard's a farmer who had been with the commando and had just come from Baas Teunis. He said that when he heard the letter, he rode away with 3 others and would have nothing to do with the matter. In the evening the same Bastard came to us and said he meant to ride to the General tomorrow to give him exact news of all the circumstances, and asked our advice. We thought this would be quite good, for Baas Teunis will hardly report the truth to the government. On the 22nd some of the farmers' commando, who had probably thought better of it, came back from Baas Teunis and rode quietly past half an hour from us. We wondered why the Hottentots had not yet come back from the Cape, and on the 25th Brother Marsveld rode to Wolfram's to get news of them, but could learn nothing.

<!-- lines 3368-3408 -->
At Otto's he heard that the rebellious farmers mean to draw up a paper about the Hottentots, to be laid before the General. There are said to be still 150 of them. On the 26th the Hottentots passed through here on their way to the Cape [3]. They had served with the first commandant of the farmers, but had run away out of fear. They said that they had had to cast many small bullets for their master. On the 28th we kept the memorial day of our moving into our house 3 years ago, and thought over the many times our dear Lord has helped us through since then. On the 29th all the Hottentots came back from the Cape at last. The General had listened to them very kindly and promised them that the farmers should do them no harm; they should go to Stellenbosch meanwhile, until the matter had settled down again. They had done so and found work there. They brought letters from the Landdrost to 6 field corporals, and one to us too, in which he writes that we should admonish the Hottentots to be patient; further, that the General intends to raise a corps of soldiers of 3 to 400 young Bastards, slaves, but otherwise volunteer Hottentots [4]; and he asked us to help with it. The General had told the Hottentots this himself, adding: "It is for the protection of your own nation." That was a hard point for us, but we must keep silent about it.

<!-- lines 3408-3431 -->
On 2 March Louise was confirmed in the Communion meeting, and on the 5th she partook of Holy Communion with us. In these days the proposal was made to thatch the roof; but we have only one man who understands this work. On the 6th Pieter Dohi visited us. He too had been called to the meeting of the rebellious farmers, but would have nothing to do with the matter; however, to learn what went on there, he sent his brother. But the meeting did not take place; why, he did not know. He told us in confidence that the farmers' intention had been to ask us whether we would leave the Hottentots and go back to the fatherland; and if we refused, to shoot us dead at once; then they would take the young Hottentots with them and shoot the old ones dead as well.

<!-- lines 3431-3458 -->
On the 7th we heard that all the field corporals in this district have been summoned to the Cape. On the 13th we had a visit from several farmers, among them young Teunis, who after the meeting started an angry row with the Hottentots, because they had gone to the General and told him lies about his words. There are some among the Hottentots who can answer well, and the quarrel grew so heated that we had to step in. He then announced, as he said on the General's instructions, that the Hottentots must pay their debts to the farmers or work for them; and further, that the General intends to raise a corps of soldiers from young Hottentots. A gentleman from the Cape was present, who told him that he had behaved badly in the quarrel with the Hottentots, and that if it came to a complaint he might come off badly. In the end he admitted it himself, and the parting was fairly friendly after all.

## Notes

1. The transcription reads *über ihre Vergnügung*, "over her pleasure", probably a misreading of *Vergehung*, "offence". Rosina was probably one of the women baptised on 17 May 1795 (entry 04).
2. *daß er ſehr wünſchen würde, daß die Hottentotten gleich zum Landdroſt, und nach der Cap gelaufen wären*: literally "that he would very much wish that the Hottentots had run at once to the Landdrost and to the Cape". The sense requires a negative ("had not run"), which may have been lost in the transcription. To be checked against the image.
3. "The Hottentots": probably a different group from those who had gone to the Cape on the 14th, since the next sentences describe men who had served with the farmers' commandant, and the others return on the 29th.
4. *ein Soldaten⸗Corps von 3 bis 400 jungen Baſtaarden, Sklaven, übrigens aber freywilligen, Hottentotten*: the list is unclear; the sense may be "of young Bastards and Hottentots, all volunteers". The British raised a corps of Khoekhoe soldiers at the Cape in 1796. To be checked against the image.
