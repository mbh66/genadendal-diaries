<!-- lines 2178-2198 -->
Pieter Dohi, who rode with us to bring us through the river, said when he saw the leave-taking with the Hottentots: "It must surely be hard to leave these poor people." He advised us to spend the night with his brother-in-law Jan Dohi, which we did, and we had a real conversation with him, since he has truly been converted from [his] hostility [1]. On 2 August we crossed the Palmiet River [2], which was much swollen, not without danger, and because the [...] river [3] was still more dangerous to cross, we had to spend the night at Mr Morgel's [4]. On the 3rd the water [had gone down] [5], and late in the evening we arrived at our friend Mart. Schmidt's at the Cape. This family was full of astonishment to see all three of us arrive, and asked at once what this meant. They were not a little dismayed when we told them the circumstances.

<!-- lines 2199-2219 -->
On the 4th we went first to the Edle Heer Commissioner [6], told him how things had gone with us, and asked his advice. He was much surprised at Bisani's presumption, but said we had done well to obey the order, for those people were full of fury and had done much damage. He said we should simply stay for a time now and wait for what followed. We then visited our gentlemen of the government, who all showed us much sympathy. They could not wonder enough that Bisani should have been given such a commission, since he had deserved the gallows more than once. The matter soon became known everywhere among our friends, and all showed much sympathy, but also confidence that everything would yet change to the glory of God. Because Mr Schmidt had so many soldiers quartered on him, we lodged this time with Mr Schönberg.

<!-- lines 2219-2251 -->
When we went out on the 5th, a slave came running after us and said his master asked us to come to him. We went and found that it was one of the Nationals (who were here to negotiate with the government [...]) [7]; how astonished we were to see one of those people, named Van der Wad [8]. He wanted to hear from us ourselves from whom we had received the order to leave. When he heard of Bisani, he said: "This Bisani we do not recognise as a commander among us. Here at the Cape so much evil is said of us, as if we had committed a crime, and yet we are quite innocent. This morning we held a meeting about your case, and today I must still ride to the Landdrost at Swellendam with letters that concern you. I assure you on my honour that you can go back to your place and that nothing shall happen to you." This gentleman is going to Swellendam to fetch a few hundred more men from there. For the real commandant of the Nationals, named La Bort [9], is here near the Cape with his people. They have been [allowed to come] because of the enemy, and have been promised that their case will be dealt with afterwards [10]; they had now fought against the English.

<!-- lines 2251-2270 -->
On the 6th we reported the above to the Edle Heer Commissioner. He said we should wait a little longer; he would inquire more closely and let us know. This happened early on the 7th. He said he believed we could now go home again in peace, and he would give us a letter to take with us. This was now for us the most [welcome] and precious news [11]. But we were at a loss how to get away now, for we had sent our wagons back at once. It so happened that 3 wagons of Pieter Dohi's had just arrived with barley and were to go back after this evening. The opportunity was rather sudden for us, as we still had much business to see to; but we decided to use it.

<!-- lines 2270-2291 -->
Until now it was still quiet at the Cape. But in the afternoon at 3 o'clock we heard a signal shot, and soon afterwards 4 more. This was the sign that the English [had attacked] and [our people] were in distress, and [all] must give help [12]. The action was at Muizenberg [13], 3 good hours from the Cape. The consternation in the town was indescribable. The drums beat the march, the blood flag flew on the Castle, the burghers all took up arms, and the women fled into the country. Before 4 o'clock the cavalry rode out of the town towards Muizenberg. They were followed by the soldiers of the Castle, then the Cape burghers, who went into the batteries in front of the town, and the Edle Heer himself went with them on horseback. In the midst of this tumult we pushed out of the town and got as far as the Doual [14], where we stayed the night.

<!-- lines 2291-2323 -->
On the 8th we heard heavy firing at Muizenberg again. On the 9th we came to Jan Dohi's and on the 10th to Pieter Dohi's [15], where the wagons were [unloaded] [16]. From here the Brethren Marsveld and Kühnel rode home on the 11th, and Brother Schwinn followed on the 12th with the oxen. The joy [our people] had [17] at seeing us come back was indescribable. They said: "Oh, how shall we thank the dear Saviour enough that he has given us back our teachers!" They at once offered oxen to fetch our things back from Baas Teunis. This was done on 13 August. This time we kept this important memorial day [18] only in quiet, partly because several of our baptised and communicants were not at home, and because in general we first wanted to see how things had gone among them during our absence. For the rest, we were full of praise and thanks that we were here again, and could not wonder enough at the mighty hand of God that had led us so. No one would have thought or believed that within 13 days we would get leave to go back, still less that we would find an opportunity for it so soon; for all the wagons and oxen that could be had had been seized to carry cannon and ammunition, and it was a wonder that our wagons were allowed to go.

<!-- lines 2323-2338 -->
On Thursday the 16th [19] we began the meetings again in their usual order. We saw that many were missing, but they came back little by little. We had been away only 12 days, and in that short time the enemy had succeeded in leading some astray, so that we had to exclude 2 baptised persons and a candidate, and gave the others a reprimand. On the 17th we learned that our Sara [20], who was working for a farmer, was ill. Brother Marsveld rode there and found her in an edifying state of heart. She said: "I can believe that the Saviour has forgiven me my sins and will take me to himself."

<!-- lines 2338-2369 -->
Early on the 19th we received news of her going home. Her daughter Martha saw to it that the body was brought here, and we had her son, who can work with wood, helped to make a coffin. On the 20th was the funeral. This Sara [21] was the first who came to us when we still lived at the place near Baas Teunis, [...] and testified to a longing to be saved. On 14 June 1795 [22] she became a candidate for baptism, on 13 August of the same year she was baptised, and on 17 May 1794 she came to Holy Communion. She often said: "Oh, how shall I thank the dear Saviour enough for all the grace he shows me!" She had heartfelt joy over the conversion of her daughter Martha, and often expressed her wish that her other children might be as well off. She was much troubled about our flight and said: "Oh, if only they would do nothing to our teachers! They might do with us what they like." After the address for the funeral we set the people in order in front of the house behind the coffin [?], which was carried by [...] [23]: the children of the departed went first, then the baptised, and behind them the candidates for baptism, all two by two. We went in front and held the burial according to no. 62 of the little book of liturgies [24].

<!-- lines 2371-2381 -->
On the 25th we heard that Commandant Pisani, who had driven us away, is sitting in prison at the Cape with a secretary and adjutant. On the 29th we kept our choir festival [25] very pleasantly and blessedly, with a fine lovefeast, the reading of a choir address [26], and the partaking of Holy Communion. Today we were in spirit especially often in the congregations [27], and wished with all our hearts to have news of them soon. But at present we saw no possibility of it.

19. 16 August 1795 was a Sunday, not a Thursday; 13 August was a Thursday. Either the weekday or the date is wrong, in the manuscript or the transcription. To be checked against the image.
