<!-- lines 1445-1464 -->
On 3 June a family moved here from about 20 miles away, to hear God's word. We also heard of a man in another district who would like to move here, but whom the friend [he works for] will not let go. In that district the farmers are said to be very concerned that the Hottentots should be instructed. We were told as the truth that a commando of farmers from the Strandveld [1] would come here to take us away and send us to Batavia, by which means they thought they would soon be done with the Hottentots. When such rumours come, the Hottentots are surprised [2] that we are not afraid; but we tell them that nothing can happen to us without God's will, and so we are not anxious. The Hottentots, however, are no longer quite so fearful as at the beginning.

<!-- lines 1464-1481 -->
On the 8th Jacob went to the farm. Baas Teunis took him to task for not having forbidden the Hottentots to tell Mr Claude that his son had impounded cattle of theirs [3]; now he had to leave the place. Jacob answered: "We are taught that we should not lie but speak the truth." Baas Teunis replied: "I know very well that you are taught to speak the truth; but you should not have said this all the same, for now I must leave the place and have got into trouble with the authorities," and so on. On the 9th the youngest child of our Martha was baptised with the name Maria, at the mother's repeated request.

<!-- lines 1481-1506 -->
On the 13th we heard 4 cannon shots, which we took for signal shots, as was confirmed the next day. Such a signal means that all Hottentots who can bear arms must go to the Cape without delay. Ours seemed to have no wish to go; so we thought it necessary to remind them in the meeting that it was their duty to obey the command of the authorities. Straight after the meeting Jacob and Isaac set off, and the others soon followed. Abraham and David were ill and stayed at home. Isaac asked whether, when he now came before the enemy, he should shoot him dead. We could answer nothing else than that he must do what he was ordered to do. We also admonished them to commend themselves diligently, body and soul, to the dear Saviour. We received a letter from Baas Teunis that all Hottentots must go to the Cape; but they had all gone already. We are very eager to hear how things look at the Cape. It is generally believed that the French are approaching [4].

<!-- lines 1509-1545 -->
On the 17th Mrs Teunis sent us a letter asking whether we knew anything new from the Cape. But we knew nothing, for at present neither a wagon nor any other opportunity comes from there. On the 19th a tiger [5] was seen not far from us, which had killed 5 young ones [6] belonging to the Hottentots. Some went after it with our guns, but it had already made off. On the 20th [we] learned at last that it was not the French but the English who were off the Cape. On the 21st we heard that a Hottentot from Stellenbosch who was supposed to go to the Cape had come here to [hide] [7]; so we reminded them in the meeting that no one should give shelter to such a person. Soon afterwards Baas Teunis came here and was very friendly. He had received orders to send to the Cape all the Hottentots he could find. So the cripples, the sick and the old were called together in front of our house. We ourselves had not thought that so many were still here, when so many had gone. 35 men came together, of whom he picked out 18 and told them that they must leave very early tomorrow. Then he told us that we were now in a situation that could not be worse: the enemy lay before the Cape, and in the country the farmers were in open revolt against the authorities, which went so far that they were deposing Landdrosts and appointing them. At Swellendam the Landdrost and the secretary had already been deposed. The farmers who did this were 120 in number.

<!-- lines 1546-1562 -->
On the 22nd we again heard many disagreeable rumours. Many farmers who will not side with the English [8] are now fleeing to the Cape, because they are no longer safe at home; for those who will not join in are threatened with death. We did not feel easy, since we knew very well the farmers' feelings towards us and our work among the Hottentots. Some of our people had heard a farmer's wife say: "Soon we shall see Baviaanskloof burning." On the 23rd we heard that the rebels were already on this side of Swellendam and meant, probably, to come to Baviaanskloof and then go on to the Cape. On the 24th the rumours became still more alarming.

<!-- lines 1562-1590 -->
We thought this way and that of a friend who might give us good advice, but could think of no one but Andreas Otto, a field corporal [9], to whose ward we properly belong. He lives 3 miles from here, and the Brethren Marsveld and Kühnel rode to him. Brother Schwinn, who was now here alone, declared that he had never had such a day. One rumour came after another that the rebels were quite near. The baptised said: "Oh, if they should drive our teachers away!" and asked whether we would flee. Brother Schwinn said he did not know; we must hear what his brothers brought back, and then we would see what we had to do. They said they would all go with us wherever we went; if we stayed, they would stay too, and would die with their teachers. The candidates for baptism said the same, and many others. A candidate for baptism said: "Should God, who made heaven and earth with a word, give the rebels this power? I do not believe it. He is surely mighty enough to keep these people away from here; let us only pray to him for it diligently."

<!-- lines 1591-1611 -->
"Towards evening," writes Brother Schwinn, "as it began to grow dark, I was standing in the room thinking over our circumstances, when all at once I saw a crowd of people standing in front of the window who seemed to me to be armed. I was not a little frightened and thought it was the rebels; but when I had collected myself, I saw that they were Hottentots, 12 in number, each with a stick over his shoulder from which a bundle hung. They were some of those from the Snake River [10] who would like to move to us to hear God's word. They had already been with Baas Teunis for a couple of days, who tried to talk them out of their plan; and because he did not succeed, these 12 were ordered to the Cape. Their wives and children are still with Baas Teunis, and he will on no account let them come here; they are to go home again."

<!-- lines 1611-1646 -->
On the 25th at midday the Brethren Marsveld and Kühnel came home again. They reported the following from their journey: "When we came to the farmer Mary, we heard to our great joy that the rebels had turned back and would come no further this time. At Gilderheis's this news was confirmed, for they had letters from that district. At Otto's we learned nothing new, except the certainty that as soon as the rebels came to us our place was to be destroyed; for they themselves had said that this must be their first [task]. We stayed the night with him. Next morning we rode home again. We called in at Gilderheis's again, but as we were about to leave they called us back and said that a messenger was coming from below, straight from the Cape; we should wait a little to hear what he brought. He came from the Cape with letters for them, reporting that the English were preparing to attack the Cape, but that the Cape would be defended to the last man. False Bay, however, the English already held. Here we [were] also told [11] that the rebels had said they would not kill the baptised Hottentots but take them with them, and [...], and the people [?] they would cut to pieces."

<!-- lines 1643-1660 -->
The 12 men who came yesterday are staying here today, so as to hear the word of God at least once, and they came to the meeting in the evening. 8 women who belong to them were here on the 26th. Baas Teunis has allowed them to visit here, but not to stay here; why, or by what authority, this is done we do not know. The 12 men set off for the Cape this morning. They went away very unwillingly and would gladly have stayed longer, to hear more of the word of God. But we encouraged them: when their service at the Cape was over, they could come back.

<!-- lines 1660-1671 -->
The 27th was the day for Communion; but we are putting it off for the present, because both we and our Hottentot communicants are too much distracted in mind by the present circumstances. The unquiet times still went on, and one could not settle on anything. On the 29th the superintendent [12] was with us on a visit. Of the war we heard nothing more from him than that no decision had yet been reached at the Cape. He stayed the night with us.

## Notes

1. *Strandfeld*: probably the Strandveld, the coastal country to the south-east of Baviaanskloof.
2. The transcription reads *Die Hottentotten wurden ſich*; probably *wundern ſich*, "are surprised". To be checked against the image.
3. The transcription reads *daß ſein Sohn ihn Vieh gepfändet hätte*; *ihn* is taken as *ihnen*, "of theirs". Mr Claude had bought the land where young Teunis had set up his place (entry 03).
4. After this sentence the transcription has the sign *₰* on a line of its own (line 1506), probably a mark in the manuscript. It is left out of the English.
5. "Tiger": the Cape Dutch *tijger*, the leopard.
6. *5 Jungen*: "young ones". Probably young livestock, though *Jungen* can also mean boys. To be checked against the image.
7. The transcription reads *um was zu ſagen*, "to say something", which does not fit the warning that follows. "Hide" is a guess. To be checked against the image.
8. *die es mit den Engländern nicht halten wollen*: literally "who will not side with the English". The sense may be the reverse, "who will not side with the rebels"; the rebels threatened those who would not join them. To be checked against the image.
9. *Feld=Corporal*: a local official under the Landdrost. Andreas Otto is probably the "Field Cornet Otto" of A363597.
10. *Schlangen Revier*, "Snake River"; not identified.
11. The transcription reads *Hier erzählten wir auch*, "here we also told"; the sense requires "we were told". The rest of the sentence is garbled: *u. dem ihre mit ihnen ſtreitig werden, u. vollends die Menſchen wolten ſie in Stücken ſchneiden*. The English gives only what can be read. The last clause may refer to the missionaries. To be checked against the image.
12. The transcription reads *war das Aufſehen gar ungemein Leute bey uns zum Beſuch*, which is garbled. *Aufſehen* is probably *der Aufſeher*, the superintendent of the warm bath (see entry 03), since the passage goes on "He stayed the night with us". To be checked against the image.
