<!-- lines 1671-1690 -->
We held Holy Communion afterwards on 30 July [1], and Sister Bodgen partook with us [2]. Today we received a letter from Mr Heuser at the Cape. He wrote that the English were still off the Cape, but that the burghers meant to defend themselves to the utmost. Further, that the ship which had things for us on board had been seized by the English. "It is also said," he writes, "that the whole of Holland is in the hands of the French, and that the Prince of Orange is in England." Now we saw ourselves cut off from all news for once. We would gladly have known how our brothers and sisters in Zeist and Neuwied had fared, but saw no way of learning anything about it.

<!-- lines 1690-1722 -->
On the 5th we received a messenger from Baas Teunis. He had heard that Hottentots had moved here with many cattle; they were to be ordered to the Cape and the cattle driven back. He sent word that if Hottentots came to us we must send them to the Cape at once. One order comes after another that everyone who can be found must march to the Cape. The government has also sent a message to the rebels that they should come and put forward their complaints, they would be heard; but in the meantime they should help to defend the country. They answered that they would not come, but would keep to their purpose. Baas Teunis's messenger did not find here the Hottentots he had been looking for; so he rode back and took the road by which they were to come here; whether he found any we do not know. Amid all the confused circumstances we noticed a mighty work of the Holy Spirit among our people, especially among the new people. But since we did not know what may yet become of us and of them, we had no heart for the present to think of a baptism. The poor people are terribly anxious, and the farmers tell them ten times more than is true.

<!-- lines 1722-1739 -->
On the 9th 7 of the Hottentots whom Baas Teunis had had searched for here actually arrived. We had them come together and asked whether they did not know what Baas Teunis had told them. They said yes. We explained to them that they could not stay here but must go to Baas Teunis, and that we would give them a letter to take with them. On the 10th some who had been with the Baas [told] us [3] that a commando of the rebels would come here that very day to [take] all the men on the road to the Cape, and that they had 300 wagons with them. We did not let ourselves be disturbed, and it turned out that it was not true.

<!-- lines 1739-1762 -->
On the 11th a Hottentot who had arrived not long ago told us that he could not keep quiet but must say what he had heard. One of the farmers who had deposed the Landdrost of Swellendam had asked him whether he too wanted to go to school. Answer: "Yes, Baas." The farmer then said he might as well go; the school would soon be finished. The Hottentots were not to be made clever, but to stay what they were; so many Christian children [grow up like] heathens [4] and learn nothing, so why should the Hottentots learn anything? "We shall soon do away with the school," he said, "for now we are the authorities ourselves, do you know that?" On the way here another farmer had told him the very same. We also heard that the rebels meant to hold a meeting at Swellendam on 3 August.

<!-- lines 1762-1789 -->
On the 12th we received a letter from Father Schmidt at the Cape. He lamented the sad circumstances greatly and wished that he could be with us for a while. Among us the people, even those who had stood firm, are growing ever weaker through the many rumours and threats. Many [5] thought a great deal about fleeing; they asked what we meant to do and wanted to act accordingly. We had to admit ourselves that we were not at ease about it. We did not know what advice to give, but told them that if they had a mind to flee they might go; we were not fleeing yet. When they said they would wait a while too and then go with us, we told them that we could not help them then. David had been away for a couple of days and came home on the 14th. He had heard that the rebels were at Swellendam again waiting for reinforcements, and that they had 300 Hottentots with them too. When the others had joined them, they meant to come and shoot us and the Hottentots dead. We heard similar news from every side and were very uncertain what we should do.

<!-- lines 1789-1830 -->
We resolved that the Brethren Marsveld and Kühnel should ride out tomorrow, the first to Pieter Dohi and the latter to Gildenheis and Mary, to learn something certain. This was done on the 15th. Towards evening Brother Kühnel came home again. At both farmers' there was nothing but sad news, both from the Cape and from the farmers in the country. Yesterday the 4th messenger of the government to the rebels had gone back with nothing achieved. They meant to advance as far as the Kuils River [6], 2 hours from the Cape, and only negotiate there. On the 16th at midday Brother Marsveld came home too. He had not been able to learn much at Pieter Dohi's; but Dohi promised in case of need to take our cattle to his place, and also to lend us horses for flight to the Cape. Since many of our Hottentots would gladly have fled, but no one wanted to make the start, and they were also ashamed to leave us, since we were here for their sake, we told them in the meeting that in this they should act entirely as their hearts told them, and not go by us; no one would be blamed if he left us now. We had seen for several days [7] that our girl who is in service with us was very anxious and worried, and so we told her especially that she could do as her heart bade her. Late in the evening Anna Maria, Elisabeth, Rahel and Magdalena came to us with an old Hottentot; they meant to hide in the mountains, because they are very frightened at night. They came to say goodbye, which was very moving to see, for it was so dark that one could not see one's hand before one's eyes.

<!-- lines 1830-1868 -->
On the 17th Brother Kühnel rode to Baas Teunis to ask his advice in the matter. He said at once: "Things could not look worse than they do." He told him that he had spoken with several of the rebels, who now call themselves the Nationals [8], and had found that they are extremely angry about the school, and this because the Hottentots are leaving them on its account. They say: "Who is to do our work for us? For we have no slaves. The Hottentots have been among the farmers so long and were never taught; why should they be taught now? You who live on the Sonderend River can get as many Hottentots as you want, but what are we to do? Why do the people not come to our district? They shall be as well off with us as anywhere else!" His advice therefore was that we should direct all the Hottentots who had moved to us from the district of Swellendam to go back to their places; the farmers would be satisfied with that; if it did not happen, a great calamity might follow. He said: "All this time I have held back the people from there as much as I could, and there are still 53 persons lying on this side of the water whom I have forbidden to go to you. I cannot get them to go back; but if you tell them, they will go, for one word from you counts for more than ten from me. The others, who belong to this district, they will not harm, I believe, and they will probably leave you in peace then too." In the evening Brother Kühnel came home again.

<!-- lines 1868-1898 -->
Baas Teunis's advice put us in great perplexity. We turned it this way and that before the Saviour, and at last believed that for the time being it was the right advice and really the best for us. But think, dear Brethren and Sisters, how we must have felt! To tell so many people eager for salvation, in whom one felt the work of the Holy Spirit most mightily and powerfully, who cannot hear enough of Jesus' agony and death, and who stand firm against all harsh threats and say, "Let things go as they will, we will not give up God's word": to tell such people that they must go away was a very hard thing for us; our hearts bled over it. Yet in view of the dangers threatening them it seemed necessary; we had to overcome ourselves and commend them to God's mercy. He still sits at the helm and can change these circumstances too. We decided to suspend the schools entirely for the time being, and to call the people together early tomorrow morning and tell them our mind. That night most of the women and children had hidden in the mountains for fear of being attacked in the night. Early in the morning they could be seen coming out of the mountains on every side. We had them called, and soon they were all together.

<!-- lines 1901-1936 -->
So the matter was told them. What a stir it caused among them cannot be described; everyone broke into loud weeping. Within an hour [9] 15 came at once to say goodbye. A heart of stone would have been moved to pity. Women and children wept. Pitiful was the sight of little children tied quite naked on their mothers' backs, and in the bitterest cold and rainy weather besides, at a time when all the rivers are so swollen that here with us, for example, the water comes up to the middle of the body, and further down the rivers are deeper still. Others came and said: "How am I to get away? I am not well and have little children who cannot walk yet; I can hardly walk myself, how am I to carry them?" Such people, and others who had yet other difficulties, came and asked for advice, which we did not know how to give. We meant to count how many left at once, and counted up to about 30; then in our grief and sorrow and distress we forgot to count further. For the leave-taking went on until towards evening, and we cannot say how many moved away. What a day this was for us we cannot describe; we shall not forget it as long as we live. We [had meant] to hold a meeting in the evening, but let it drop today [10]. In the evening we closed the week with a truly mighty prayer on our knees, begged the Saviour most urgently for help, and commended these poor black people to his merciful heart.

<!-- lines 1937-1952 -->
We had lately read together, with the greatest compassion and sympathy, the diary of the Indian congregation on its pilgrimage [11] from April to October 1792. Yet the situation we are in is sadder still. If the Indians have to flee, they at least have a place to go to; but the Hottentots have not. The Indians can stay together and have their teacher with them; but we may not be with the Hottentots. Whichever way these go, they come to farmers who want to make slaves of them. The Indians can carry away the little they have in a canoe; the Hottentots must carry it on their backs.

<!-- lines 1952-1974 -->
On the 19th, early, our baptised came to the litany. None of them has moved away yet, although some of them too belong to the district of Swellendam. We thought that now we would have room enough for the sermon, since so many had moved away yesterday; but the room, the entrance hall and the kitchen were quite full again. More people have lived here than we ourselves knew; for we saw that the house was always full, but those who had to stand outside we could not see. On the 20th we received a letter from Mr Teunis. He told us that yesterday a commando of the Nationals had been at Mr Lind's [12]; if we rode there we might perhaps learn something about the school, for a paper had been read out which was to be submitted to the government, and in it there was something about the school.

<!-- lines 1974-2019 -->
On the 21st Brother Kühnel went there and found that the paper [13], to our distress, had run roughly as follows: "There must be no right to teach the Hottentots; for since there are so many Christians who are not taught, the Hottentots must not be taught either, but stay as they have been. Those who are born on a farmer's land must stay on it and serve him without pay until they are 25 years old; after that they shall serve for wages. The Hottentots must live among the farmers and not all together as in Baviaanskloof. The Bushmen whom we capture must remain slaves for life. Further: [...] [14]", and more such things, which had been agreed in the name of liberty and equality. The people said: "What was read out is all still bearable; but not a third of it was read to us, and we had to sign it, for one's life is dear to one after all." 3000 people are said to have signed already. They would have nothing more to do with the Company, and mean to resist the English as well. Brother Kühnel rode back as far as Baas Teunis's, where he stayed the night. Teunis told him that lately a commando of 25 men had been on the way to us to put an end to the school. Holzhäuser, who had been sent by the government to [...] [15], met this commando, and because they now had [?] guns again, they turned back; but they told him that the school must come to an end, because rogues and thieves were staying there. On the 22nd, towards evening, Brother Kühnel came home again and brought us no cheering news.

<!-- lines 2019-2050 -->
On the 24th, early, we received a letter from Pieter Dohi, who told us he wished to speak with one of us about things that could not well be written in the present circumstances. Brother Schwinn went there. Some of the Nationals had been here [at Dohi's] too, and had at once begun to talk about the Hottentots, and said: "Why does the Company send teachers for the Hottentots and not schoolmasters for our children too?" Pieter Dohi knew how to calm them, by telling them: "The Company did not send the Herrnhuters; their brethren did, and the Company gives them nothing; they have to get everything from their brethren," and so on. They also brought forward all sorts of complaints [16], which it would take too long to list. For the rest, Pieter Dohi thought there were decent people among them too, only at present they were still very prejudiced; much might change, and we might yet come through quite well. On the 25th we thought it good to hold Holy Communion by ourselves alone. Today we wrote several letters to good friends at the Cape, because we had heard that some of them are in correspondence with the commandant of the Nationals. Pieter Dohi undertook to see to the letters.

<!-- lines 2050-2085 -->
On the 29th, towards evening, we saw 2 riders coming towards us. It was Holzhäuser and Hendr. Welksel, good friends at least, so that we were not anxious. When they came into the house they were quite silent at first. When asked, "What good news is there?", Holzhäuser said: "Not much good. We bring an order from Commandant Bisani [17], to announce to you that within 3 days you must leave this place and move either to the Cape or to a burgher's farm; otherwise you have bad consequences to expect." What a blow to the heart this was for us we cannot describe. We asked why, and what we had done. He said he knew nothing more to tell us; a commando of 8 men had come to them with the order to deliver this to us. This Commandant Bisani is going round behind the mountains with 800 men towards Kaffer Land [18] and so on to the Cape. In the hurry we did not know what we should do, or where we could take our things, especially because of the rising water. Brother Kühnel rode to Baas Teunis that same night, and he said: "In these circumstances I know no advice but to obey the order. Might goes before right; only, the same rabble may be expected any day. As for your things, I will send a wagon tomorrow to take them to my place. I do not know how it will go with me, but where my things stay, yours shall stay too."

<!-- lines 2086-2116 -->
Here among us the matter soon became known among the Hottentots. Young and old came and wept, and also helped us with the clearing up. [In] that time of turmoil they had 12 wagons standing, which they tried to pull across the river with [...] [19]. Whoever had an ox harnessed it at once. Brother Kühnel came back that same night, and we packed what we could. On the 30th Baas Teunis's wagon came too. Not much could be loaded on the wagons here; still, we now had three of them. All day long the Hottentots lay round our house, and there was no end to the lamenting and weeping. Towards evening we had them all come together in front of our house, for there would not have been room for them inside. For sorrow [20] not much could be said; so they were admonished in a few words to think diligently of what they had heard here. Then Brother Kühnel offered another prayer on his knees, and so for this time we lay for the last time with these black people before the face of Jesus, who was entreated from the heart [21] not to leave them or forsake them, but to have mercy on them and deliver them in time from this distress. The emotion that was stirred on this occasion God has surely counted; men could not have counted it.

<!-- lines 2116-2146 -->
On the 31st the wagons with our things went [22] to Baas Teunis. For our journey, and to carry the most necessary linen and so on, we took our own wagon; some of us [23] meant to ride. The Hottentots brought together 20 oxen for us, of which 10 were inspanned and 10 went ahead unyoked, to change over. Early in the morning all the Hottentots were gathered in front of our house again. Many wanted to go with us, and we had much to do to talk them out of it. They said: "We are to blame that this is happening to our teachers; we have not been thankful and obedient enough, and so they are being taken from us. Oh God, forgive us our sin!" We charged some of the baptised to stay in our house and look after the garden. So we left our congregation and our hopeful work in the school, with many thousands of tears on both sides. Many ran after us for an hour's way [...] [24]. It was a day for us that must remain beyond description. Yet in all our pain we comforted ourselves that we were full of faith and confidence that the Saviour will still complete the work he has begun, and in such a way that he shall have praise and honour from it.

<!-- lines 2146-2169 -->
The watchword on 29 July, when we received the order to leave, was: "He hath made his wonderful works to be remembered: the Lord is gracious and full of compassion"; and the text: "I am the Christ, the Son of the Blessed; and ye shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven." On the 30th, when we took our leave with the meal [25], the watchword was: "I will seek that which was lost, and bring again that which was driven away, and will bind up that which was broken, and will feed the weak, and will care for them as is right"; and the text: "The Lord said unto my Lord, Sit thou at my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool." And on the day we set out, the watchword: "I will give thee rest from all thine enemies"; and the text: "I am Jesus, whom thou persecutest." [And the verse:] "Thou wilt stand by me against all enemies; when thou speakest but a word, they soon [become] friends and must lay down arms and weapons [26]."

<!-- lines 2169-2178 -->
Today we drove as far as Pieter Dohi's, who was quite astonished at this event. He entertained us meanwhile [...] [27]. Here a Hottentot woman offered us her wagon in place of our cart, which we accepted. In the night a great crowd of Hottentots, young and old, had come after us, and when we travelled on on 1 August there was again a tearful leave-taking.

## Notes

1. *am 30te July*, in both columns. This cannot be right: the sentence stands between 29 June and 5 July, and entry 08 says that there had been no Communion with the Khoekhoe communicants "since 3 July". The Communion postponed on 27 June (entry 05) was probably held on 3 July, and *30te* is a misreading of *3te*. To be checked against the image.
2. *die Schw. Bodgen war unſere nähmlige Mitgenoſſin*: *nähmlige* is unclear (perhaps *nämliche*, "same", or a misreading). Sister Bodgen is not otherwise identified; entry 08 mentions the baptism of "the little son of our Mr Bedgen", perhaps the same family.
3. The transcription reads *ſezten uns einige, die bey dem Baas vorn geweſen*: a verb is missing (perhaps *ſezten uns in Kenntniß*, "informed us"). "Take" in the next clause is also supplied: *um alle Männer auf dem Weg nach der Cap*.
4. The transcription reads *es ließen ja ſo viele Chriſtenkinders heiden, die nichts lernten*. The sense seems to be that many settler children grew up as ignorant as heathens. To be checked against the image.
5. The transcription reads *jemand*, "someone", but the verbs that follow are plural (*fragten*, *wolten*). "Many" is a guess.
6. The transcription reads *bis das Vernd. Kuylen*; probably *bis an das Revier Kuylen*, the Kuils River, east of Cape Town. To be checked against the image.
7. The transcription reads *angeſagt*, "announced", which makes no sense here; probably *angeſehen*, "seen".
8. *die Nationalen*: the name taken by the rebel burghers of Swellendam (and Graaff-Reinet) in 1795, who deposed their Landdrosts and declared themselves independent of the Dutch East India Company.
9. The transcription reads *Ohne eine Stunde darauf*; probably *Kaum eine Stunde darauf*, "hardly an hour later".
10. The transcription reads *Wir waren alle den d. ganzen Abend eine Verſammlung zu halten, u. ſetzten ſie heute aus*, which is garbled. The English gives the probable sense. To be checked against the image.
11. *von ihren Pilagnefart*: read as *Pilgerfahrt*, "pilgrimage". Probably the Delaware congregation of the Moravian mission in North America, which moved in 1792 to the Thames River in Upper Canada. *Indianer*, "Indians", is kept as the diarists wrote it.

13. The transcription reads *Gerücht*, "rumour"; the sense is the paper (*Aufſatz*) read out at Lind's.

15. The transcription reads *an die Fuhr von geſchickt*; unclear. The next clause, *weil ſie nun wieder Büchſe hatten*, is also unclear: perhaps the commando turned back because Holzhäuser's party was armed. To be checked against the image.
16. The transcription reads *Er gab noch alles bey Beſchwerden*; the subject may be the Nationals.
