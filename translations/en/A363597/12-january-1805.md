<!-- lines 1806-1813 -->
On the Festival of the Saviour [1], 6 January 1805, there was a baptism of 7 adults and 3 children in the morning. A sick man had already been [baptised] in his home that morning [2]. In the evening, in a prayer, we commended to the Saviour all our congregations among the heathen, with the men and women who serve them, and called on him to further the work of his hands everywhere on earth.

<!-- lines 1814-1821 -->
On the 7th a widowed sister fell asleep after a long illness. Although she had much to bear [3], she was always found full of trust in the dear Saviour, on whom she had set her only hope in hard days too. By her upright life she had earned the love and respect of old and young in the congregation.

<!-- lines 1821-1836 -->
The next day a 7-year-old girl, who had always been very sickly, also departed this life, in the end of consumption. This child was especially loving and patient in her illness, and in the last days of her life looked forward with all her heart to going home. The day before, she had all her acquaintances and playmates called to her, took a touching leave of them, and said she would soon go to her Saviour and find there all her friends who had gone before her. "I am heartily glad of it," she added; "here below I need nothing more than Christ's blood and righteousness."

<!-- lines 1837-1843 -->
Brother Rose's illness, which had reached a high degree about this time, broke towards the end of this month, and the patient grew better from day to day. From the middle of January the schools and meetings could again be held in their usual order.

<!-- lines 1844-1868 -->
On 19 January we received 4 wagonloads of grain from the government, which made us very glad, since it enabled us to give out to the wives and children of the Hottentots serving as soldiers the quantity set for each of them. Because the grain did not ripen at all in these parts last year on account of the great drought, and there is therefore great scarcity at present, the government has set up a special commission to see that the necessary grain is brought here. According to the proclamation issued on this, 1 pound of grain a day is reckoned per head; and since this is less than was originally set for the Hottentot women and children here, the government intends, as Governor Janssens soon informed us, to give one sheep a day for every 50 women. The only great difficulty at present is that, because the drought still continues, the livestock here find no grazing.

## Notes

1. The transcription reads *Heilandsfeste*. In entry 08 the same day, 6 January, is called *Heidenfest*, the Festival of the Heathen; this may be a misreading of the same word.
2. The German says the sick man had been "brought into his home" (*in seine Wohnung gebracht*). In the context of the baptism, the sense is probably that he had been baptised at home. To be checked against the image.
3. *viel Zudringlichkeit*, literally "much importunity". The sense is unclear; it may refer to troubles pressing on her.
