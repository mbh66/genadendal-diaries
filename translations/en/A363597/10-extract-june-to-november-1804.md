Extract from the diary of Baviaanskloof, from June to the end of November 1804.

<!-- lines 1514-1521 -->
Just as we had an extraordinary drought in the past months, so it began again this month. It puts our whole district in great difficulty: nothing can be sown, because the ground is like rock, and the poor cattle cannot find their food either and in many places must die of hunger. This drought lasted until [1]

<!-- lines 1521-1529 -->
On 9 and 10 June there was so violent a storm that it threatened to tear off our roofs and several others, and the church roof especially, entirely, so the church was shut. All the men hurried to help, by day the roof [2], and the faithful Saviour let the means succeed and preserved our building, so that we suffered only slight damage, which was soon repaired. For this we thanked the Saviour.

<!-- lines 1530-1534 -->
On the 13th we held Holy Communion, and also had the joy of receiving letters from Brother von Forestier in Berthelsdorf and Brother Schreiter in Zeist, which the Governor forwarded to us.

<!-- lines 1535-1542 -->
On 14 July we were visited by Captain Weimere, who is coming from China and bound for Emden. We would gladly have sent Brethren [3] with him to Zeist, but because of the war he had misgivings about taking them. He did, however, ask to borrow our register of personal records, which he wanted to show in Zeist as proof that he had been here.

<!-- lines 1543-1565 -->
On 14 July our friend Peter Ditoy came to visit us with his brother and their wives and children. Daniel Ditoy visited our old Simeon Rokkers and some other Hottentots who had often worked [for them] [4], and said to Simeon: "Since I grew up in the house under your care, you are still dear to me; but who would have thought that you would live to be so old?" Simeon answered with tears in his eyes: "Yes, Baas, the merciful God has done this. He has spared me so that I should come to know his Son Jesus, my Saviour, and through him be washed of my sins with his blood in holy baptism. This good fortune [has come to] the Baas [5], to change my evil heart. But Baas must not think that everything is now done. No: sin still lies at the door, and old as I am, I must still see and feel that much is still lacking in me; and when I forget to pray, sin comes in at the door again," and so on. Daniel Ditoy looked at the old man with astonished eyes and went away from him with tears in his eyes.

<!-- lines 1566-1576 -->
On the 19th a certain A. Mast Osworter [6] came, and on hearing that some had been called to become candidates for baptism and some to holy baptism, she said: "The evening before, I went into my garden, threw myself on my face and said: 'Dear Saviour, even if I and my house are so ungrateful, do not let others in the congregation suffer for it!' And now I see that the Saviour does not despise the prayer of poor sinners."

<!-- lines 1577-1585 -->
On 28 July we had extraordinarily bitter cold, so that in the night the mountains were entirely covered with snow and there was ice. The month of August went on and on with harsh, cold weather and endless downpours, so that the rivers could no longer be crossed. The rain had now softened the parched ground again, so that the plough could get into the earth once more. The dearth continued.

<!-- lines 1586-1597 -->
On the 13th two women named Erdmath and Sara were baptised into the death of Jesus, and in the evening we held Holy Communion, at which four persons looked on as candidates. On the 24th Brother and Sister Kohrhammer set out for the Cape, to the Hottentots stationed in the camp. On the 27th we received from Commandant Linde, on the government's account, three wagonloads of grain to distribute to the wives and children of those Hottentots whose husbands and fathers are in military service in the camp. A load of grain costs 72 Rixdollars.

<!-- lines 1598-1604 -->
On 3 September we had news of the safe arrival of Brother and Sister Kohrhammer in the camp, where they were received with much love by the officer commanding there, Mr Lesueur, and afterwards by General Commissioner De Mist and Governor Janssens.

<!-- lines 1605-1613 -->
The preacher Voos [7], formerly stationed at Roodezand in Africa, who went to England two years ago, arrived in Cape Town a few days ago by way of Copenhagen with three other missionaries, among them Mr Ringeltaube. He had asked for permission to stay here again, but did not receive it, and will go with his wife and two missionaries to Ceylon, while Ringeltaube goes with three missionaries to Madras.

<!-- lines 1614-1639 -->
Brother Rose also received greetings from a soldier in garrison at the Cape named Johann Christoph Becher, a native of Suhl in Thuringia. He was brought up as a child in the institution at Ebersdorf, learned weaving there, was received into the congregation and admitted to Holy Communion, but then left the congregation, led astray by his stepfather. Later he travelled to Holland on business, fell there into the hands of the soul-sellers [8], was robbed of all he had and sold as a soldier, and was sent here when the Cape was newly taken over. He sought out Brother Rose while Rose was at the Cape and poured out his heart to him, more with tears than with words. Among other things his letter says: "The state of my soul is most sad and dangerous, as remorse and despair rage in my heart. Only anxious sighs and tears to my dear Saviour bring my oppressed heart some relief. Oh, if only I had taken your kind and gracious parting words with me, perhaps I would not have come into this wretched state. Pray for me to him from whom all help comes and who knows our need."

<!-- lines 1640-1655 -->
On the 5th [9] a dispatch rider from the Governor's office announced the following persons for a midday meal with us: Captain Paravicini di Capelli, the Governor's adjutant; the Governor's son with his tutor, Mr Lichtenstein; Captain of Horse Harting; Lieutenant de Labat; and Lieutenant Meyer, with three dragoons. They stayed here a few hours and dined with us, and during the meal a number of schoolchildren entertained the company by singing some verses, which gave them much pleasure. They then left ten Rixdollars for the children and continued their journey; dried peaches were handed out [10].

<!-- lines 1655-1665 -->
On 2 September Brother Kohrhammer preached the first sermon in the camp, in the open air. After that the Governor had a large tent put up, under which he, the preachers and the officers took their places. The Hottentots sat around the tent. When the corps was later ordered into the town to defend it, Brother Kohrhammer was released by the government with many thanks, and arrived back in Baviaanskloof on 12 October.

<!-- lines 1666-1693 -->
In a friendly letter the government asked us to take in the wives and children of the men in military service; the Governor's office would provide for their necessary upkeep. On the 11th the first transport from the camp at the Wynberg [11] arrived here. The sight of some [number missing] and 80 women, not counting the children, half starved and almost without clothing, moved us deeply, and we tried as far as we could to still their hunger at once and to find them shelter. On the 13th the second transport arrived and on the 15th the third, so that we now have 187 women and children, of all kinds of people, with us. This is no small burden for us. But in the hope that the seed of the Gospel might yet bear fruit in some of them, we gladly took on this difficulty and showed them mercy. The commander of the Hottentots, Lesueur, sent us by express 20 Rixdollars from the soldiers in service for their wives and children, and it was distributed at once. A commission concerned with the distribution of grain also came to us; we distribute it weekly.

<!-- lines 1694-1699 -->
All the inhabitants of the country now had to take up arms for its defence, and to this end 24 Hottentot men were also called up here, leaving their wives and children behind. It grieves us to leave this poor people without care, but we must resign ourselves to it.

<!-- lines 1700-1703 -->
Note. The month of November contains nothing remarkable apart from Brother Rose's illness.

## Notes

1. The sentence breaks off in the manuscript or in the transcription (line 1521), and the next begins with a new date.
2. The German is incomplete here: *bey Tag das Dach* ("by day the roof"). A verb, such as "to secure", seems to be missing.
3. The transcription reads *Brüder* ("Brethren"). *Briefe* ("letters") would make better sense with a ship's captain who then borrows a register as proof of his visit. To be checked against the image.
4. *die oft in Arbeitsgemein waren*: the word is unclear. The sense seems to be that these men had often worked for the Ditoy family.
5. The sentence *Nun dieses Glück hat die Baas, mein böses Herz zu verändern* does not make grammatical sense as transcribed. Simeon may be saying that this good fortune has changed his evil heart. To be checked against the image.
6. The name is unclear in the transcription (*ein A Maſt Oſworter*). The pronoun that follows shows the speaker was a woman.
7. Probably Michiel Christiaan Vos, preacher at Roodezand (Tulbagh), who later worked in Ceylon; and Wilhelm Tobias Ringeltaube, later a missionary in south India. To be confirmed.
8. *Seelenverkäufer*: recruiters who trapped men into service with the Dutch East India Company or the army, often by debt or deceit.
9. The month is not stated. The passage sits between 3 September and a passage dated 2 September, so the order here is not strictly by date.
10. The end of this sentence is garbled in the transcription (*für den Wirth getrocknete Pfirsige*). It may mean that dried peaches were given out for the travellers, or on the host's account.
11. *Lager an den Weinbergen*: probably the military camp at Wynberg near Cape Town.
