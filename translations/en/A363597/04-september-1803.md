It cleared up again on 3 September, but on the 13th we again had such cold rain that the mountains near us were covered in snow.

<!-- lines 425-441 -->
On 17 September one of our communicant sisters went happily home. Since being admitted to Holy Communion she had kept watch with a faithful heart over the grace given her, and the other brothers and sisters loved and valued her for her childlike, believing attachment to Jesus. In her illness she was visited often, and was always found turned towards the Saviour and full of joy that she would soon have the grace of seeing her Redeemer and being with him for ever.

<!-- lines 441-497 -->
For some time we had noticed with sorrow that our schoolchildren gave way to frivolity, in school and in the meetings alike, and allowed themselves all kinds of bad behaviour. Since all the warnings so far had borne no fruit, Brother Rose had to try something in the school. After the children had recited to him, with great pleasure, what they had learned, he put it to them kindly but firmly how much trouble their teachers took to acquaint them in their youth with their God and Creator, and to impress on their hearts how much it had cost the Saviour that they could become his dear children. The children listened to this in great silence, and Brother Rose then told them that if they went on behaving as they had until now, grieving the Saviour and us, we would be obliged to close the school and stop the children's meetings. He asked them to tell him quite simply and honestly what they now meant to do. When the children promised to be obedient in future, Brother Rose said that those for whom this was the truth, and no empty promise, should stand up and give us their hands on it; but anyone who did not mean to give the Saviour their whole heart should stay seated. At once the children hurried in floods of tears to Brothers Rose and Kohrhammer. Each wanted to give the first handshake, and the little ones, who were held back, raised both hands high just to be seen. One could feel that they meant it in earnest, and this event had good results.

<!-- lines 497-525 -->
In these days we suffered the pain of seeing one of our communicants fall so deep into open sin that he had to leave the congregation. His unfaithful disposition had not been wholly hidden from us, but he knew how to do his evil deeds so much in the dark that we could prove nothing against him, and when we spoke to him he used to deny everything. It was the first case of its kind here, and it filled all the brothers and sisters in the whole congregation with sorrow. The man had taken his son of about 16 with him by force. But as they were nearing the capital [1], the son said: "Father, I cannot stay separated from God and his Word. I will turn back and go to Baviaanskloof." To which the father answered: "Well then, go where you like."

<!-- lines 525-544 -->
On the 20th [2] several more Hottentot families, most of whom had lived here before, came back to us from the camp. At their request they were given permission to stay, on their promise to keep our rules. However willing these people are to confirm that promise with a handshake, they often leave us at a loss, because several of them were without our care for six or seven years, and in that time everything they had learned from the Word of God has slipped from their memories and their hearts.

<!-- lines 545-572 -->
On the 25th a widowed sister fell asleep, who had been a communicant since 1799. Among us she proved a true sister: her unfeigned love for the Saviour, her honest conduct towards everyone, and her patient endurance in her long and painful illness were an encouragement and a blessing to others. More than six years ago, going barefoot, she had trodden on the skeleton of a snake and received what seemed a trifling injury. But the poison had nevertheless entered her body, and in spite of every remedy tried, she gradually lost the use of several limbs. Whenever she was visited during this illness she declared that she was going to the Saviour as a poor but pardoned sinner, and that we could be fully comforted about her.

<!-- lines 573-604 -->
For some days a rumour had been spreading here that war had broken out again in Europe [3], and now, sadly, we learned that it was entirely true. For on the 25th Captain Lesueur arrived here, accompanied by Field Cornet Otto. The captain handed us a written order from the government, to this effect: Governor Janssens had instructed Captain Lesueur to go to our settlement and recruit a volunteer corps of Hottentots for military service. We were therefore asked to put no obstacle in the captain's way in carrying out this commission, and to support him in every way. At the same time we were assured that due care would be taken to preserve the good morals we have instilled in the Hottentots.

<!-- lines 604-639 -->
So at the end of the sermon it was announced that all the males in the church should stay there, and that all those in their houses should come at once when the bell was rung as a signal. When all were assembled, Brother Rose first explained the purpose of the meeting to them; then Captain Lesueur read out the government's order, and its content was explained sentence by sentence until they all declared that they had understood everything. Finally they were asked to think carefully about what they had heard, and then to give the captain their views one by one. No one was to leave here before he had given his answer. After the midday meal Field Cornet Otto rode home again, while the captain sent an express to ask Field Cornet Linde to come here, presumably because this man knows our people well and could be of good service to the captain in his business.

<!-- lines 639-657 -->
Mr Linde arrived the next day, and on the 27th all the males present here, old and young, were asked for their decision by the captain and Field Cornet Linde in the presence of Brother Rose. It turned out that 22 men enlisted, among them one communicant, two baptised men and two candidates for baptism. They asked only that provision be made for the upkeep of their wives and children, who number 40 persons, and the captain promised to speak to the government on their behalf.

<!-- lines 658-708 -->
For us this whole affair was very unpleasant, all the more because we had to fear that it will not stop at this first attempt, since the government wants to get a considerable number of reliable men from among our baptised, who could be appointed as non-commissioned officers in the Hottentot corps. For our part we did everything possible to convince the government that we do not wish to stand in the way of its purpose. For our own security Brother Rose had written down everything that had happened, and at our request Messrs Lesueur and Linde confirmed this document with their signatures. In the following days these gentlemen left, each with a part of the recruited Hottentots. We sent the recruits off with heartfelt exhortations about their new station and calling, and asked them not to lose sight of the teaching they had received from us out of God's Word, and to live by it. Many took leave with tears in their eyes, and at this sight we too were so moved that we had to go off by ourselves and let our tears flow freely.

<!-- lines 708-725 -->
To do everything in our power, on our side, for the good of these Hottentots, Brother Rose had written to Governor Janssens, congratulating him on his return from the Kaffer country [4] and commending most earnestly to his charity and care the wives and children left behind by the Hottentots who had volunteered for service, who are now entirely without support.

## Notes

1. *Hauptstadt*: Cape Town.
2. The diary gives only the day for this passage and the next two. The month is presumably September, since the passages fall between 17 September and 4 October. Two events are dated "the 25th".
3. War between Britain and France resumed in May 1803, and the Batavian Republic, which then governed the Cape, was drawn in on the French side.
4. *Kaffern-Lande*: the Xhosa country beyond the eastern border of the colony. Governor Janssens toured the eastern frontier in 1803.
