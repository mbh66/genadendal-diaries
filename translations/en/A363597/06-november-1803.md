On 2 November the unbaptised wife of a Hottentot brother, who is a communicant, departed this life. Although she had lived here for many years and heard God's Word, no one ever noticed that it had made any impression on her heart; she lived on quite unconcerned about herself until at last she saw that she was going to die. Then she began to seek grace and mercy from Jesus, which, as we trust in faith, she will also have found. Her husband and her children, who go to school, sang her some verses before her end, and she died while they sang.

<!-- lines 853-863 -->
On the 4th four of our Hottentots who have taken military service again left for the camp. Among them was one candidate for baptism. Each of them was spoken to suitably at parting, and what they have heard here was laid on their hearts once more.

<!-- lines 863-894 -->
On the 5th a man named Carl Schmidt arrived here on his way from Cape Town to the lower country. He told us that he was born in Lübbenau in Lower Lusatia, had come to the Brethren's congregation at Ebersdorf in 1780, and had been admitted to Holy Communion there. He later went to Gnadenfeld and left the congregation in 1792. After that he enlisted as a soldier in Holland and came to Cape Town in 1793. Now he lives with his wife and child on Krumbeck's River [1], seven miles below Swellendam. He regretted the step he took in his youth in leaving the congregation [2], and said: "Outwardly I suffer no hardship and no want, but oh, I feel that the hand of God finds me even here in Africa. It is hard to struggle against that hand, and a wounded conscience is never silent." He attended the Sunday meetings and took leave with many tears.

<!-- lines 895-909 -->
On the 6th a large party belonging to the newly founded African Society [3] arrived on a visit, accompanied by Mr Wesel Wessels. They all attended our meetings, to their edification, as they said. Part of the party left again on the 8th, and on the 9th Mr Wessels [4] followed them with his wife and children. He was very grateful for the blessing he had enjoyed here.

<!-- lines 910-929 -->
On 13 November an excluded baptised woman departed this life, who had lain painfully ill for two years and had served others as a warning example. Soon after she had become unfaithful to the grace given her in baptism and fallen into sin, she was struck by apoplexy, which robbed her of hearing and sight and left her entirely crippled. We gave her many charitable gifts; but since no one could talk with her, one always had to leave her with a sorrowful heart, and could do nothing for her soul except commend her to the mercy of God.

<!-- lines 930-978 -->
On the 22nd Field Cornet Otto informed us that it had pleased the Lord of life and death to take his wife to himself. At the same time he asked that a couple of the Brethren would do him the kindness of accompanying her body to its resting place. So Brothers Kohrhammer and Kühnel travelled there and were received very warmly by Mr Otto, who said he was most grateful that we had not refused his request. He added that his late wife had always felt great love for the Brethren and Sisters and had received much blessing for her heart among us. He himself now thought quite differently from before. In the past, when he heard people speak of the pardoned and the unpardoned, the converted and the unconverted, he had taken everything said about it for mere fables and foolery; but at his wife's end he had been vividly convinced that there is a great difference between believing and unbelieving souls, between the pardoned and the unpardoned, and that one can die blessed only in Jesus Christ. He wished that this conviction might never leave his mind again; but, he added, he felt no true peace in his heart and could not yet believe in the comfort of the Gospel. The Brothers told him that God often uses such means to convince us of our natural unbelief. Then one learns to understand the words rightly: "Lord, help thou mine unbelief!"

<!-- lines 979-1001 -->
On the 24th Field Cornet Linde visited us. He has been commissioned to buy the grain needed for the wives and children of the Hottentots in service. The government had fixed one mud [5], or almost one Dresden Scheffel, a week for twelve women, and the same for 24 children, and we were asked to supervise and to see to the distribution with Mr Linde's help. Since no rye was to be had anywhere in the district at present, barley was given out instead. For the rest, we, like those who benefited from this kindness, were heartily grateful for the generous care of our beloved authorities.

<!-- lines 1001-1012 -->
On the 26th, after an extraordinarily long drought, we again had a refreshing rain, which relieved not only the dryness but also the plague of caterpillars, which had begun to do great damage to the garden and field crops. We gave our heartfelt thanks for this blessing to the Giver of all good things.

<!-- lines 1012-1029 -->
Towards the end of this month many of our Hottentots, men, women and children, left here to help the farmers with the harvest. Since we knew how busy the enemy is on such occasions in leading the Hottentots into sin, especially through drink, we took care to put it to them firmly what harm they could do to their souls if they did not keep the Saviour in their hearts and before their eyes.

## Notes

1. *Krumbecks Revier*: not identified. *Revier* is the Cape Dutch *rivier*, "river". The "miles" are probably German or Dutch miles, much longer than English miles.
2. The transcription reads *Er bereute te Jahr seinen ... Schritt*; *te Jahr* is probably a misreading at the page break and is left out.
3. *Africanische Societät*: probably the South African Missionary Society (Zuid-Afrikaansche Zendelinggenootschap), founded in Cape Town in 1799. To be confirmed. Entry 08 calls Mr Desch one of its directors.
4. The diary names him first as Wesel Wessels and then as Mr Wesel.
5. *Mud*: the Cape muid, a dry measure for grain.
