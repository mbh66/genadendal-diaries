<!-- lines 920-935 -->
On 2 Jan. we received a letter from the Cape from Brother Haensel, who was on his way from the East Indies to Europe [1]; we would very much have liked to speak with him in person. We gave him a letter, and one to go to Europe. On the 3rd Baas Teunis said that tomorrow we would drive to Baviaanskloof and lay out our house, and see whether we could get people who were willing to work.

<!-- lines 935-957 -->
On the 4th at 9 o'clock in the morning we set off. We drove to his son's house, to take tools from there, and the mason who is building his son's house. This mason is also to make the stone foundation of our house. At 3 o'clock we arrived at our place, Baviaanskloof; the house was pegged out at once. The space inside will be 36 feet long and 15 wide. It will have a living room, a bedroom and a kitchen; 7 feet up to the roof. Then a tent was put up, under which we and the mason were to lodge. We had hardly arrived when 9 Hottentot men came, and soon afterwards 10 Hottentot women, to welcome us.

<!-- lines 957-1074 -->
We showed them [where to sit, near] the dear Saviour [?] under the pear tree that Brother Schmidt planted, which is quite big [2]. As soon as the house was pegged out we began to carry stones from Brother Schmidt's former house, which stands 15 paces from ours. We are building ours 15 paces further south. Brother Schmidt's house had walls of stone; ours will be of clay. Baas Teunis assures us that it could stand for 50 years. His house is of clay too; it has stood 50 years and is still good. The gables and inner walls will be of unburnt bricks. The Hottentots helped carry as long as it was day. Baas Teunis spoke to them about helping for a fair wage, but at their own cost for food. He said that it must be finished before the *quaimaison* [3], that is, the winter, when it usually rains so hard that nothing can be built. They all promised to help; but at their own cost for food, that is hard for them, for they have nothing. When they have done some work for the farmers, they go to the kraal and eat and drink as long as there is anything there. Many of them will do nothing at all, but lie in their huts the whole time; when they are hungry they look for some roots and fruits and are content with that; when they have something, they dance and are merry and in good spirits. Two of them were respectable people, who have many cattle and fine gardens too. They are not real Hottentots; they have a Christian for a father but a Hottentot woman for a mother; such people are called Bastards. There are many of this kind, who have for a father a Christian, or a black man of those who are here as slaves. These two could do it [4]; they are our nearest neighbours, and no one lives nearer to us, about half an hour away from us. Half an hour away they dug [?], close by [5]. They have promised to help us with vegetables. They could also be of great service to us; for where are we to get anything from at first? If we go to the farmers, they will give us nothing, because they do not like to see us coming here. And even if that were not so, it is too far from us: the nearest is two hours away. To the post, to Baas Teunis, it is four hours for us by wagon; how far on foot I do not know, because in this country one does not go on foot, and cannot, because of the many rivers where water is always running; the oxen often have to go into the water up to their bellies. There are no footbridges or bridges; one has to go through. When one asks how far it is to this place or that, the answer is: so many days' journey or hours by ox wagon, and so and so many on horseback; by ox wagon twice as long as on horseback. With an ox wagon one gets as far here as with the ordinary post in Germany; the oxen have to go at a good pace, and they do so for 12 hours without stopping once. There are no inns where the driver can stop, so there is no delay. On horseback it goes very fast, almost always at a gallop; if the road is not too steep, it goes as fast uphill as downhill, and the horses are used to that. It was said above that the farmers have so many horses; someone might well say: what a lot of stables there must be, and timber is so scarce! The answer is: they have no stables, neither for horses nor oxen nor sheep; none of the cattle comes under a roof all year. They have a piece of ground fenced with a clay wall, or even just with poles, which they call a kraal, a separate one for each kind of stock: for all the horses, for all the oxen, for all the sheep. When they come from pasture they are driven into it. Horses and oxen often do not come home for 8 days or longer; when they are to be used, someone has to go and look for them; they are sometimes an hour away at pasture. Horses used only for riding come into a stable at certain times and get some grain to eat; the others get nothing all year but what they find for themselves.

<!-- lines 1074-1095 -->
Towards evening Baas Teunis drove home again. He and his wife did not want us to stay there, because we could not help anyway, and they feared we might fall ill, because we were not used to working in this country: working by day in the great heat (the heat is at its greatest now) and sleeping at night in the tent, we might catch an illness. "And what do you want to do? No Christian knows how to build a house here; Hottentots must do that; they understand it best." But we meant to make a start on something in the garden all the same, and wanted to be there; so he gave us a sheep to take with us, and some vegetables to cook. Then we lay down in the tent, but could sleep little.

<!-- lines 1096-1139 -->
Early on the 5th we had a Hottentot slaughter the sheep. Today the foundation had been dug and a start made on the walls. The Hottentots, who were to come to work at 5 o'clock in the morning, did not come until about 9. They said that they had first had to look for food; they would gladly help if only they had something to eat. One of the two [men] mentioned above [6] said it would be hard: when they go to the farmers and want something to eat, the farmers say that if they will work for them they will give them food, but not otherwise. He said that if we would pay him out of what the Hottentots earned with us, he would buy flour for bread for them; but if we gave the money to the Hottentots, he would get nothing for his flour. We promised to do this, and they were all satisfied with it. Today, 5 January, the foundation stone of our house was laid. The text was: "Nevertheless the Lord thy God turned the curse into a blessing unto thee, because the Lord thy God loved thee [Deuteronomy 23:5]. – If I have the Head for my friend and am beloved of God, what can the band of enemies and adversaries do to me?" And the doctrinal text: "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you; that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven [Matthew 5:44-45]. – Truly, that we may forgive our enemies from the bottom of our hearts."

<!-- lines 1139-1183 -->
Today old Lena, who was baptised by Brother Schmidt, also came to welcome us. Where she lives is an hour away from us; she had brought three more with her. She said that she had been the first to come to Georg Schmidt, and had been his cook; she had cooked for all who worked on his house. We asked where Schmidt had got the food to cook, and whether many had worked on his house. She said he had bought flour and barley for the people; there had been many who worked for him, and at that time there was no such poverty among the Hottentots as now; at that time they had many cattle, and there was meat and milk in plenty. She said further that at that time everything here had been full of Hottentots. We asked why they had moved away, then, and where they had gone. She said that after Georg Schmidt went away the Hottentots went to the farmers, and many died, and so this place became quite empty. When the house is finished she means to come and learn again. When one talks with her about Jesus, she is as ignorant as the others. It will be hard with this people. It is a curious thing: they all know Dutch here, but when one says anything Christian to them, it is as if one were speaking to them in a foreign language; they have never heard these or such words.

<!-- lines 1183-1230 -->
On Sunday the 6th we visited the man who means to buy flour for the others. Things look orderly with him; we bought milk from them. He also gave us some cucumbers and some *Staub* [?] [7]. He and his wife, and the children too, go properly clothed. Those who are respectable Hottentots go clothed, and the women too. Many of them will not work but lie in their huts all day. The clothing of the latter consists of nothing but a sheepskin, which they call a kaross [8] and hang over their shoulders. (In front they have a small skin hanging, with which they cover their private parts. When they do any work they take off the skin and so go quite naked, keeping on only the small skin in front of the lap.) In the afternoon 11 Hottentot women came again with their little children to visit us. They carry the children on their backs with the skin over them, so that only the head sticks out; under the child's back they tie it with a thong, so that it does not fall through. Almost every man and woman, when they come, carries a bag made from a steenbok [skin], which they strip off without cutting it open, so that a bag is ready at once. Each one carries this on her back, with tobacco, pipes and tinder box in it. When they come, they sit down on the ground; then one lights her pipe at once, takes a couple of puffs, then gives the pipe to the next, and so it goes round. When it is finished, another fills hers, and so they stay sitting for a whole half day. The men do the same.

<!-- lines 1230-1259 -->
On the 7th work began; 14 came to work. Today they began to prepare clay; this is some way from the house, as there is none near the house. It was done in the following way. One of the two Hottentots mentioned above took a plough, the kind used for ploughing, put 6 oxen to it and ploughed up a piece of ground. When that was done, a harrow was taken and hitched on, and the oxen were driven round in the clay until it was well and finely trodden. Then an ox hide was taken and 6 [other] oxen put to it, the clay put on the hide, and so dragged to where it was to go. Others made bricks [and] stones, and some began to build up the walls [9], a foot high every day; then it had to dry again for a day. Brother Kühnel was not quite well today. It was so hot by day and so cool at night, and there had been such a heavy dew, that the mattress in the tent was wet through and through.

<!-- lines 1260-1305 -->
On the 8th the Hottentots who are working for us came with their wives and children and set up their dwelling inside the old wall close by our house, to live there until the house is finished. On the 9th a baboon came right into the garden, climbed a peach tree and took peaches. A Hottentot noticed it. He had our gun, but it did not go off, and so the baboon ran away. Whether we shall be able to get anything from the trees in the garden we do not know. The pear tree Brother Schmidt planted had a lot of pears, but the baboons stole them before they were ripe. There are baboons here in great numbers; they come over the mountains in whole troops and make a fearful howling; it is astonishing how such an animal can scream. Old Lena visited us again today too. We now saw that Baas Teunis had told the truth, that we could not help here, and we were also afraid that it might come true that we should catch an illness, of which signs were already showing. We decided to go back to the post to Baas Teunis. His son drove there on the 10th; we went to him and drove with him. Baas Teunis has handed over the building and its supervision to his mason, who is a Dutchman. In the afternoon we arrived back at the post at Baas Teunis's. He and his wife were very glad that we had come back and had not stayed there to lose our health.

<!-- lines 1305-1358 -->
On 12 January, the congregation's Communion day, we talked together [about Holy Communion]; but we had to forgo it for the time we are here, until we come into our own house [10]. We do have a room of our own here, but we cannot be undisturbed in it for half an hour. How lively it is here is hardly to be believed: first the many slaves and slave children, then the constant visits of strangers. Not one day has been empty since we have been here. They stay one or two days, then go on again. What this costs Baas Teunis is astonishing; they eat and drink and hardly say thank you for it; that is the custom of the country. On these occasions we get to know many farmers and many farmers get to know us. Towards us they are fairly friendly; but how it is behind our backs is another matter. In Baas Teunis's presence they may not let it be seen. Baas Teunis says that we have few friends but many enemies. But it is no wonder that they are angry; many Hottentots are already saying that they will no longer work for the farmers but will go to us and learn. Who is to do the farmers' work if the Hottentots do not? They cannot buy so many slaves, for at one time they have much to do and at another less. When they have much work, they hire Hottentots by the day or by the week; as soon as the work is done they send them away again. That cannot be done with slaves; and many farmers are forbidden to buy slaves, because they have treated them so unmercifully; these must do all their work with Hottentots. Further into the country there are said to be farmers who have 20 to 30 Hottentots at work.

<!-- lines 1358-1392 -->
On the 14th a Hottentot came who had come from 16 days' journey away. He had been at the Cape with the commander and asked him for permission to come to us and learn. The commander sent him here to Baas Teunis and told him that he would find us here. He has run away from his master, whom he has served for 7 years. He also has 5 children whom he would like to fetch here [...] [11] people to learn; but he does not know how he is to get them. The children also serve with the farmer. If he goes back there now, he is in danger of the farmer punishing him. Baas Teunis advised him to go to Baviaanskloof and stay there for the time being; our house must be finished first before we could begin to teach. We must also say that when a Hottentot wants to move from one district to another, he must report it to the Landdrost to whose district the place belongs, or to the field corporal, and say why he wants to go there. The purpose of this is that no one who has done something bad in one place should escape punishment by going to another district.

<!-- lines 1392-1432 -->
On the 20th we received letters from Brother Hänsel from the Cape. He wished we had written to him in detail. We would gladly have done so too, if we had known that he would stay there so long. He wrote that he was to go on in 8 or 10 days. The messenger came late in the evening and left again early in the morning; and if one does it in three days, the Hottentot must be a good runner [12]. We wished very much, though, that Brother Hänsel could have come to us and seen us at our work, and our situation and our beginning; then he could have told our dear brothers and sisters in person how things are with us. In these days Brother Kühnel and Brother Schwinn were not quite well, but it is better again now. We thought it would be good if one of us were at the building of the house, since Baas Teunis's son is now building his house most of the time; so that one could eat and sleep at his place and go to our house every day. Brother Schwinn was busy putting up sauerkraut and beans and drying peaches for us for the winter; we get all this free from Baas Teunis. And Brother Kühnel is busy writing. So Brother Marsveld drove there with him, until one of us has time to relieve him.

<!-- lines 1433-1468 -->
On the 26th the Landdrost of Stellenbosch came here on a visit [13]. He told us that a letter had gone out from the government at the Cape to all landdrosts and field corporals that they were to see to it that no farmer might hold back a Hottentot who wanted to learn, or refuse to let him go; if this happened, it was to be severely punished and reported; much less might anyone put anything in our way. This Drost told us that if anything happened we should report it to him at once. Our place is under his command. The government has, as we hear from the Cape, issued three letters: one to the Landdrost at Swellendam, who is the greatest of the drosts, one to Baas Teunis, and one to us, which we enclose here [14]. On the 28th Baas Teunis received a letter from the Domine at Swellendam [15], in which he writes very seriously that we had not visited him. That we had not visited him? We did not know that there is a Domine there; and now that we know it, how are we to get there? It is more than two days' journey there, and to have a wagon, at least 8 days go by; that costs money and time.

<!-- lines 1468-1509 -->
In these 2 months that we have stayed here at the post we have heard much of what the farmers say; but mostly that they do not like the Hottentots being taught. A farmer who is a field corporal met a man who was a German on the road to the Cape. He asked the German whether the gentlemen were still at the post. Answer: yes. He said he had orders from the Landdrost to ride to us twice a week and ask whether we had anything to complain of. He said that he would leave that alone, because he got nothing out of it and had to live in fellowship with the farmers. Then he asked the two Hottentots who were driving the German's wagon whether they wanted to learn too. They said: "Yes, Baas." The farmer said: "Just you wait, you will get terrible beatings. I have heard that these people beat cruelly. They have brought a whole chest of rattan (cane) to beat you with." The Hottentots said: "We have seen with the farmers that when the children would not learn they got beatings, and so we must put up with it too." At that the farmer was silent. The Hottentots long very much to learn. God grant that it lasts!

<!-- lines 1509-1553 -->
All this while we took turns with one another at the building of our house, until 22 February. Since the room [?] [16] was now ready to move into, Brother Schwinn and Brother Kühnel moved in with the text: "With thy blessing let the house of thy servant be blessed for ever [2 Samuel 7:29]. – Hold thy pierced hands over the congregation too, and fulfil all the blessings that stir in thy heart for us!" And the doctrinal text: "He that overcometh shall inherit all things [Revelation 21:7]. – Lord, thy bloodshed, thy bitter suffering, and thy dying on the cross for me have earned it for me." Brother Marsveld stayed at the post until the rest of our things could be brought here. On the 25th a Hottentot captain from Graaff-Reinet [17] came to visit us. He was going to the Cape. This place is close to the Kaffers; it borders on the Kaffers [18]. One needs at least a month to travel there. A church for the Christians there has recently been built, and a minister has come there too. Today we had a great fright. The mason was plastering the chimney, and the Hottentot he had as his labourer fell 4 ells from the scaffold. He fell onto a stair [?] [19] below and has hurt himself; he cannot work. He was the most diligent and orderly of them all; he longs very much to learn. It is bad when something like this happens, because there is no one here who knows anything of healing. On the 26th Brother Marsveld came with the rest of the goods, and we now live together in our house.

## Notes

1. Johann Gottfried Haensel (Hänsel), a Moravian missionary on the Nicobar Islands, who returned to Europe by way of the Cape.
2. The transcription reads *Wir wieſen ihnen den lieben Heiland unter dem Birnbaum an*, "we showed them the dear Saviour under the pear tree", which reads oddly. It may mean that the missionaries spoke to them of the Saviour there, or a word may be missing. To be checked against the image.
3. *Quaimaſon*: the Cape Dutch *kwaai moesson*, "the bad monsoon", the winter season of rain and storms.
4. "Could do it": could afford to work at their own cost.
5. The transcription reads *Eine halbe Stunde weit Griſſen ſie, dicht dabey*; the normalised column has *gruben sie*, "they dug". The sentence makes no clear sense and may repeat the one before. To be checked against the image.
6. The transcription reads *Einer von den vorerwähnten Zeugen*, "one of the witnesses mentioned above"; probably a misreading of *zween*, "two", referring to the two men of mixed descent.
7. The transcription reads *etwas Gurken mit noch Staub*; *Staub* ("dust") is probably a misreading, perhaps of *Salat* or *Kraut*. To be checked against the image.
8. *Croſs*: the kaross, a cloak of skins.
9. *zu Klieben* in the transcription, "to cleave"; the normalised column has *mauern*, "to build walls". Perhaps *kleben*, to build up walls of wet clay.
10. The sentence is compressed. The missionaries seem to have discussed holding Holy Communion among themselves on the day of the congregation's Communion at home, and decided to wait until they had a house of their own.
11. A line is missing between the foot of page 25b and the top of page 26a; the transcription resumes with *leud zum lernen*, "people to learn". Line 1369 ends *hierher ho⸗*; lines 1370 and 1371 are empty.
12. The transcription reads *und wenn es einer in drey Tagen gehat*; the verb is unclear. The sense seems to be that the Khoekhoe messenger had made the journey to the Cape and back in three days.
13. The Landdrost of Stellenbosch at this time was probably Hendrik Lodewijk Bletterman. To be checked.
14. The copy of the government's letter enclosed with the diary is not in this file.
15. The minister of the Reformed church at Swellendam.
16. The transcription and the normalised column both read *Römer*, which is not a word in this sense. Perhaps *Kammer*, "room", or *unſer Haus*, "our house". To be checked against the image.
17. *Graß Pened* in the transcription: Graaff-Reinet, the frontier drostdy founded in 1786. Its first church was built in 1792.
18. *Kaffers*, the diarists' word for Xhosa people; see `glossary.csv`. Graaff-Reinet bordered on Xhosa country.
19. The transcription reads *Stienge*, the normalised column *Stiege*, "stair" or "steps". Perhaps *Stein*, "a stone". To be checked against the image.
