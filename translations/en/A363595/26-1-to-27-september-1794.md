Diary of the Brothers Marsveld, Schwinn and Kühnel at the Cape, from 1 to 27 September 1794.

<!-- lines 9875-9894 -->
Early on 1 September the one came whom the dear Saviour had assigned to us on 30 August as a candidate for baptism [1] (we had not yet been able to tell her, for she had been at a farmer's to fetch food and only came home yesterday evening). She said that she had dreamed the night before that children had come to her who told her that she should come to us; and so when she woke she could think of nothing else than to do it. We told her that the dear Saviour had given her permission to come to the little meeting and to belong among the candidates for baptism. [When she heard this] [2] she was very glad.

<!-- lines 9895-9912 -->
On the 2nd, after the children's school, we gave them a treat. We had a patch of white turnips in the garden which had almost all bolted, so that the turnips are only like a spindle; these we gave them [to take] [3]. It was a pleasure to watch, as some 50 children fell on them. Although there was little to them, it was still a great joy, because many of them have certainly eaten nothing for more than a day. Just now one can get little from the garden, for it is the month when everything runs to seed. Only now does the proper planting time begin. We planted long ago, but now that it is almost ready to eat, it runs to seed.

<!-- lines 9912-9931 -->
On the 3rd was the speaking of the candidates for baptism. We examined most of them, so that they might be able to explain themselves better in words; but one must have patience with that. It does lie on their hearts to live wholly for the dear Saviour. On the 4th a sick woman sent to ask Brother Kühnel to visit her. When he asked her whether she thought of the dear Saviour and his torment, she said: "I do, and I think that I believe in him; but I do not know it quite for certain." A candidate for baptism also came and complained that she did not love the dear Saviour as much as she would like to, and could not, although he had borne so much for the sake of our sins.

<!-- lines 9931-9966 -->
On the 5th 6 Hottentots were out hunting on horseback. In the afternoon a buck [4] came running about not far from our house. At once many Hottentots ran after it; they came and asked for our guns, and we gave them all three. Then those on horseback came after it over the mountain. One of them, our Rahel's husband, shot it so that it dropped on the spot. Then the others came up on horseback and fell on the buck at once and wanted to keep it for themselves, and the man who had shot it was to get none of it. He and Brother Jacob came to us at once and told us. We told them to go at once and tell them to bring the buck here to us. They came, and had laid it on a horse, but had already taken out the entrails. We told them that now they should skin it; the skin should be ours, because we had given the guns, the powder and the shot, and more than one shot had been fired; and they should divide the meat among themselves: all who had hunted must have some. With that they were all content. Later came the heathen who had hunted, among them also one of ours, who had followed it on foot for four hours [5] and had not left it until it was shot. He got his good share.

<!-- lines 9966-10025 -->
On the 6th we were busy planting in our garden, which gives us much work now, because everything must be planted now. Early on Saturday the 7th [6] 8 Hottentot women and 6 men came from the Breede River, which is 3 days' journey on horseback from here. They have a great wish to move here, and could not wait any longer. But they have heard much that is bad about us from the farmers, who spread it about us [7], so they wanted to come themselves to see and to hear. There were so many people at the meeting that it was not possible to hold it in the house, because only the smallest part could have got in. The singing meeting on Friday we also held in front of the house, because the house was much too small. There was moonlight; when it is dark we cannot do that. It is the same with the meetings, especially on Sundays: when the sun shines, we cannot well sit in the hot sun without a hat, and when it is windy, the wind drives the dust and sand so hard that that will not do either. At midday our Hottentots went to those who had come here this morning to ask them why they had really come. They answered that they liked it very much, and found and heard things quite different from what the farmers had told them; they had come to hear God's word. Towards evening they all came to us and said that they liked it very much, for they would gladly hear God's word, and the 8 women wanted to stay here at once if we allowed it. But the men wanted to go home again after a few days, [to fetch] their cattle and wives [8] and children, and several more of their friends who also wanted to hear God's word here, and had waited there until they had news from them whether the bad things said about us were true; then they would all live here together. This we gladly allowed them, and said that all who wanted to become children of God and to live according to the mind of Christ could come; for that was why we had come here, to tell the poor Hottentots the way to salvation.

<!-- lines 10025-10055 -->
On the 8th 3 more women came who complained of the unrest [9] of their hearts. The 8 women came to school today, asked for books and said that they had a great wish to learn. 2 new children also came to school. On the 9th another woman came with 3 children, of whom the smallest is about 10 years old. She had lived in the Brandveld [10] (that is 7 days' journey from here), and asked leave to stay here, to hear and learn God's word with her children. 3 more women also came who did not know where to turn for unrest of heart. In this night and the one before, a tiger [11] has been in our garden and devoured a cat there; we are afraid that he will get among our cows. To lie in wait for him and shoot him is too dangerous, for if he is not well hit, the man who fired is in trouble: as soon as the shot is fired, he is said to spring on the man. And setting a gun [as a trap] does not do in the garden, because instead of the tiger it might hit a dog; for one must put meat there, and the dogs smell that too, for the place is full of dogs.

<!-- lines 10055-10077 -->
On the 10th we began to raise the foundation of our new little house a little above the ground in masonry, because then one can soon begin to build up [the walls] with clay, since the rainy season is nearly over. In the evening our sister Sophia came and asked forgiveness for having come late to the meeting. She said: "I was fetching wood and was late because of it." Question: why did she not send her girl for wood? She should keep her at work. Answer: she was not at home today. Question: where was she? Answer: she sat on the mountain among the rocks all day and wept. Question: about what? Answer: she was so uneasy in her heart, and so troubled that our dear Saviour had had to suffer and bear so much for the sake of our sins; and so she [Sophia] had not seen her [12]. The girl is about 14 years old.

<!-- lines 10077-10114 -->
(On the 11th a son of Marcis [13] came to visit us. He stayed over midday; it seemed he would like to stay longer. We had to excuse ourselves that we must get on with our work, for we had no time to spend with him. In the afternoon 2 of us must hold school, one for the men, the other for the women, and the third has his hands full of work. The women's school is now so large that our room or chamber will no longer hold those who are still able to learn [14].) On the 12th towards midday we had the joy of welcoming our friend Martin Schmidt with his dear wife and one of his sons. Young Mrs Kuns [15] came with them, and her son too. These drove off again towards evening, and Father Schmidt's son with them, to go hunting there with Kuns's son, because he is a great lover of it. On the 13th a farmer also came to visit us. On Sunday the 14th, after the meeting, we drove with Father and Mother Schmidt in our cart to the kraals an hour from here, which he wanted to see. He went, or rather crept, into the houses and looked at everything. On the 15th we let the women's school lapse, because one cannot move, and more come every day; for on Saturday another large family moved here. Father and Mother Schmidt attended the children's school in the morning.

<!-- lines 10114-10174 -->
Early on the 16th they left us again. We had them driven to the post with our oxen (we borrowed a wagon from a Bastard), and he had ordered his horses to be there. From there he goes to visit his son, who lives 2 days' journey away. "I am amazed," he said, "at the word of God among the Hottentots. I had heard much, and I believed that the Spirit of God proves itself mightily in the Hottentots; but as it really is, no one can believe it. One must see it oneself, and then one's understanding stands still at the eagerness with which they sit in the meeting to hear the word of God, and the emotion in it is astonishing; my understanding stands still at these things too." On 3 evenings a crowd of children sat together and sang, and he and his wife could not hear enough of it. He said: "I have been far about in the world and have heard much, but never anything like this in my life, for it sounds as if the sound came from heaven." Yesterday evening, as soon as he heard it, he said: "I would like to go there to see them." It was very dark, cold too, and a good way [16] from our house; there they all sat together on the ground. He said: "These are heathen, and they sing of the torment and death of Jesus; we must be ashamed." Of the narrowness of the place he said: "This will not do; you must have a church." We replied that we had long seen the need and had wished for it, but that we could not take this cost on ourselves until we had permission from our brothers in the fatherland. He said very often: "If I had the means that I wish, a church would soon stand, for that would be a work of God. When I get back to the Cape I will see whether I cannot find people there who will take a share in it; I will not be the one who gives the smallest share." He also said: "I wish I might have the good fortune to see Brother Reichel [17] here. If he comes, I will bring him; then I would come again at once."

<!-- lines 10174-10211 -->
Brother Schwinn drove with him to the post to speak with Baas Teunis about sheep, for until now we have not been able to get any. There is to be an auction at Juffrouw Lind's [18] later this month, and a large flock of sheep will be sold there too. So we thought of asking Baas Teunis to buy for us if they are not too dear. He promised, but believes that they will be driven too high. In the evening Brother Schwinn came home again. He told us that Father Schmidt had spoken with Baas Teunis at once about a church. But Baas Teunis advised against it, and said that we should give it up and not build much. "For," he said, "you cannot keep this place; you must have a bigger one. If it goes on like this, how are the people to support themselves? They will starve together." He said that very many more from far up country wanted to move to us and would not be kept back by anything. He said: "Lately I tried to dissuade a party, and told them that they would starve there; they should stay where they were, for the sickness was there too, of which so many die, and it would go the same way with them. They gave me the answer: 'Even if we die, we shall have heard God's word.'" So our hope of building the church is gone again.

<!-- lines 10211-10244 -->
(On the 17th the speaking of the baptised was to have been held [19]; but since time was so short, we let it lapse and announced in the meeting of the baptised that those who wanted to speak with us could come in these days. On the 18th 7 women came who had moved here on the 13th, and said that they had come so far to hear God's word, for which they had had a very great longing. Now they had heard something of it, and it had made them so uneasy in their hearts; what could that be? Question: what were they uneasy about? Answer: that our dear Lord God had had to suffer so much. This led to a fine conversation. In the afternoon they all came to school and asked for books, because they had a great wish to learn; they are all still young. A man also came who had not been able to sleep the whole night for unrest of heart. On the 19th 2 girls came, about 12 years old, who said that they were so troubled that Jesus Christ had had to bear and suffer so much for the sake of their sins, and they too would gladly become children of God.

<!-- lines 10245-10314 -->
We also received a letter from Mr Schönberg at the Cape [20], who wrote among other things that he wished that one of us would come to the Cape and stay there some days, since he believed that this would be very good for us, humanly speaking, and also for several people of their society; for it was wished that one [of us] would come. This was a strange letter, which gives us much to think about. Our thoughts mostly run on whether [we must not move] from our place [21]; for Baas Teunis's [talk] has lately been so peculiar, as if the government had already been thinking of another place for us. But nothing definite can be got out of him beyond what has already been mentioned, namely that we should give up building the church. That he knows more is quite certain. If only he were honest with us! But he is not, as we have already found in many things. We are in the greatest perplexity; if we only had one honest friend, we should be happy. To leave this place now, on which we have spent so much money and trouble, and to turn our backs on the garden from which we are now getting something to eat, is no small matter. We spent so much trouble and money here on this place because we believed that it was rightly ours. For all who know the law say of what we have in writing from the authorities about this place that it could not be more secure; even the commander at the Cape [22] said: "If this is not valid, nothing is valid." We do not really believe that they will take this place from us. Who could have thought that the dear Saviour would bless us with so many people hungry for the Gospel? There is a crowd of people, and for [the purpose] for which we came into the country [23] this place is certainly much too small, especially as many more are to be expected. What shall we do if the government now offers us another place? Our wish to have one of our dear brothers of the Unity Elders [Conference], and superiors, with us, to make use of his good counsel, grows greater every day. If only we at least had an answer to our letter about this place! What else shall we do but ask our dear Saviour to counsel and help us in all circumstances according to his heart, as he has done until now, so that he may carry out thy [24] purposes of peace with us and these poor Hottentots? That is and shall remain our prayer.

<!-- lines 10314-10331 -->
On the 20th, in the conference, we put a girl of about 14 before our dear Lord as a candidate for baptism, and he approved her. The man who, as already said, was accused of having poisoned [25] a slave also died today. He had not yet been to the Cape, for his child was ill, and when the child recovered, he fell ill. In these days he had sent for Brother Marsveld; he put to him [his wish] to be saved [26], and believed that if he died he must be lost. He directed him to the love of Jesus.

<!-- lines 10331-10377 -->
Our sister Maria also came in great perplexity and said that she could no longer keep it quiet but must tell us: the farmers were all very angry with us. The reason was this, that we forbid the Hottentots to drink, to whore, to dance and so on; and they had said: "If all these must go to hell, then we Christians must all go in, for we do all that; and then hell would be much too small. But we will all get together and soon make an end of these people, and of the Hottentots too." We told her she should just be calm; without the dear Saviour's will nothing bad could befall either them or us. It is true that one will find very few [farmers] who do not do these things, for the farmers practise whoring with the Hottentot women not secretly but openly; that is no shame, much less a sin. It is true that the farmers are very hostile to us; but if one of them comes to us, or one of us to them, they are all very friendly; when we are gone, then they fall upon us. In these days our sister Sara was at a farmer's whom until now we had believed to be our friend, and also a very near neighbour (by his words). He said to Sara: "You people must not believe what these men tell you, for it is all lies." When we asked the Hottentots what kind of man he was, we got the answer: "He whores openly with the Hottentot women, and yet he has a wife and a large troop of children; he was born in Berlin." We thought: if he lives like that, one can well make sense of what he said.

<!-- lines 10377-10412 -->
This evening we held the first lovefeast with our 4 communicants, before Holy Communion; and in the evening we partook of this great good [27] for the strengthening of body and soul. Early on the 21st the farmer Harme came to visit us and to attend the meeting, which was very well attended. In the afternoon the man who died yesterday was buried. His [wife] [28] came this morning and said that as long as he could speak he had asked Jesus Christ for mercy, and he had told her that if he should die she should go to us and ask us to pray to Jesus Christ for him, that he would have mercy on his poor soul. In the afternoon a sick woman sent to ask that one of us would come to her; she longed to see one of us. Brother Kühnel went. She said that she had had a very great longing to hear a word of God, and had not been able to bear it any longer at her place. Now she was here and had heard his word often enough in the meeting, but had sat in front of the house far from the door and could not understand everything; but what she had felt at it she could not say; and she was now asking Jesus Christ for mercy. She moved here only a short time ago.

<!-- lines 10412-10428 -->
On the 22nd some 30 women went to the farmers to weed [29] the corn (or wheat, for rye and [other grain] one has little here, and what there is is not used for bread); for this work a day labourer here gets a shilling and food. But they are so much in debt to the farmers that they get no money for it; it only reduces the debt somewhat, and when they came home they had not a mouthful of food. The women's school was small. We thought that there would be room to spare in the meeting, but many still had to sit outside the door.

<!-- lines 10429-10474 -->
On the 23rd a couple more women came, and also a child, who complained of the unrest of their hearts. A woman also came who moved here a short time ago. She and her husband had conceived a strong wish [to live] here [30], and had been in service with a farmer. Their time was up; then he asked the farmer [leave] to go away from him and move to us, and to pick out his cattle, which had been running with the farmer's. The farmer allowed him this. When the Hottentot afterwards had picked out his cattle and wanted to bring them here, the farmer was at his second place. So the Hottentot drove off with his cattle towards us. When the farmer learned of it, he became so angry that he sent "Christian" men [31] after him, each with a gun, who told the Hottentot that if he did not turn back at once they would shoot him down. What could the poor Hottentot do but turn back? Then the farmer told him that because he had done this he must serve another year. The woman said that now she had nothing to live on. We told her that we could not advise her in this case; she should go to Baas Teunis and ask him for advice. That is how the farmers live with the poor Hottentots. Those who still have a few head of cattle, if they want to leave the farmers, must run away and let their cattle and wages go, unless they want a good few blows [32] into the bargain. It has happened more than once that when they asked for their wages they got a back full of blows instead of wages; and a Hottentot may not speak against a Christian.

<!-- lines 10474-10512 -->
In the evening, during the singing meeting, a Hottentot house caught fire. When the singing meeting was over they ran, and it was put out after all. The woman was in the singing meeting and the man was not at home; only small children were there, who had made too big a fire, and a girl was burnt by it. On the 24th towards midday 6 gentlemen came on horseback from Stellenbosch, who were going to the auction at young Mrs Lind's, about 4 German miles from here. One was Botha [33], the second the secretary, one Mr Morgel; the other 3 we did not know. One of them had not long come here from Holland; he said that he had only just arrived [34]. They asked the question in what the Brethren and the Reformed differ: whether it lies only in ceremony, or in the articles of faith? They were given information about it, to their satisfaction. The one who had just come from Holland, and another who, as far as one could tell, was a doctor, wanted to know much about the arrangements of our congregations in our fatherland; but they had quite wrong ideas. After a stay of a good hour they rode on. The auction mentioned will be large, especially in livestock; for there are still over 200 cows, as many horses, still more oxen, and also over 1000 sheep, and that is only one farmer's widow [35].

<!-- lines 10512-10547 -->
On the 25th we sent 2 Hottentots to young Mrs Lind's, to bring the sheep, of which Baas Teunis is buying some for us. If we get none there, we do not know how we are to come by sheep. Since in this time we had thought and spoken much about the letter from Brother Schönberg, and about what we have heard here (of which we have already spoken), we resolved that one of us should make the journey to the Cape as soon as possible; namely, Brother Schwinn should ride there with a Hottentot, to see and hear how things stand. On the evening of the 26th those we had sent for sheep came home again. Baas Teunis sent us word that he had bought no sheep, because they had been driven far too high. That sheep, indeed all livestock, are very dear is because of the Kaffers [36], who have taken away or killed all the farmers' cattle in that region; for the farmers in that region are said to have had astonishingly many cattle, and to have brought large herds here and to the Cape for sale, so that it was very cheap. This is now lacking, and the Kaffers are not yet quiet. It seems that we are not to get any sheep.

<!-- lines 10547-10562 -->
3 more children came to school. Since the schools of both the women and the children are so large (for each is about 60 strong), we have appointed a woman in the women's school and a girl in the children's, and this arrangement works well. There are 25 men here at present who go to school; the others are [away] at the Cape [37]. There are many more young men than women here who are too old and do not come to school, but come diligently to the meetings [38]. Some, especially of the women and of the children, are now beginning to read quite nicely.

<!-- lines 10562-10589 -->
On the 27th we resolved that Brother Schwinn should set out on the journey to the Cape tomorrow, if the weather allows (for today is a day of rain), and take this diary with him, in the hope that a ship will soon go to Europe. We wish that this, as well as what we have already sent to you, dear brothers, may all come into your hands; as we also long for reports from you, dear brothers. According to the rumours one hears here about the French, things must look sad in Europe. How are the dear brothers and sisters at Neuwied, then, and the dear congregation at Zeist? Now, dear brothers! From this diary you will see that the word of God is still going on in blessing, which we commend, as we do ourselves, to the prayers and remembrance of all the congregations before our dear Lord. Hendr. Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn, Johann Christn Kühnel.

5. The transcription reads *vier paar Stūnden*, "four couple of hours"; the normalised column has "four hours".
6. 7 September 1794 was a Sunday, which fits the large meeting; *Samſtags*, "on Saturday", may be a misreading of *Sonntags*. To be checked against the image.
7. The transcription reads *das ſie uns nachjagen*, "which they chase after us"; probably *nachſagen*, "say of us".
8. The transcription reads *Haū⸗ſen*; probably *Frauen*, "wives", since the eight women were staying.
9. The transcription reads *über die Urſache ihres Herzens*, "about the cause of their hearts"; probably *Unruhe*, "unrest", as in similar entries.
10. *Brand Veld*, a district seven days' journey away; not identified.
11. *Tiger*: the leopard (see `glossary.csv`). *Finte ſtellen* is probably *Flinte ſtellen*, to set a gun as a trap, with meat as bait.
12. The transcription reads *darūm habe ſie ſie nicht geſehen*, "therefore she had not seen her"; the sense seems to be that Sophia had not seen her daughter all day and so had to fetch the wood herself.
13. *Marcis* is not identified.
14. The parenthesis opened before "On the 11th" is not closed in the transcription; it is closed here, at the end of the day's entry. The parentheses round some passages in these diaries may mark matter for leaving out of copies.
15. *Kūnze*, probably the family of Christian Kuns (see `glossary.csv`; also *Chr. Kune* in the August diary).
16. The transcription reads *ein gūt Wind von unſerm Haūße*, "a good wind from our house"; probably *ein gut Stück Weges*, "a good way", or similar. To be checked against the image.
17. Probably Johann Friedrich Reichel of the Unity Elders' Conference, whose address was read on 13 August (entry 24). Why Father Schmidt hoped to see him at the Cape is not said; perhaps Reichel had passed through the Cape on a journey to the Moravian missions in the East. To be checked.
18. *Jūffroūwd Lind*, called "young Mrs Lind" on the 24th; perhaps of the family of Field Cornet Linde (see `glossary.csv`). The July diary mentions her too (entry 22, note 19).
19. The parenthesis opened before "On the 17th" is not closed in the transcription. It is left open here, as in the manuscript, since it is not clear where it should end.
20. The transcription has *von der Caß* here and throughout this entry, a misreading of *Caap*. On Mr Schönberg, see entry 22, note 23.
21. The transcription reads *als ob nicht weg an unſerm Platz iſt*, which is not clear; the context (Baas Teunis's hints, the fear of losing the place) suggests "whether [we must not move] from our place". The next clause reads *Baas Teunis ſeine Andern*, probably *Reden*, "talk". To be checked against the image.
22. *der Befehlshaber an der Caß*, the commander at the Cape, probably the acting head of the government, Commandant Rhenius (see `glossary.csv`).
23. The transcription reads *und der weil ben wir ja hier ins Land gekommen ſind*; the sense seems to be "for the purpose for which we came here into the country".
24. *Deine*, "thy", addresses the Saviour directly in mid-sentence, as in the manuscript.
25. *vergeben*, in the older sense "poisoned". The man has not been mentioned before in the surviving text; the earlier mention was probably in the lost part of the August diary.
26. The transcription reads *er lies ihm vorlegen uns Seliger werden*, which is garbled; perhaps *er legte ihm ſein Verlangen, ſelig zu werden, vor*, "he put to him his wish to be saved".
27. The transcription reads *genoſſen wir dieſes ſo ſehr Gut*, "we partook of this so very good"; probably *dieſes große Gut*, the sacrament.

31. The transcription has *„Chriſten" Menſchen*, with quotation marks, and *ſchalt*, "scolded", probably for *ſchickte*, "sent". The colonists called themselves "Christians" to set themselves apart from Khoekhoe and slaves; the diarists' quotation marks seem to question the name.
32. The transcription reads *einen Parel Schläge*; probably *ein paar*, "a few", or *einen Buckel voll*, as in the next sentence.
33. Botha was a common family name in the Stellenbosch district; this man is not identified. The secretary was probably the secretary of the Stellenbosch district (drostdy). *Hr. Morgel* is not identified.
34. The transcription reads *daß er erſt noch aus Abgereiſt ſey*, "that he had only just set out"; the sense is probably that he had only just arrived.
35. The transcription has *eine Baurs⸗Frau*, "a farmer's wife"; since her property is being auctioned, she was probably a widow. The auction is at young Mrs Lind's.
36. *Kaffern*: the diarists' word for Xhosa people; it later became a severe racial slur in South Africa. The translation keeps it because the manuscript has it; see `glossary.csv`. The passage refers to the fighting on the eastern frontier of the colony in 1793, in which Xhosa and colonists raided each other's cattle.
37. The transcription reads *die andern ſind auf der Weide auf der Caß*, "the others are at pasture at the Cape". *auf der Weide* is not clear. Men from the mission were then serving at the Cape (see entry 10, July 1793, and entry 13).
38. The sentence is contradictory as it stands (*Es ſind viel mehr junge Männer als Frauen hier, welche aber zu alt ſind, und nicht in die Schule, aber in die Verſammlungen fleißig kommen*). It may mean that many men and women are too old for school but come to the meetings.
