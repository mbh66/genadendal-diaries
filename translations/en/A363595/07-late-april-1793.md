[...] [1] of the man made a blessed exposition and explanation. 30 persons had gathered for it. He asked me how the Hottentots were shaping. When I told him that so far we have the best hopes, he showed great joy at it. He asked us to visit him; he has visited us already. If it were not so far, we would do it too; but it takes two days on horseback to get there. Afterwards someone came to me who said that his mother had been at the Cape, and Martinus Schmidt had given her a little parcel for us, which had come from the fatherland. "From the fatherland?" I said; "that can hardly be." He assured me that it was from the fatherland, and that he had handed it to Baas Teunis. I went and fetched it, cut it open eagerly, and saw the weekly news, and also congregation news, as well as letters from Zeist. Although it was already late, I could not stay there [2], but resolved to ride home at once. Baas Teunis and the Juffrouw were not happy about it, but none of this could keep me from sharing my joy with my brothers. It was quite unexpected for all of us; we had often reckoned it out and expected nothing before August. So we were very glad and thankful for it. From a letter from Brother Rothe we learn of the homegoing of our dear Brother Joseph [3]. On the 22nd the captain mentioned above [4] was in the meeting again with his people; after it he thanked us, took his leave, and now meant to continue his journey.

<!-- lines 2907-2945 -->
On the 23rd we had a visit from a farmer. He asked for permission to take one of the Hottentots who live here into work for some time. He was a [...] (on horseback) [5] from here to his home. None of the Hottentots goes away, even if it is only a couple of hours from here, without coming to ask permission; this surprises us, since we have told no one to do so. It is the same with the children in school: if one of them has to go out (for an important reason), each one first asks permission. On the 24th we were busy painting the windows and doors of our house. On the 25th: in these days we were busy reading the news we had received, and were very thankful to the dear Saviour that he had let it reach us safely. We can only read in the evening, though, since we are busy with schools all day; for as soon as one is over the next begins, and so all three of us are never together during the day. In the weekly news we found the painful news that the dear congregations of Gnadenfrei and Gnadenfeld have been struck by so great a disaster by fire, the first on 4 August and the second on 17 September [6]. We implored our dear Lord that he himself would comfort all these dear brothers and sisters and help them again.

<!-- lines 2945-2979 -->
On the 26th: when school or a meeting was to begin, we had to call the pupils each time, or have them called, because they have no clock. This was troublesome. We had bought a piece of sheet brass at the Cape, thinking we could use it in the house for various things. We made a bell out of it. It had to be done in three hours, so as to get a sound [from it] in the evening within hearing [7]. We worked at it together for those hours, and now we have a bell. We have fastened it to a pole and set it up high, and it has now been in use for eight days and is of good service. Our dear brothers and sisters will think: a bell made in three hours, that must ring beautifully! It is true; but we think: let it ring as it will, so long as it does its service. On the 27th we were busy (because it was Saturday and so there was no school) planting potatoes and sugar peas. So far we still have fine warm weather, but at night it is cool. The rainy season is late this year; they say that here it usually begins in the middle of this month (April). Altogether it has been a very dry year, and rain is much longed for, so that grass may grow for the cattle.

<!-- lines 2980-3076 -->
On the 28th the mason who did the masonry on our house was in church with us for the second time. He seemed moved, and asked that he might come again on Sundays. He said he meant to change and lead a better life. He works an hour from here, but will soon go away from here to work elsewhere. He is a Dutchman, but has been here for many years and has led a bad life until now. When we had eaten our midday meal we felt like going for a walk, into the forest, which was said to be an hour from our house in the mountains. We took the Hottentot we have in our service with us, because these people know their way well in the mountains. Each of us took a gun on his back, since we thought we might perhaps meet something to shoot, perhaps baboons or wolves. Directly behind our house the way goes fairly steeply uphill. With much climbing we came to the forest, which is very small, however. There are strong trees in it, but not tall, and all crooked; it would be good for firewood, but it is impossible to get it out. One can hardly get there on foot; how is one to get there with oxen and drag it out? When we came near the forest, the Hottentot said: "I must whistle, to see whether there is a bird here that will bring us to a bees' nest." He had not whistled long when a bird came to the edge of the forest and whistled too. "Now," said the Hottentot, "we must follow the bird; it will bring us to a bees' nest." We did so; the bird always flew a couple of trees ahead of us. When we had gone some way, it stayed sitting at the top of a tree and whistled like a person. "Here there will be a nest," said the Hottentot. We examined the tree; the tree was hollow, and there really was a bees' nest in it. The Hottentot said: "If only we had fire, so that we could take the honey out!" But we had brought no tinder box, and nothing to put the honey in if we got it out. The Hottentot said: "That is no trouble; we take bark [8] from the trees, lay the honey on it, and carry it home on that. If only we had fire!" We fired off a gun, took the cord on which the powder horn hung over our shoulders, cut [9] it open, poured powder on the pan, and so made fire. Then the Hottentot climbed up; the nest was about six ells high. He took a charred piece of wood that was smoking and smoked the bees away with it. But when it came to taking out the honey, it turned out to be a young swarm that had not long begun to bring in honey, and what was in it we could very well eat up on the spot. In this way, though, there is often a very rich haul, and one often gets a great deal. Further into the country, where there is more forest, the farmers fetch whole casks full. They all make use of this bird; it is called the tell-tale [10]. But one must take good care, for if this bird knows where a wolf is lying, it will lead a person there too; but if it knows neither wolf nor bees' nest, it is said to lead no one. When it has brought someone to a beehive and the honey is out, then it claims its share [?] [11] and is content with that. On this walk we saw nothing we could have shot.

<!-- lines 3076-3091 -->
On the 29th a farmer named Conrad, from two hours away, visited us. On the 30th a couple of Bastard Hottentots were here. (These are people who have a Christian for a father and a Hottentot woman for a mother. One can tell them by their hair: the real Hottentots have short woolly hair, but the Bastards have proper long hair.) They said that where they live there are many Bastards who would like to come here to learn; they only wanted first to try to earn something, so that they would have something to live on here; and over the summer they would be here too. In the evening they were in the meeting as well.

## Notes

1. The text resumes at page 10 of the second diary (image 64a), in the middle of a sentence. Pages 1 to 9, from 1 April 1793, are missing; see entry 06, note 4. From the context, the dates on the next page (the 22nd and 23rd), and the Sunday meeting, the passage probably belongs to about 21 April. The man who "made a blessed exposition" and then asked about the Khoekhoe was perhaps a minister or a visiting farmer; he is not named on this page.
2. The transcription reads *ſo kunte die Mägdt nicht da bleiben*, "the maid could not stay there"; the next sentences show that it was the writer who would not stay the night at Baas Teunis's but rode home to share the news. *die Mägdt* is probably a misreading. To be checked against the image.
3. *Bruder Joſeph*: August Gottlieb Spangenberg, bishop of the Moravian Church, known in the Church as Brother Joseph. He died at Berthelsdorf on 18 September 1792. Brother Rothe has not been identified.
4. The captain is mentioned on one of the missing pages.
5. The transcription reads *Er war ein Pagraniſe (zu Pferde) von hier zu Hauß*; the normalised column has *Reiter*, "rider". The word is not clear; the sense may be that the farmer lived a certain distance from here on horseback. To be checked against the image.
6. Gnadenfrei and Gnadenfeld, Moravian congregations in Silesia. The fires were in 1792.
7. The transcription reads *um es Abends und in hören einen Klücker zu kriegen*, which is garbled. The sense seems to be that the bell was made quickly, in three hours, to have something that could be heard at a distance.
8. The transcription reads *Rieſen*; the normalised column has *Rinden*, "bark".
9. The transcription has *ſchleudten* at the foot of page 16 and *ſchneidten* at the head of page 17; the normalised column has "flung". "Cut" follows the second reading.
10. *Verklicher* in the transcription: the Dutch *verklikker*, "tell-tale", a name for the honeyguide bird. The normalised column has *Verweiser*.
11. The transcription reads *dann ſpricht er das Verborgenbleibenn*, which is garbled; the normalised column has "it declares its claim". The honeyguide feeds on the wax and grubs left in the opened nest. To be checked against the image.
