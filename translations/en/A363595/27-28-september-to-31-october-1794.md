Diary of the three Brothers Marsveld, Schwinn and Kühnel at the Cape, from 28 September to 31 October 1794.

<!-- lines 10600-10621 -->
Early on 28 September Brother Schwinn set out for the Cape with one of our Hottentots, accompanied by our heartfelt good wishes. The meeting at 10 o'clock was very well attended. Brother Marsveld spoke on Matthew 7, verses 15 to 24, and the Spirit of God proved himself powerfully in the hearts of these people. On Sundays we now usually speak on the Sunday Gospel. Today the Gospel was Matthew 6:24 [and following], where it says: "Take no thought what ye shall eat, or what ye shall drink," and so on. But we have to pass over these texts, for the Hottentots take no thought for the next morning. If they have anything to eat, they eat it up all at once if they can, and do not keep it for the next day; we must rather commend thrift to them.

<!-- lines 10622-10662 -->
On the 29th an old woman came who had been ill, and was still so weak that she could hardly reach our house with her stick. She said: "I have long wished that I could come here to speak with the teachers. I have come so far to hear God's word and to live by it, since God has shown us such great mercy and sent us teachers (she has moved here from about 20 German miles away). Since I came here I have heard God's word twice, for I fell ill. Now I would gladly die thankful, and thank our dear [Lord] [1] for his great love and mercy. But there is an old man in the kraal where I am, who has also moved here. He has nothing but bad talk and [knows] of nothing but bad things, and makes so much [noise] [2] that I cannot possibly bear it any longer; for now I want to think about our God, and can no longer listen to such things; and so I [have taken] heart to say this." We said that she should tell this man to come to us at once; we would speak with him. He came at once; he is lame and must walk with a stick. We asked him why he had moved here. He answered: to hear God's word. We put his behaviour to him and asked whether he wanted to serve two masters, and said that if he wanted to serve the devil he could not live here, but could go back where he had come from. He was silent and began to weep.

<!-- lines 10662-10685 -->
At midday a sick man sent to ask that one of us would visit him. Brother Marsveld went. He asked him among other things whether he had anything to say to him; he said no. Question: but if he should die now, did he know where his poor soul would go? Answer: "I pray that the dear Saviour will have mercy on me." Question: had he heard in the meeting that he must pray to the dear Saviour? Answer: "I have not yet been in any meeting; I fell ill as soon as I came here. Those who go to the meeting and visit me have told me that I should ask the dear Saviour for the forgiveness of my sins, and I do this." In the evening Brother Kühnel read the 5th and 6th chapters of Mark in the meeting.

<!-- lines 10685-10718 -->
On the 30th several came who complained of the unrest of their hearts. Our sisters [3] Anna and Sophia also came. The first said: "I am often very perplexed about myself, for I feel so bad that I believe it is all up with me. I never believed that I was as bad as I now feel, and every day I feel worse." She was directed to the dear Saviour, to whom no sinner is too bad. The Holy Spirit is going to work very thoroughly with her, for she believed that she was good by nature and much better than other people. Sophia said that for some time she had been very content. Question: about what? Answer: "When I think about what our dear Saviour has borne for us, I feel such a joy in my heart. Then I wanted to ask you: when I lie down to sleep, I ask the dear Saviour to let me lie in thy [wounds] [4]. Is that praying rightly?" She was told that she should pray quite simply; the dear Saviour does not listen only to the words, but looks at the heart and what it desires.

<!-- lines 10718-10764 -->
On 1 October the speaking of the candidates for baptism was to have been held, but we let it lapse this time and announced in their meeting that those who wanted to speak with us could come to one of us. Then a sick woman sent to ask that one of us would visit her. Brother Kühnel went. He asked her how she was. Answer: "I always think of our dear Father, and ask for grace and mercy." He commended to her the love of Jesus for poor sinners. Many others were present, and a truly blessed feeling prevailed. Several of the candidates for baptism also came and spoke very openly with us. The Hottentots also brought us a young crane [5] to give us pleasure. But this kind of bird is very fierce and bites people, especially children, and they grow very big. This one was still very young and had no feathers yet, but its legs were already 23 inches long [6]; each step it took was 2 feet. When it is full grown it is nearly as tall as a man, like the ostrich. We slaughtered it, and it made good soup; its meat tasted like chicken. On the 2nd towards midday young Mrs Mareis [7], who lives 2 German miles from here, came to visit us with one of her sons and her sister, who lives at Stellenbosch. All 3 were on horseback; they were very friendly. Unfortunately we had to let the schools of the men and the women lapse, because they stayed until 3 o'clock. On the 3rd several came again who were very uneasy. 2 of the candidates for baptism also came and asked with many tears for holy baptism. The singing meeting, which Brother Kühnel held, we had to hold in the house. There was moonlight, but it was cold. Even when it is hot by day, the evenings are often not only cool but cold.

<!-- lines 10764-10773 -->
Towards evening on the 4th Brother Schwinn arrived back with us, safe and well. We found much cause to thank the dear Saviour with him for all the protection he had experienced on this journey. He writes of it and of his business as follows:

<!-- lines 10774-10826 -->
"I set out from my dear brothers on 28 September with a Hottentot, and at 3 o'clock we came to the Butter River [8], which was still very high, so that the water came up to the horses' saddles; but we got across safely. In the evening we came to a farmer named Guber, to spend the night there. I asked him whether I could not stay the night for money and good words. He asked: 'Where do you come from?' Answer: 'From Baviaanskloof.' Then I got no further answer. He did not ask me to dismount and come into the house, which is the custom here and the first thing that is done. I got off my horse and went into the house, but no one spoke a word to me; the man went away and left me sitting. I asked the wife to allow me a little place in her house and to give my horse fodder; I would gladly pay for it. She said: 'I have nothing against it, but my husband is against it,' and so on. Even if I had [wanted to] ride on [9], I had to stay here for this night and let them do with me as they pleased, for the road [10] ahead of me was extremely dangerous. Yet I was glad and thankful that I could be under a roof, and that my horse had fodder. On the 29th I rode away early, after I had paid, without taking anything, and at half past 3 [11] came to the Palmiet River, which was very dangerous, for it is very broad and deep and has many holes, so that if a man does not know the right way he can perish together with his horse; and that has happened in these days, both with horse and with wagon. By great good fortune I found a man who showed me the way, and so we got across safely. Towards midday we came below the Kloof, where I had my horses given some fodder; and at half past 5 we came to the last house before the Cape, Hottentots Holland, from which it is 4 hard hours before one comes to a house (4 hours of hard riding is more than 4 miles on foot).

<!-- lines 10826-10863 -->
"I wanted to stay the night there; but this man, instead of urging me to stay there, since it looks dangerous in this wilderness, worked to make me ride on: I would still get to the Cape well enough. I thought: here it will go no better than the night before; and I said to my Hottentot: we will ride through the night. But he had no wish to, and did not tell me [why] [12] until we were in the middle of the wilderness. Then he said: 'Let us ride as hard as our horses can run. Many slaves stay here who desert, and they have already killed many people.' I asked why he had not said so before; but there was nothing to be done: we had to go through. I commended us to the dear Saviour, and we rode as hard as our horses could run; and although we saw fires on both sides at once, nothing happened to us, and at half past 9 in the evening we arrived at the Cape safe and sound. We were kindly received by the children of our friend Martin Schmidt, who were astonished that I came so late, and said that I might have come to harm. On the 30th I went at once to the Governor [13], who received me kindly; and when he had inquired about everything, how it goes with us and among us, and I had told him the truth, he was glad and wished that it might go on so in future.

<!-- lines 10863-10920 -->
"I then began to [speak] with him about this place [...] [14] give way; and whether there was anything in the rumours that we heard, and that people of whom one could believe that they knew something [said] that we were to leave this place, which would be painful to us, because the blessing of God rested on it. He said: 'My dear friend, you must keep this place where you now live. The Commissioners-General gave you this place, and I will see to it that you remain living there in peace until they come back from Batavia. The Directors,' he went on, 'gave you permission only on the condition that you settle where there is no Christian congregation [15]; and then you would have to go as far as the Kaffers, for everywhere that Christians live can be called Christian congregations. But I will hold to it that you can remain living there in peace until the gentlemen come back.' I thanked him for his justice, and also put to him the narrowness of the place in our house [16], that over half the people had to stand outside the door when we held a meeting, and that good friends had been found, some of whom had seen the matter for themselves, [who thought] it necessary to build a meeting house and wanted to take an active share in it. He said: 'I do not allow that, and cannot allow it; it must stay as I have arranged it. If you build a bigger house, at once they say you are building a church, and then I have trouble [17] again; and that you may not do where there is a Christian congregation.' I replied that we lived far from a Christian congregation after all, and a farmer's place did not make a congregation. He answered: 'I cannot allow it. You have far too many enemies, and if I had not held to it so, you would long ago have been driven off as far as the Kaffers.' And so there was nothing more to be done. I also visited several gentlemen of the government, who were very friendly and glad that the work of God goes on so in blessing, and said: you must keep this place, and not be driven away.

<!-- lines 10920-10963 -->
"On 1 October I went to the Gezaghebber [18], Mr Rhenius, who, it seems, is a true friend of ours. When he had inquired about everything, he asked what the Governor had said, and advised me to go to him again (but not to give him away) and ask him for permission to put up a Hottentot hut, so that we would at least be sheltered from wind and weather. I thanked him for his advice and went to him again; but I was not well received. He said: 'What I have said shall stand. Just tell your brothers to keep dead quiet, and when you come here again, come to me, and then we will speak of the matter again.' I could do nothing further than commend our whole situation, in which we humbly [submit] [19], to the dear Saviour. On the 2nd I went once more to some gentlemen and friends, to take leave and to commend us to their protection and remembrance. Mr Lesueur [20], who is a gentleman of the government, gave me a small present, as he said, for our recreation, 25 rixdollars, and our friend Schönberg gave me 10 rixdollars. The dear Saviour bless them for it! At 4 o'clock in the afternoon we set off, to visit our friend Mr Lücke [21] and spend the night there. He has bought a country place two hours from the town, not far from the road I had to take, so as to get away early next morning and make the return journey again in 2 days. For the enmity of many farmers towards us and the poor Hottentots is so great that they do not like to grant one a night's lodging. Mr Lücke received me kindly and entertained me well.

<!-- lines 10966-11018 -->
"Brother Heuser [22], a watchmaker, who is a good friend of ours and rode with me, rode back to town in the evening, and told me where I should stay the next night. But this night I had a restless night until morning; I was so anxious and did not know why. I opened a text, which said: 'He shall deliver thee in six troubles: yea, in seven there shall no evil touch thee,' Job 5:19. Yes, how [faithfully] the gracious God has spread his wings over us [23]! I set out from there early in the morning of 3 October, and an hour on we saw a farmhouse and rode towards it. My Hottentot dismounted and asked the way; I stayed in the saddle. My horse, which is young, presumably wanted me off too. All at once it reared up on its hind legs and went over with me; I came underneath, and my horse lay on top of me; and although I was pinned down hard, the dear Saviour sent his angels, who kept me so that no harm befell me, and I felt nothing more than a slight bruising. I now took the other horse, which the Hottentot had, mounted again at once, and we rode on our way, full of praise and thanks to my good Lord, who had kept me so graciously; and I asked him to keep us further, which he has done. My Hottentot was so frightened that he felt more pain than I did. At 7 o'clock in the evening we came to the farmer I had been directed to, who received me kindly and entertained me well. On the morning of the 4th we rode off from there again very early, and towards evening arrived safe and sound at my dear Baviaanskloof, with my dear brothers, who received me very warmly. Praise and thanks be to the dear Saviour for all that he has done for me on this journey."

<!-- lines 11019-11062 -->
Now the diary continues: On 6 October a Hottentot woman came who had been at a farmer's, and said that a slave woman had stolen milk from her master, and she had drunk some of it; had she sinned by this? Question: had she known that the slave had stolen the milk? Answer: yes! She was told that she was just as much a thief as the one who had stolen the milk. She asked forgiveness, promised not to do it again, and said that she had believed that as long as she did not steal herself, it was not a sin. Since it was dry in our garden, we meant to water today. This costs a great deal of trouble, and still did not work, for there are so many moles everywhere here, which bore through everything, so that the water runs into [their holes]. We did not know how we are still to get [anything of] what these creatures have not quite eaten (and so spoiled). We do have a big garden, but we got little from it. Then there is also a kind of small beetle that eats everything off down to the ground. When something is planted for seed, it never comes properly to flower; many thousands sit on one plant or bush. When something is sown, they eat it as soon as it comes out of the ground. When these are past, the caterpillars come and eat what the beetles have left. But it is not only so with us; the complaint is everywhere. In the fatherland we were told of the splendid cauliflower that grows at the Cape; but there is a great difference between the Cape and here. We have not lacked food, but so far we have got little [from the garden].

<!-- lines 11062-11103 -->
On the 7th a woman came and said that the others had told her she must slaughter an ox, so that they would have something to eat. She asked whether she had to do this (she is one of those who have only just moved here). We told her that she should not do it; she should keep it. She still has some cattle, and it is the custom among the Hottentots that when someone still has something, he must give to the others who have nothing; and so they live very much in common in everything. Then she also asked what it was: the others had told her that lately a light had gone from kraal to kraal (or house). We replied that this was nothing; they were certain little creatures that fly at night, which she could often see. There are certain little creatures here, somewhat bigger than the glow-worms at home, which fly at this time of year. Brother Kühnel held the singing meeting in the evening. On the 8th the Saviour owned the meetings in a quite special way. On Wednesdays there are 4 of them: early, for the candidates for baptism, and after it the children's meeting; the first is held by Brother Kühnel and the second by Brother Marsveld. In the afternoon is the communicants' meeting, and towards evening the meeting of the baptised, which Brother Schwinn held. On Saturdays there are 3; the communicants' meeting is only once a week [24]. On Wednesdays a piece from the Idea fidei fratrum is read to the baptised; in the others we always speak on the daily text or on some verse.

<!-- lines 11103-11154 -->
(On the 9th our Jacob went with 2 other Hottentots to the Landdrost at Stellenbosch to complain about the farmer Gildenheuser [25]. This Gildenheuser has a place about three quarters of an hour from the Sergeants River, and would like, if it were possible, to drive the Hottentots with their cattle away from the Sergeants River altogether; yet he has no right to do so. Some time ago the Hottentots already complained about him to the Landdrost. With those who had complained, the Landdrost sent a letter to us, which we were to have delivered to Gildenheuser by one of our people, and we did so. The Hottentot handed over the letter and went away again at once. The wife especially became very angry that this letter had been brought by a Hottentot, and said that if we sent a letter from the Landdrost to them by a Hottentot again, the Hottentot should wait until they had read the letter, and then they would break his arms and legs to pieces. If the Landdrost had a letter to send, he had messengers; she was a burgher's wife and no Hottentot woman. Baas Teunis advised us to ask the Landdrost to spare us such commissions. Since these men were going there, we did so, and gave them a letter, so that we might not get into difficulties. The reason why they have now gone to the Landdrost is this: the Hottentots have a [flock] of goats [26], which a boy herds. The farmer came and beat the boy for coming so near him with the goats, although he could have come a quarter of an hour further towards him without touching what was his. We have our goats running there too, and he has likewise already told the one who herds them that he should not come too near him, or he would get a good pack of blows.)

<!-- lines 11154-11189 -->
On the 10th one of our candidates for baptism came and said that her heart longed very much for the dear Saviour. She was told that that was good. Yesterday and during the night it rained so hard in the mountains that such a flood came here as we had not had even in the rainy season. It had come into the garden, but had done no damage. On the 11th, in the conference, we put 2 women before our dear Lord for baptism, of whom he approved one. On the 12th was the baptism, which Brother Schwinn held. He spoke on Acts 2, verses 36 to 39. She received the name Lena [27]. The dear Saviour owned this act in a quite special way. At such an act there is always a quite special stirring, and the Spirit of God is very active there in glorifying [the martyred] Jesus [28] to the heart. After the baptism several came who declared their desire to be saved. When such an act has taken place, it is as if a new life came among them [29]. On the 13th an old man came and said that he was very troubled that he could not retain all that was said in the meetings; but wherever he went, his heart was with our dear Lord. Today was as cold a day as in winter; we could very well use woollen stockings, if we had any.

<!-- lines 11188-11243 -->
On the 14th a woman came who has not lived here long, and said: "I have two thoughts, and I do not know which to follow. One is that I should go back to my husband (her husband lives about 10 German miles from here, but has no wish to be converted; he did not want her to move here either, but she left him and came here); the other thought is that I should stay here and be converted. Now I do not know what to do." She was told that she should ask God to give her only one thought, and should consider well what it meant to leave God's word. (Jacob and the 2 Hottentots also came home again from the Landdrost. They did not find him at home; he was away on a commission. The deputy Landdrost told them that they would be called again as soon as he came back. This night a cow with its calf died [30] in the field, and also one belonging to the Hottentots, as well as 4 calves, which the Hottentot [31] could not find in the evening. [One] had lost almost its whole tail [32], and the other 4 calves all died. But we do not believe that this was done by a wolf or tiger, for it would certainly have carried off one or more; we rather believe that it was done by Hottentots.) We let the [singing meeting] [33] lapse, because it rained hard. On the 15th was the speaking of the baptised. With some we had to speak earnestly, because they seem to be growing somewhat sleepy; but taken as a whole, one could rejoice. The previous night, and all day today, it rained very hard, so that the water [34] came a whole hour's distance; it also washed away whole pieces of the Hottentots' gardens. Early on the 16th the water had fallen a little, and today was a fine day. Today many Hottentots went to the farmers again, to weed [35] the corn.

<!-- lines 11243-11281 -->
(On the 17th a woman [36] came and said that a Hottentot had asked her to take him for her husband. She had told him that she had not come here for such things, but to hear our dear Father's word. He then told her that he had spoken with us about it, and we had allowed it. This man's wife died here of the sickness that is going about; he has children who are not yet grown up. He was with us a few days ago, and after lamenting his need, asked whether he should not take a wife again. We told him that he needed one, and should look for one. Now he had chosen this one. She asked us to tell her whether she would grieve our dear Lord by it. She was asked whether she wanted him, and she answered yes, if she did not grieve our dear Lord by it. She was told that if they lived together properly she would not grieve the dear Saviour; she should ask him to let them live together in love and peace, and so on, which she promised to do. Her own husband also died of the sickness mentioned, but not here; she moved here only afterwards. Another woman also came who had had many visions in a dream; but these accounts from people who have visions in dreams are now falling off greatly; at least they do not tell us of them so much any more.)

<!-- lines 11281-11345 -->
On the 18th, at the beginning of our conference, we held a heartfelt conversation together. We were much ashamed before our dear Lord and presented ourselves before him as debtors, when we looked at the blessing he has given us and saw ourselves so far behind in all that we ought to do. We asked him for forgiveness, and also that he would [prepare] us, and bless our fellow partakers of his body and blood this evening. Then we put Maria and Anna before him, the first for confirmation and the second as a candidate, of which he approved the second [37]. (In the afternoon Baas Teunis's son came with two others whom we did not know. Young Mrs Teunis sent a letter with him, in which she wrote that Mr Lesueur's daughter was with her, who wanted to visit us tomorrow; she would also like to attend the meeting. Mr Lesueur is a gentleman of the government at the Cape. In the evening we partook of Holy Communion, with the dear Saviour owning us poor ones. On the 19th towards 10 o'clock the daughter of Mr Lesueur mentioned above came, and with her his sister, and another gentleman from the Cape, accompanied by Baas Teunis and his wife, 5 persons in all. After they had first drunk coffee, the meeting began.) Brother Marsveld spoke on the Sunday Gospel, Matthew 22, verse 34 to the end. There was great emotion among the Hottentots and among the visitors too. Baas Teunis tried to hold back his tears, but could not. He and his wife were quite silent afterwards. Those from the Cape said: "This is God's word, and no one shall be able to hinder it, though many want to hinder it, indeed to destroy it." They were amazed; even Baas Teunis said: "Where do all these people come from?" The house and the kitchen were full, and probably half still had to stand outside. They all ate here at midday; afterwards they drank coffee again, and towards 3 o'clock they drove away. Mr Lesueur's sister is one of the pious society at the Cape. Such a visit also troubles us somewhat, especially when they stay over midday. There are many at the Cape who would like to come here if time allowed and their business permitted; which in one way we would gladly see, but on the other we do not wish it, because of the cost.

<!-- lines 11345-11406 -->
On the 20th. It was said lately that many of the Hottentot women had gone to the farmers to weed the corn; among them were also 3 of our baptised. Today some came back, including 2 of the baptised, Eva and Susanna. They came at once and told how it had gone in that time, and related with sorrow that Dorothea had got drunk at the farmers'. The farmer's wife had also given each of them a *pullumpen* [38] full of wine; they had not drunk until they were leaving, and then Eva drank a glassful. But Dorothea, on leaving, drank another *pullumpen* full, and then brandy as well. They would gladly have had the other 2 with them, but could not get them away; and the others too, who go to school but are not in closer fellowship with us. These, and Dorothea too, did not come home with the others, but put up at another farmer's again. We are learning more now than before about how things go on, for until now we had heard little bad, since the Hottentots are very loyal to one another, and one will not betray another. Now in the school, during the teaching, we gave very earnest warnings. Since [they all care] very much about the school and the teaching [39], we have said (and we mean to keep to it) that if they behaved badly, whether here in the kraals or at the farmers', whether in talk, drinking and so on, they might no longer come to school, but we would take their book from them and they could go where they liked; for we did not want to have people in the school who wanted to serve the devil, and spend so much trouble on them; and it would be much better for them not to know God's word, for the punishment would be much greater for those who knew it and still did not do it. And those who knew that this woman or that man had done something bad, we would regard just as those who had done it, if they did not tell us but kept quiet, and we would send them out of school as well as the others. They are all very much afraid of this, and now those who know something come to us and report it, even if they are not more closely acquainted with us.

<!-- lines 11406-11433 -->
On the 21st those who had been at the farmer's came and asked for their books, to go to school (for when anyone goes to a farmer to work there, they must hand in their books for that time). Dorothea asked for her book in front of the house. She was told nothing more than that she did not need one, because we wanted to speak with her alone. The other came into the school; she was told at once that she should go where she liked; we did not want people in the school who behaved as she did. It is a sad thing about the drinking. They say that the farmers now give them much more than ever before, so as to make them drunk if they can. Eva too said that one day she was given a *pullumpen* full of wine, and since she had held out a long time [40], a whole bottle of wine was set before her, and she was told to drink and help herself; but she did not. Today an old woman was also buried, our Jacob's mother.

<!-- lines 11434-11472 -->
On the 22nd Dorothea came and said that she had drunk at the farmer's, and asked forgiveness. We put it to her that she had not drunk, but boozed and made herself drunk. This she would not admit, and we could not feel much remorse in her either, and so we were obliged to exclude her and to tell her that for now we could no longer regard her as our sister, and did not want to be called brother by her any more. Those who called us brother must do the will of God, and not the works of the devil. We were obliged to do the same with our Hanna. Until now we have looked on with much patience; she has often been with us and pleaded; but she never spoke honestly with us, and now we have heard much more, that she has behaved badly in the kraals. In the evening we announced it in the meeting of the baptised, and commended the 2 strayed sheep to the prayers of all before the dear Saviour, that they might fall at his feet and ask forgiveness; and we asked all to remain faithful to Jesus, and [prayed] that he would keep them from everything bad [41]. This made a great stir among them. Then this one and that one came who are not yet in closer fellowship with us, who had also given us grief, and asked us to take them back into the school; but this time we refused them.

<!-- lines 11473-11533 -->
Early on the 23rd, when we came out in front of the house, a large snake was lying by the vine that stands in the yard, which is fenced round with 3 dozen [stakes] [42]. We shot it, then stabbed it through the head with the bayonet on the gun, at which it stretched its mouth open so wide that a hen's egg could easily have gone in. It was only about 4 feet long, but as thick as a man's leg at the bottom. We have said several times that we want to build a little house which should serve Brother Kühnel as a workshop, and its foundation is laid. When Brother Schwinn was at the Cape lately and spoke with the Edle Heer about a meeting house, the Edle Heer said that we should build nothing; everything should stay as he had found it until the 2 Commissioners-General came back from Batavia. Now Brother Schwinn spoke of a meeting house, and not of a workshop. But the answer the Edle Heer gave, "Everything shall stay as I found it until the 2 Commissioners come," has put us in great perplexity about what we should do. Our dear brothers have already spent money on various tools for Brother Kühnel and sent them, for example lathes [43], grindstones and screw clamps; he also brought some files with him from Amsterdam; and to all appearances he could earn something to support this mission, for which, as our dear brothers know, he must have a workshop. But what shall we do now? If we build, can it not then be said: "You are disobedient; were you not told that everything should stay as it is until the 2 Commissioners-General come from Batavia?" Yet we could not get it out of our minds to contribute something to lighten the costs of the mission. We have very often sat together and said: What shall we do? Sometimes we thought that we should build. Then again: if we do that, shall we not give the whole mission a blow? At last we resolved to lay it before the dear Saviour in prayer, and to let him give us direction by the lot, whether we should wait for the 2 Commissioners-General or build now. It said: No! We must wait, and see how it will go when they [come] [44].)

<!-- lines 11533-11562 -->
Early on the 24th at 5 o'clock the excluded came. Hanna asked with many tears for forgiveness. She has fine words, but her conduct does not match her words. With Dorothea [her pleas for] forgiveness [45] do not yet seem to go very deep. So we did not yet have the confidence to take them back; we directed them to the dear Saviour, whom she had grieved so much. At once one of the others came and asked to be taken back into the school; she would cry to the dear Saviour, and not come to school yet [46]. Because it was cold and began to rain, we cancelled the singing meeting today. On the 25th Brothers Marsveld and Kühnel rode to Baas Peter Toÿ [47], about 4 miles from here. This man had written to us that he had bought cheap corn [48] for us, and that we should come and look at it. This man still seems to mean honestly by us. On the 26th they came home again. The meeting at 10 o'clock was very well attended. Afterwards several came who declared their desire to be saved; one woman asked earnestly for closer care, and said that she wanted nothing in the world but to live wholly for Jesus.

<!-- lines 11563-11609 -->
On the 27th 6 more children, 5 women and a man came and asked leave to go to school. We sent two children out of school today: one had behaved badly in the kraals, and the other had got drunk at a farmer's. This means, sending them out of school, does good service: they soon came and begged, and promised to be good in everything. And now they tell us at once if one has had even an exchange of words with another. On the 28th 2 women and 1 man came and said that they had lately been at a farmer's, and had drunk too much wine [49]. They were sent out of school for the time. On the morning of the 29th was the speaking of the candidates for baptism. We could rejoice with all our hearts; several were very open-hearted. One, about 18 or 19 years old, said that the thought had come to her that she would like to have a husband, which she had thought little of before; but she had asked the dear Saviour to take these thoughts from her, which he had done. As for this matter, we have often been in perplexity; in this the dear Saviour must help. In the afternoon we received 2 letters from Brother Treschow in Copenhagen, one of 2 April and the other of 23 June 1794. What a joy it was to us when these letters reached us, no one can believe who has not experienced it himself; for we have had no line of news for so long, and were very anxious about our dear congregations, especially Neuwied and Zeist. According to these letters they were still living in peace, for which we thank the dear Saviour from our hearts.

<!-- lines 11609-11645 -->
On the 30th several new people came again, who want to live here, hear God's word and learn too. Some women also came who declared their perplexity about being saved, and [told of] the [unrest] [50] of their hearts with many tears. 2 little girls also came, about 9 years old, who said: "We are so very troubled when we [...] [51]." Question: about what? Answer: "When we think how much our dear Saviour had to suffer and bear for our sins." On the 31st. Our Sara took into her house a Hottentot woman who had moved here. Today she came and said that the woman did not behave quietly and properly, and that she could not live with her in love and peace. Her son's boy, who is about 9 years old and lives with her, must pray every evening when he goes to sleep and when he gets up, and the other woman had often disturbed him at it, and so on. We had the woman come, told her that she must leave this house and could see where she might find a place, took her book from her, and said that this should be a [house] [52] where things went on in love and peace, not with quarrelling. A boy also came and said that he had spoken bad words against another, and asked with many tears for forgiveness. The singing meeting, which Brother Kühnel held, was [in front] of the house.

<!-- lines 11645-11700 -->
If the weather is not such that we can hold it in front of the house, we shall often have to let it lapse, because the room in the house is much too small. For the children come to the singing meeting too, since big and small care a great deal about it; and the number is growing fast. Our room or chamber is 16 feet long and 12 wide. When 60 to 70 people come into such a chamber, anyone can imagine that they sit on top of one another. We take the kitchen in too, but that is far from enough. We cannot say exactly, but at least 200 always come. Our dear brothers and sisters can imagine that, since the Hottentots are very rich in lice, we get plenty of them too, as they sit close around us. We now hold the schools of the women and children in the garden under the pear tree, when the weather at all allows; for the house will no longer do for the school of either class, women or children, since each of these schools has about 70. The men's school is in the little house which we built last year (as has been said); their number is some 30, so many go to school. Then there are many more who are too old, who come to the meeting; so that the number is at least 200. Now with this we close our diary again, and commend ourselves and our Hottentots to the prayers and remembrance of all the congregations before our dear Lord. At the end of October 1794 our little congregation consists of 4 communicants, 5 candidates for Communion, 7 baptised, 13 candidates for baptism, and in addition Lena, baptised by Brother Schmidt; in all 32 persons [53]. Hendr. Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn, Johan Christian Kühnel.
