<!-- lines 222-254 -->
[On the 20th we set out] at 4 o'clock in the afternoon. 3 of our good friends went with us a little way out of the town, where we took leave of them and got onto the wagon, which had 12 oxen in front of it. At 7 o'clock the oxen were outspanned to graze, and we too ate our supper on the wagon. Baas Teunis had promised to provide food for us on the journey. He brought a large cloth, full, out of the chest, and said that our [dear] Schmidt had sent this with him for us [1]. We were amazed when the cloth was opened and we saw roast turkey, fish and meat, and a large jug of wine. We cannot thank this man enough; he has done a great deal for us in these 4 weeks. No father can do more for his children or care for them more than he has done and cared for us; and so has his son-in-law. This dear old man rejoices from his heart that the door is now opened for the poor Hottentots too; he sees in the spirit how eager they are for the Gospel. He has often promised to visit us, if God lets him live a little longer.

<!-- lines 255-316 -->
At 9 o'clock the oxen were inspanned again and we drove through the night. At 5 o'clock in the night [2] we crossed the first river [3]. Early on the 21st we crossed the Laurens River and Hottentots Holland [4]. At 7 o'clock we outspanned below the kloof by a small cottage [?] [5] and had breakfast. Here Baas Teunis had also left a span of oxen at pasture [6], so that there should be no delay. Towards 8 o'clock the other oxen were inspanned; those that had pulled until now were left for a Hottentot boy to drive after us. From there the road went up the kloof; what a road it is that one has to cross there is astonishing. We went up it on foot. We believed it was impossible for the oxen to get up with the wagon. We had often wondered, when farmers came to the Cape with 12, 16 oxen and even more in front of one wagon, when what they had loaded would not have been enough for 2. The oxen are big and strong; but now we could believe it, and marvelled that it was possible to load even so much. Further on into the country there are mountains where they have to put 24 in front of one wagon. That is also the reason why most driving is done with oxen and not with horses. One must not think that there is any lack of horses; there are plenty. Our Baas has 250, his own and the Company's together. He is a poor farmer who does not have 50, even 100 horses! Now people will ask: what do they do with so many? Answer: not much. They go out to pasture and often do not come home for 8 days. Most of the work they do with them is treading out the corn at harvest. It is not threshed by people, as in Europe; instead the corn is spread out in the field in a large round circle edged with stones, then the horses are driven in, with a man with a big whip, and it is trodden short and small, so that not a stalk of it stays whole.

<!-- lines 316-342 -->
At 9 o'clock we crossed the Steenbras River. At 11 o'clock we camped by the Palmiet River. We lay down behind small bushes and had our midday meal. Baas Teunis asked whether we wanted to sleep that night or drive through. We said that if the oxen could stand it and he was willing, we would like to drive through. He said the oxen could go 12 hours without resting once. The farmers usually drive by night and let the oxen graze by day, also because of the heat, since it is cooler at night. In these days it was not very hot. Towards 2 o'clock the oxen were inspanned, and we crossed the Palmiet River, at 4 o'clock the Knoflokskraal River, at 7 o'clock the Botter River; at 8 o'clock we ate our supper under a bush. The oxen were not even outspanned. When we had eaten we went straight on, and so on through the night.

<!-- lines 342-362 -->
On the 22nd at 7 o'clock in the morning we crossed the Sonderend River, and at 8 o'clock we arrived at the Company post of Soetemelksvlei, at Baas Teunis's [7]. The text [8] with which we left the Cape on 20 December was: "Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth. [Isaiah 42:1] – His atoning sacrifice, so precious and so dear, thou hast declared of full worth, etc." And the text when we arrived here on the 22nd was: "And the work of righteousness shall be peace; and the effect of righteousness quietness and assurance for ever. [Isaiah 32:17] – God has pleasure in us, etc." After we had had breakfast, Baas Teunis showed us to a room of our own and said that here we could be undisturbed. We eat and drink at his table.

<!-- lines 362-399 -->
We had a letter from the Cape to a Juffrouw [9] at Genadendal [?] [10], 2 hours from here, which we wanted to deliver ourselves. On the 23rd we went there early, while it was still cool. Baas Teunis gave us one of his people to go with us, a German, who showed us the way. This woman was surprised that we came so far on foot. We had to stay there for the midday meal. Then we looked round the garden. It is very pleasant to walk in a garden here; one walks as if in a wood, in the shade of lemon, almond, apricot, fig, poplar and pear trees [11], and so it is with most of the farmers. One is much surprised to see such trees so big and in such numbers; they grow without much care, while in Europe so much care has to be given to them if they are to bear even a little fruit in the hothouses. Many branches of the lemon and fig trees were broken under the weight of the fruit. In the afternoon the woman had us driven home with 8 horses in front of the wagon. It is astonishing what a mountainous country this is. When one goes up a mountain, one thinks: now I shall be over it and have a plain. But no: when one gets up one, one sees 10 others, and so it goes on and on.

<!-- lines 400-421 -->
24 December was the day on which we were to go out and look for our future dwelling place. This was a doubly important day for us. Here nothing was to be seen of the importance of this day. In our morning blessing we asked our dear Lord on our knees that on this, his birthday festival, he would let a blessing from his becoming man flow to us poor ones; and since today was the day on which we were to go out and look for our dwelling place, that he would show us, with his Spirit and guidance and through them, the place where he wanted to have us. After we had had breakfast we drove off from here with Baas Teunis (8 oxen in front of the wagon) at 6 o'clock.

<!-- lines 421-449 -->
At 7 o'clock we came to Hartebeestkraal, where we found some remains of the walls of Brother Schmidt's first house [12]. At 10 o'clock we came to Baviaanskloof. At its mouth we went into a Hottentot kraal. We asked one of them whether he knew where George Schmidt's house had stood. He said he would show us, and went with us. A good part of the wall was still standing, and in the garden stood various fruit trees, which we thought he had planted, and also 3 oaks. But in another kraal we heard that 3 trees were still standing that he had planted, namely an almond, an apricot and a pear tree. There were also still various walls of houses of the Hottentots who had lived with him. The garden, as can easily be imagined, looks very wild. We stayed 2 hours at this place and looked at everything carefully. All three of us felt that this was the place the Lord has appointed for us for the time being.

<!-- lines 449-539 -->
Baas Teunis asked the man who had shown us the way whether he had heard that such people were here again who wanted to teach them. He said yes; the farmers had said that people would come to teach them, that they would be nice to them, but if they went to them, more would soon come and take them and sell them as slaves to Batavia. (When we came back again, Baas asked the woman whether she wanted to learn. She said no, she was too old (she was about 30) and had no wish to. Baas Teunis said to them: "Well, if you do not want to, then at least do not keep your children back, and do not let the farmers talk you into anything. The government is sending these people to you to teach you, and when you have learned, to baptise you, so that you know what is good or evil. For then you are Christians as good as the farmers, and they can do nothing more to you. The government loves you; that is why it has sent you teachers and charged me to bring them to you. If these were not good people, the government would not have recommended them to me like this, for me to bring them to you and tell you so. You know that a couple of years ago the farmers wanted to shoot you all and drive you away; if I had not stepped in, it would have happened too." They said: "Yes, Baas." (A couple of years ago a rumour had come among them (they had heard it from Christians) that the world was to pass away. Then none of them would work for the farmers (the farmers have many in their service). They slaughtered their cattle and sent word [?] everywhere among their nation and made it known [13]. Then the farmers became very angry with them; and the land they have the farmers would like to have. So they took this as the pretext and said they were starting a rebellion. Baas Teunis said that at that time more than 50 farmers came to him on horseback at night, chased him out of bed, and told him that they now meant to go into the kraals of the Hottentots and shoot the whole rabble [14] (that is what they call them). Baas told them they should just dare to do anything to one of them; they would see what would happen to them; and he gave them a good dressing-down. At this they dispersed and did nothing to them. From that time the farmers call Baas Teunis the Hottentots' god. Properly speaking, no farmer may do anything to a Hottentot or punish him; if he deserves punishment, the farmers must report it to Baas Teunis.) "And now the government has sent you teachers, so that you no longer believe such things; and they will now settle here. If you let them teach you, then no one can drive you away from here; but if you will not learn and be converted, the people will go away again, and then you may believe it: if they go away again, the farmers will come and drive you all away. As long as they stay with you, they and you will be protected by the government, so that no farmer may do anything to you.") [15]

<!-- lines 539-630 -->
Then we drove to the Sergeants River, half an hour from there, where one of Brother Schmidt's baptised is said to be still living. When we got there, all the Hottentot men and women who were at home came straight out of their huts and stood round Baas Teunis; with what reverence cannot be described. Baas asked at once for the one who had been baptised by Georg Schmidt. She was fetched at once from her hut, but could no longer walk by herself. They brought her, leading her; she had to sit down on the ground at once, for she could not stand. Then Baas Teunis spoke briefly with them [16], and they all promised to learn. Then Baas said that now we should speak with them. We asked the old woman whether she had been baptised by Georg Schmidt and what her name was. She said: "Yes, my name is Lena." [We asked] whether any more of his baptised were living. She said: "No, I am the only one left; the others have all died." We asked whether she knew that Georg Schmidt was no longer alive. She said: "Yes, I have heard that he has died." Brother Marsveld asked what Georg Schmidt had told her and spoken with her about. She said she no longer knew; she had forgotten everything. We asked whether he had not told her of Jesus, who became man for us and died on the cross for our sins. She said: "Yes." We said that we were his brothers, and if they wanted to learn and be saved, we would tell them the way of salvation as our Brother Georg Schmidt had done. The old woman (she is at least 80 years old) then said: "My God be thanked, that word went through our hearts!" She said that she still had a book from Georg Schmidt. Someone ran at once and fetched it. It had been very well kept: it was wrapped in two sheepskins, and then it was put in a leather bag as well. It was a Dutch New Testament, in which she had been able to read; but now she can no longer see. Another woman, however, still young, about 30, has learned to read from her father, who was baptised. She had to read. It was a pleasing thing: she opened at the 2nd chapter of Matthew, of the birth of Jesus, and read the chapter quite nicely. When she had read it, we asked them whether they knew what she had read and who the child Jesus was. She said: "No, I do not know that." We said that when they came here we would tell them and explain what she had just read. We said that tomorrow was the day of remembrance of the birth of Jesus, which they did not understand, however. Then Baas Teunis said: "When these people come here, help them with building their house, and do not let the farmers tell you tales; do not believe them, believe what I tell you. You know that I have always wanted and advised what is best for you." They promised everything; he counts for a great deal with them, too. Then we drove on. They sat down together on the ground and stayed sitting as long as we could see them.

<!-- lines 630-677 -->
Baas said that they would indeed say yes to everything, but they were not always to be believed. From there we drove to the house of Baas Teunis's son, which is not yet finished. He is our nearest neighbour; he lives about [17] 1½ hours from us, a very pleasant man; he is still single. At 7 o'clock we got home again. This year one cannot begin to build any more, but with the beginning of the new year it is to begin at once, God willing. Baas Teunis will help us as far as lies in his power. The timber for our house must be fetched from 2 hours further up than where Baas Teunis lives, so 6 hours' distance to our house. Where we went there is indeed wood for burning along the rivers, but not for building; it is bushes from which one can hardly cut a stick for a whip handle. It is astonishing how many great mountains there are here, and all bare, without bush. The farmers must buy all their building timber from Baas Teunis; everything the Company needs at the Cape must be brought from there, 3 days' journey. And when one sees how the timber that is here is lodged in the rocks and the mountains, one would think it impossible to get it out.

<!-- lines 677-703 -->
On the 25th (today, the first day of Christmas) there was a meeting, at which 12 of Baas Teunis's people gathered; we attended it too. First psalms were sung, then a sermon was read, and psalms were sung again; and so the service for this Christmas was over. Baas Teunis assured us that many farmers here in the country did not know that it was Christmas. Our dear brothers and sisters will readily believe that it pained us very much to spend this great festival in such a way. We went into our room and implored our dear Saviour on our knees that on this, his birthday festival, he would send a blessing to us poor ones too, in this country where he has led us, and acknowledge us poor ones in this country, and lead us with thy [18] Holy Spirit into everything that he wants us to do.

<!-- lines 703-799 -->
On the 26th Baas Teunis invited us to ride hunting with his son. Because we were afraid to ride, since we could not ride, and the hunt goes very hard, he had a wagon with 8 oxen inspanned, and we drove. Two horses were taken along for the hunt. When we came to game, the son mounted and chased after it. The hunt was meant for bontebok and ostrich. (Hunting is not done in the bush, but in the open field.) He knew of [19] an ostrich nest and thought to find the bird on the nest and shoot it there; but when we got there the bird was gone and the eggs broken, except for one, which was taken along. When we had driven on a little way we found another on the road. Baas Teunis said that one finds many eggs in the field like this, and that the Hottentots have a particular skill in looking for these eggs. They look for them and bring them for sale. Three men can make a proper meal of one; we like them better than hens' eggs; they are on the table most days. But this hunt did not turn out as hoped. We saw bontebok enough, whole herds, 20 to 30 together; many shots were fired at them too, but none of them was hit. A buck [20], of which there were also many large herds, was hit. But the bontebok are twice as big as a buck and are said to taste very good. Ostriches were seen enough too, but we could not get near enough to any; they cannot fly, but they run so that no horse can follow them. The eggs were put in a leather bag in which our bread and our jug of wine were kept; this bag was hung on the rails of the wagon. From the jolting of the wagon (for it jolts terribly, goes over everything, and is driven across the fields every which way) the jug of wine had got a crack, and an ostrich egg was in pieces. We saw this when we wanted to eat. That made a good meal for the Hottentot boys, who were given the bag to empty; there were two with us. We must also say this: when one drives with an ox wagon, a Hottentot boy must run in front most of the time and lead the front oxen. It is wonderful how hard such a boy can run. He must go through everything, through water, even if it came up under his arms; he must go through. Such a boy can keep this up all day and all night, however hard the going. The driver sits on the wagon and drives the oxen. He has an astonishingly long whip; I measured one of them: the handle was 9 ells long and the lash 15, 24 ells together. They always need both hands for it, and with it they know how to hit whichever ox they want. At 6 o'clock in the evening we came home again from this hunt; we had left at 7 o'clock in the morning. It was such a hot day today that the sun had burned us badly. The wagon [21] was open, because one had to see where the game was; otherwise, when one travels, one has covered wagons.

<!-- lines 799-826 -->
It was as if it could not possibly be Christmas, it was so hot and the days so long. On 21 December, which is the longest day, the sun shines until 7 hours 10 minutes [22]. On the 28th we drove with them into the forest, to see what it is like there. It is a wonder how it is possible to get the timber out of the rocks. On Sunday the 30th a sermon was again read and psalms sung, which happens every Sunday. In the evening the children, each of them, 6 in number, must also read a chapter from the Bible and sing a hymn to close; we attended this too. He is a very respectable man, and so is his wife; the children are very well brought up. He has his own schoolmaster, a very accomplished person in music. He teaches the daughters the clavier, the sons the violin, on which he is a master.

<!-- lines 826-865 -->
This evening the Landdrost of Swellendam also came for the festivities beginning here tomorrow, to take part in them. They said that this night is the merriest of the whole year, and that this is now the greatest festival in the whole year, and many come together here. On the 31st many more arrived for the festivities. Towards evening we went out into the field and found a little spot among the bushes, to thank our dear Lord and Saviour for all the proofs of love we have experienced from him in the year now coming to an end, and to ask forgiveness for everything in which we have grieved him and not listened to the voice of his Spirit. We implored him for his blessing in this new year, and asked him heartily that he would lead and guide us in it with his Spirit, make us faithful to one another, and take from us everything that does not befit a true servant of Jesus; and when we were at a loss, that he would counsel us according to his heart; and since we were now among these people, where he has appointed us, that he would give us words to say to these poor people that were after his heart, and open the hearts of these poor ones, so that the words of his agony and death might find entrance; and keep us long together in love and peace. We gave ourselves to him quite anew for his service. He graciously acknowledged us poor ones and let us feel his peace.

<!-- lines 866-919 -->
When we came home again, the festivity soon began; it consisted of dancing. Many had gathered for this festivity; the dancing lasted the whole night until the morning. At 11 o'clock at night there was a meal, with every possible kind of food prepared; a splendid meal; there were 30 at table. This generosity costs Baas Teunis a great deal. Whoever comes must be given something to eat; that is the custom in this country, because hospitality is at home here. When it was 12 o'clock, everyone went out in front of the house and began to shoot; there was a great deal of shooting. When that was over, they wished one another a happy new year, and the dancing began again. We now went to bed. In the morning, when it was day, at half past 4, there was shooting again. On New Year's Day the flag was hoisted and flew all day; no one may do this unless it is a Company post. The dancing went on again all day and all night. Early on the 2nd the festivities came to an end. Everything went off very peaceably; no one got drunk. But to those who are here under the name of soldiers, 13 in number (they are all Germans, and tradesmen), Baas Teunis had given a cask of wine. (They were not allowed to take part in the festivities, because they are his subordinates; they live in another house and may not come into the Baas's house.) They had laid it on the table and caroused until they were thoroughly drunk. Two of them got into a quarrel and fought violently. It is astonishing how great the pride of the Germans here is. They are farmers' daughters, yet they carry themselves so that I have hardly seen a lady in Germany grander, or with such great headdresses [23], than these farmers' daughters [24].

## Notes

1. The transcription reads *daß dieſes unſer Sel. Schmidt für uns mitgegeben*: *Sel.* usually abbreviates *selig*, "the late", but the man meant is Father Schmidt of Cape Town, who was alive, as the next sentences show. The abbreviation may be misread. To be checked against the image.
2. *des Nachts*: 5 o'clock in the morning, before daybreak.
3. *Revier*: the Cape Dutch *rivier*, river. The diary uses *Revier* for rivers and streams throughout.
4. The Laurens (Lourens) River at what is now Somerset West, and Hottentots Holland, the district at the foot of the pass over the mountains. The transcription has a stray *um* and the page number *10.10* here.
5. The transcription reads *bey einem kleinen Händelgen*; the normalised column keeps *Händelchen*, which has no sense here. Perhaps *Häusgen*, "a small house". To be checked against the image.
6. The transcription reads *Hier hatte Baas Teunis auch ſeiner Eheweibe einen Zug Ochſen auf der Weide gelaſſen*, literally "had also left his wife a span of oxen at pasture". The words *ſeiner Eheweibe* make no sense here and are left out of the English; perhaps a misreading. To be checked against the image.
7. *Soete⸗Melksvalley*: Soetemelksvlei, the Dutch East India Company's post on the Sonderend River where Baas Teunis lived.
8. *Looſung*: the watchword, the daily text from the Moravian *Losungen*, with the hymn verse that follows it. The first text is Isaiah 42:1, the second Isaiah 32:17; the Bible references are added in brackets. The Bible verses are given in the words of the King James Version.
9. *Juffrouw*: Dutch, a form of address for a married or unmarried woman of standing.
10. The transcription reads *an eine Juffrouw in Genfecraal*. The name is probably a misreading of a farm name ending in *-kraal*; it cannot be Genadendal, a name first given to Baviaanskloof in 1806. The place was 2 hours from Soetemelksvlei. To be checked against the image.
11. The transcription reads *Bier⸗Bäumen* here and *Bier⸗Baum* at line 438: "beer trees", a misreading or dialect spelling of *Birn-*, pear. The normalised column has pear.
12. Georg Schmidt, the first Moravian missionary among the Khoekhoe, lived first at Hartebeestkraal on the Sonderend River (1737) and then at Baviaanskloof until 1744.
13. The transcription reads *ließen Herren allerwegen unter ihrer Nation*, "let lords everywhere among their nation", which makes no sense. Perhaps *ließen es hören* or *ſandten Boten*, "sent messengers". To be checked against the image.
14. The transcription reads *das Gut*, "the goods"; the normalised column has *Geſindel*, "rabble". Perhaps the Dutch *goed* or *gespuis*. To be checked against the image.
15. The parentheses in this paragraph are the diarists' own. Their account of the farmers' threat of a few years before is set inside Baas Teunis's speech, which then continues. The opening parenthesis closes at the end of the speech.
16. The transcription reads *auf wenige Art*, "in few ways"; the normalised column has "briefly".
17. *zierlich*, "daintily", in both columns: a misreading or slip for *ziemlich*, "about".
18. The prayer changes from "he" to "thy" (*mit Deinem Heiligen Geiſt*), as in the manuscript.
19. The transcription reads *Er mußte ein Strauſs⸗Vogel⸗Neſt*, "he must an ostrich nest"; probably *wußte*, "knew".
20. *Ein Bock*: probably a smaller antelope, a springbok or grey rhebok; the diarists did not yet know the names.
21. *Der Magen war offen*, "the stomach was open", in both columns: a misreading of *Wagen*.
22. That is, it sets at 7.10 in the evening.
23. *Kopfzügen*: not a known word; perhaps *Kopfzeugen*, "headdresses". The sentence *ſie geben aber, daß ...* is also garbled; the English gives the sense.
24. In this passage the diplomatic transcription runs about three lines behind the normalised column: the end of the sentence is at lines 920 to 922 in the diplomatic column.
