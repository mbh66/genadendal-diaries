I. Diary of the 3 Brothers Heinrich Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn and Johann Christian Kühnel, of their stay at the Cape and their further journey into the country, in the year 1792 to 31 March 1793.

<!-- lines 00036-00056 -->
From 23 November to 20 December we stayed in the town. In these 4 weeks we became acquainted with many people in the town. There is a society of people here who hold meetings together twice a week. We were invited to it at once; more than 60 belong to the society. There really are many very dear people among them, who want to be saved only through the merit of Jesus and seek forgiveness of sins at Jesus' feet. Their meetings are also conducted quite simply. They have read the *Idea fidei fratrum* with blessing [4]. They say that this book has removed a great many false ideas about the Brethren.

<!-- lines 00056-00085 -->
Domine van Lier had provided for us in a great many things in advance [5]. He had gone on a journey into the country for his health; before he left he arranged lodgings, so that if the Brethren came in his absence they might get good lodgings. On the 28th he came home, but very weak. He has consumption and, to all human appearance, will probably not live much longer. In him we are losing a true friend. He is much loved in the whole town; we had to dine with him a couple of times. The society mentioned above is heartily glad that God has now opened the door to the poor Hottentots too. When we took leave, they gave us to understand that they had wished, and also believed, that we would set up proper congregational arrangements among them. We told them that this was not the purpose for which we had come here, and that if it happened, it could be very much to their disadvantage and ours.

<!-- lines 00085-00107 -->
During this time we were busy writing and packing our things. We had to look through all our things to see whether anything had been spoiled on the ship; we found everything still in good order. Then we had to pay many visits in the town and were invited to meals. The Commandant, with whom we were several times, was very friendly towards us, and so were the Commissioners who are here at present [6]; also Mr Gordon, who is the head of the military and the second authority in this country [7]. We had to dine with him too, in his garden, which lies outside the town and is very splendid. Well-meaning people here named many places to us where we ought to go.

<!-- lines 00108-00121 -->
The gentlemen of the government wished us to wait until Baas Teunis came, whom they expected any day; they meant to give him orders to take us with him and look after us. This man is in the Company's service and is Baas on a Company post; what they tell him, he must do. He is also said to be a very respectable man, one much honoured in the country. His district reaches far into the country; the farmers and Hottentots are under his command.

<!-- lines 00121-141 -->
On 2 December a gentleman from Constantia sent to us and asked us to visit him the next day. He sent a wagon with 8 horses at 6 o'clock in the morning and had us fetched; the gentleman's name is Clüde [8]. It is 2 hours from here by horse wagon; it is the place where the precious Constantia wine grows; in the whole country no other grows that equals it. A splendid place. This gentleman said that he had gone to school with our present [?] Brother Schmidt [9]. He wanted us to stay with him for some days, but we could not, because we did not know whether Baas Teunis might not have come. The next day he had us driven home again.

<!-- lines 141-172 -->
We heard all kinds of talk that the farmers from the country were saying about us in the town. Some said: "If the Hottentots are to be taught, we might as well become Hottentots too." Others: "If the Herrnhuters come into the country and want to teach the Hottentots, we will kill them straight away at the start." The first, says Baas Teunis, spoke the truth; he assures us that a great many of the farmers cannot read a word. None of this troubles us; God will make things go as he wants them. (A gentleman in Amsterdam, a born Capeman [10], but one who saw clearly, told us that we should keep quiet at the beginning and not say why we had come; if we said it, the farmers would make things very hard for us.) We had not been long in the town when farmers came to our Mr Schmidt and asked whether it was true that Herrnhuters were here who wanted to go into the country to teach the Hottentots. It is going round the country like wildfire; many believe that we are quite a different kind of people.

<!-- lines 172-202 -->
On 17 December Baas Teunis arrived at the Cape. On the 18th the Governor or commander (there is at present no proper governor) [11] sent for us, and for Baas Teunis as well, and recommended us to him in the best terms, and told him to take us to the kraals [12], so that we could look at the area and choose the place where we wished to settle, and to help us build a house. Baas Teunis promised to do as much as lay in his power. The commander said that he had spoken with the Commissioners, and he and they thought it best that for the present we should go first to Baviaanskloof, where our Brother Schmidt had been [13]. We said that we could not well have a place fixed for us, because we must first see it. He said we should just look at this area. Baas Teunis said that most of the Hottentots still lived in this area. We were pleased that these gentlemen directed us there; other people had advised us to go to the Tigerhoek [14].

<!-- lines 202-222 -->
On the 19th we took leave of the commander, commended ourselves to his protection and asked for his love and goodwill. He promised to serve us wherever he could. So did the two Commissioners, with whom we had a quite separate audience. They promised us their love and goodwill and wished us God's blessing. Then we also took leave of the 4 ministers here, 3 Reformed and one Lutheran. They were all very friendly and said that if they could be of service to us, it would be a pleasure to them. Then [we took leave] of the other good friends from whom we had received much love and kindness, and on the 20th we set out

## Notes

1. *tten,*: a fragment at the top of the first image, probably the end of a word on a label or a page that is otherwise not reproduced. It has no sense on its own.
2. Archive labels, left untranslated: the Rijksarchief in Utrecht, and the archive of the Evangelische Broedergemeente (Moravian Church) at Zeist. *Z. Z. G. 1143* is a shelf mark.
3. The contents list is a later archivist's note, in modern German, on the second image. It gives the diary's parts and their page counts. The words "duplicate or reworking of V." stand before *VI* and probably describe item VI: the file has two diaries for July 1794 (lines 8765 and 9024).
4. *Idea fidei fratrum*: August Gottlieb Spangenberg's summary of Moravian doctrine (1779), known in English as *An Exposition of Christian Doctrine*. It had been translated into Dutch.
5. *Domine van Lier*: Helperus Ritzema van Lier, minister of the Reformed church in Cape Town and a friend of the Moravian mission. *Domine* is the Dutch title of a minister. He died in 1793.
6. *Comiſſarii*: the Commissioners-General Sebastiaan Cornelis Nederburgh and Simon Hendrik Frykenius, sent by the Dutch East India Company to reform the Cape government, 1792 to 1793. "The Commandant" (*Comandant*) is probably the acting governor, Johan Isaac Rhenius; see note 11.
7. Robert Jacob Gordon, commander of the Dutch garrison at the Cape. See `glossary.csv`.
8. *Cluͤde*: probably Hendrik Cloete, owner of Groot Constantia, the estate that produced the famous Constantia wine. He is probably also the "Mr Claude" of A363596, entry 03, who bought land near the mission in 1795. To be checked.
9. The transcription reads *bey unſerm jezu Bruder Schmidt*; the normalised column has *jetzigen*, "present". Georg Schmidt, the first Moravian missionary at Baviaanskloof (1737 to 1744), had died in 1785, so "present" makes no sense. The word may be a misread *ſel.* (*selig*, "the late"). To be checked against the image.
10. *ein geborner Caper*: a man born at the Cape.
11. The Governor, Cornelis Jacob van de Graaff, had left the Cape in 1791; the acting head of government in December 1792 was Johan Isaac Rhenius. He may be the "commander Rhenius" of A363596, entry 03.
12. The transcription reads *daß er uns in Craale brächte*; the normalised column has *nach Kraal*. A kraal (Dutch *kraal*) was a Khoekhoe settlement of huts, and also a cattle enclosure. The sense seems to be "among the kraals" of the Khoekhoe.
13. Georg Schmidt, who worked at Baviaanskloof from 1737 to 1744.
14. *die Tigerhoek*: a farm and area on the Sonderend River, east of Baviaanskloof.
