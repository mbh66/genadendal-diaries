<!-- lines 2350-2390 -->
"After I had made the necessary arrangements with my dear brothers Daniel Schwinn and Johann Christian Kühnel, I travelled on 4 March (1793), accompanied by their brotherly blessing, to Baas Teunis at Soetemelksvlei, to go with him to Cape Town. He received me with every kindness, and after everything needed for the journey had been arranged, we set off with two wagons on the 6th at 1 o'clock in the afternoon. At 6 o'clock in the evening we met a cart whose driver had letters for Baas Teunis, and handed them to him, adding that he also had a small cask of wine on board. Baas Teunis said to me: 'The wine is for you'; and when I answered that we had not ordered any, he gave me a letter that was enclosed in his. Now I saw that it really was so, and that the wine was from Mr Le Sueur [?] [1], from whom I had not expected any such thing. He wrote among other things that since our departure from the Cape had happened so quickly and he had not given us a breakfast, he had thought it would be fitting to send us a small cask of wine; and he wished us God's blessing. I had the letter and the cask of wine taken to the post at Soetemelksvlei, and we continued on our way and drove through the night.

<!-- lines 2391-2441 -->
"On the way an ox fell from the second wagon [2], so we had to drive in the dark with two fewer, and now had twelve oxen in front of each wagon. After we had crossed the Bot River on the 7th at half past midnight, we outspanned, let the 24 oxen graze, made a fire and drank coffee under the open sky. Then we sat down on the wagon and slept until 9 o'clock. But when we wanted to inspan now, our oxen were gone. Baas Teunis, I and four Hottentots went to look for them, but did not find them until 1 o'clock in the afternoon. At 4 o'clock we drove on. Baas Teunis said: 'If only we were up the Houhoek already!' But when we came to the foot of this mountain, our wagons were already going very slowly, and when we had climbed for a quarter of an hour our oxen could go no further at all. So we had to outspan them and put on 20 other oxen. On foot it is about an hour and a half's climb to the top of this very steep mountain. I went on foot, and when I was at the top I had to turn back, for the wagon had stuck not far from the murderous spot, in the deep ascent, and could not be moved forward. So all of us there were had to push the wagon back, and at 7 o'clock in the evening we at last reached the top of the mountain with much trouble. As I said, it is very steep and virtually solid rock, so that if one stays sitting on the wagon the jolts make one's very insides ache.

<!-- lines 2441-2484 -->
"Then we drove on, and on the 8th at 2 o'clock we crossed the Palmiet River, and at half past 2 came to the Hottentots Holland Kloof. Here the oxen must drag the wagon up a steep staircase of rock steps, each as high as a chair. We put on 20 oxen again and got up the mountain at 4 o'clock. Now we went down on foot; but a *Poeter* [?] [3], so violent a wind, was blowing that we had to hold on to one another. I had my roquelaure [4] on, but had to take it off so as not to be blown down from the terrible height. When we had got down safely, I sat down in a cave (*in een Spelonke op de Kloof*) [5], poured out my heart before the Saviour with many tears and asked him to strengthen my brothers who had stayed at home, and give them what they needed. I was ashamed before the dear Saviour that he has called me, a poor sinner, to so important a task, since I am so wretched a creature; and I was a wonder to myself that I should still live to see such a thing. For when I came to the congregation I had always thought: if only I might become the kind of brother who had nothing else to see to than just to earn his bread, however poor it might be. I said: 'Dear Saviour, thou hast chosen something poor for thy servant.' I felt the nearness of the dear Saviour very powerfully, and so went down the road comforted.

<!-- lines 2485-2527 -->
"When I had got down, I saw our wagon up on the height, [in] a spray [of sparks] [6] which looked marvellous, and came from the back wheels being locked with chains and striking fire as they dragged over the stones. At 7 o'clock we had got safely down the mountain and outspanned our oxen to let them rest a little. A fire was made and coffee cooked, and afterwards we lay down to sleep under the open sky [7]. At 12 o'clock Baas Teunis said: 'Shall we not roast a piece of meat?' I answered: 'We have no gridiron.' But he called a Hottentot and told him to roast a piece of meat. (We had brought meat with us.) 'Well,' I thought, 'how will that work?' The Hottentot took a little stick, split it at one end, bent it into a two-pronged fork, stuck a piece of meat on it, made a fire, let it burn right down, then laid the meat on the ashes and afterwards turned it once. When it was done, he laid it on the grass, for we had no table, and no plates either. Meanwhile I took a field stone, which I could have at a pinch, in place of a plate or a dish. I had no fork either. At 3 o'clock we drove on and came to Hottentots Holland, where we stopped at the widow Morgel's.

<!-- lines 2528-2581 -->
"She was friendly and offered us all kinds of things; but I asked only for a glass of water, which she handed me at once. After a stop of half an hour we continued our journey and at 8 o'clock arrived at Jacob Contermann's, where Baas Teunis had meant to stop. He was very friendly and asked who I was. When I answered that I was one of the missionaries among the Hottentots, he was very glad to make my acquaintance, and I had to drink a glass of wine with him. He is an old man who could still remember our predecessor, Brother Schmidt, and we had a pleasant conversation together about our salvation. When I asked him where I could stay if I should ever travel to the Cape, as Brother Schmidt often did, taking his night's lodging with a farmer, he answered: 'With me, with me, my friend!' After we had rested here for an hour, we set out again, drove through the night once more, and on the 9th came to the Dunes [8]. Here we outspanned by a wretched hut belonging to a Negro and asked for a pot to cook meat in. The Negro gave us one which, if it had been whole, would have held a good 6 cans (*mengels*) [9]; but because it was broken, only 2 cans went into it. That was a miserable business of cooking! And the Negro had no lid at all. Then we went on at 3 o'clock and arrived in the evening, happy and safe and sound, at our dear friend Martin Schmidt's (in the Saxstraat) [10]. He was as glad as a child, and so eager to hear something of our settlement that I could not tell him enough.

<!-- lines 2582-2603 -->
"On the 10th a farmer and his wife came to our old friend Mr Schmidt, because he had heard that a Herrnhuter was there. (I happened not to be in the house.) But when I came back and Mr Schmidt told them it was me, they gaped at me from head to foot, so full of wonder that I thought: 'Are these people in their right minds?' Then we sat down at the table; I began a conversation and steered it to the matter of our salvation. At this they changed completely, and at last said that I must on no account pass their house if I ever came through their district. I answered: 'But I am a Herrnhuter.' 'Yes, yes,' they replied, 'that is all the same.'

<!-- lines 2604-2641 -->
"On the 11th I went to the Castle and had an audience with the Commandant, Mr Rhenius [11], who was very pleased to see me. Since the time was too short for a full conversation, as he had several more people to hear, he said to me: 'Come back tomorrow evening at half past 6, and then you must stay with me until 9.' So I took a friendly leave, and he accompanied me as far as the street, which is a great honour. Now I went to visit Colonel Gordon. He said at once: 'Why, Brother, you are here! Take a chair. How are things?' I then told him one thing and another. Then he said: 'Here are grapes, do eat.' So I took a big bunch; at that moment some distinguished officers came in, to whom I wanted to show due honour and courtesy; but he said: 'Brother, just go on eating your grapes.' I stayed half an hour with him and then went to the honourable Commissioners [12]. They gave me an audience, and though one usually speaks with them standing, I had to sit down with them. They asked how things were with us; and after I had told them everything in detail, in which the talk also came to [...] [13], they gave me their hands and wished me God's blessing.

<!-- lines 2642-2701 -->
"On the 12th I visited the awakened people who hold together [14] in their houses. On the 13th I went to the (Honourable) Commandant Rhenius. He offered me a pipe of tobacco at once, which I accepted. Then he asked whether I would rather drink wine or beer. I asked for beer. He also showed me his very valuable collection of paintings, and likewise of shells and stones found in this country. On this occasion I was able to speak with him about our place, as I also had our written documents from the authorities with me. I asked whether we could not buy the place, and received the answer: 'That is not necessary; the place is yours.' I asked further what we would then have to pay each year. Answer: 'Nothing.' I further expressed the wish that this had been stated in the documents, which contained nothing about it; for though I was fully assured that as long as Your Honour lived we need fear no difficulty on this point, yet if another came in your place, he might perhaps want to take the place back and drive us away. To this he replied: 'Even if 10 governors came, they could not do it, because the Council of Seventeen [15] has so ordered it.' Question: 'How is it, then, with buying and selling between us and the Hottentots?' Answer: 'You may buy and sell what you like, only not so as to carry on actual trade or barter. But what you need for your own consumption, and what they also need, you may buy and accept without hesitation, as I do myself, giving them in return what may be of use to them, if I have anything of the kind.' When I then took my leave, he gave me a slave with a lantern to go with me, because it was very dark, and at the same time invited me to dine with him the day after tomorrow.

<!-- lines 2701-2747 -->
"On the 14th I visited the Reformed ministers (who were very dry); I also drank tea with one of them, named Vleck [16]. At midday I dined with Colonel Gordon, who promised me a good dog, which one very much needs in this country. On the 15th I went to dine with the Commandant Rhenius; the conversation was chiefly about the politics of the fatherland. His wife was very pleased to see me and talk with me; and both showed me as much honour (as if I were a learned minister). After the meal I had to look at his cabinet once more; then I took my leave and visited Pastor Kolver [17], with whom I had a pleasant conversation about the sufferings of the Saviour. On the 16th I visited Colonel Gordon, who ordered his adjutant to provide me with powder and flints. So I went with him to his house, where I had to sit down and drink a little glass of sweet wine. At parting he wished me God's blessing. On the 19th I paid farewell visits at the Castle to the Commandant and Colonel Gordon. They wished us God's blessing and promised to do everything possible for the furtherance of the Gospel. I spent the rest of my time visiting the awakened people; and after I had taken leave of all good friends, I began my return journey from Cape Town on the 20th at 7 o'clock in the morning.

<!-- lines 2747-2792 -->
"At 9 o'clock we came to a country estate called the Brewery [18], where we had to stop. And since the minister van Lier was staying on another country estate nearby, I visited him. He could not speak for weakness, but only gave me his hand. I soon left him, but there was a blessed feeling about him. We stayed the night at the Brewery, and one party after another drove up to visit Mr van Lier. On the way I had already spoken with some good friends, who told me beforehand how things stood with him. On the 21st at 4 o'clock in the morning a messenger came with the news that he had passed away [19]. At 8 o'clock we drove on, came on the 22nd to the Hottentots Holland Kloof, and on the 23rd at 3 o'clock in the morning to the warm bath. I stayed there the day and went into the bath once too, but could not stay in it long; for the water is so hot that one can scald oneself if one steps in suddenly. At 9 o'clock in the evening we set out again, came in the night of the 24th to the post at Soetemelksvlei, and I arrived on the 25th at half past 11 at night, full of praise and thanks, with my dear brothers at Baviaanskloof. The Saviour graciously kept me from all harm, although travelling in this country is very dangerous and one has to pass mountains and roads of which one can have no idea in the fatherland. The reason I had to stay so long at the Cape was Baas Teunis's many affairs with the Company."

## Notes

1. The transcription reads *der Wein vom Eß-Le sieur war*. The same sender appears in entry 04 (line 2243) as *dem Eß⸗Sieur*. The name may be Le Sueur; a Captain Lesueur appears in A363597. To be checked against the image.
2. *fiel ein Ochs*: an ox dropped out, died or collapsed. The next clause says "two fewer", perhaps a pair.
3. The transcription reads *es wehete ein Poeter und ſo heftiger Wind*. *Poeter* is not a known word; perhaps a local name for the wind, or a misreading. To be checked against the image.
4. *Roquelaure*: a knee-length cloak.
5. Dutch, "in a cave on the Kloof", as the diarist wrote it.
6. The transcription reads *auf der Höhe jenes Sprühen*, which is garbled; the sense is a spray of sparks.
7. The transcription reads *unter ſtrengem Himmel*, "under a severe sky"; the normalised column has *freiem*, "open". Earlier in this entry the transcription has *unter freyem Himmel*.
8. *de Duÿn*: the dunes of the Cape Flats, on the road from Hottentots Holland to Cape Town.
9. *Mingles*: the Dutch *mengel*, a liquid measure of about 1.2 litres.
10. *Saxſtraat* in the transcription: a street in Cape Town, not identified; perhaps a misreading. To be checked against the image.
11. Johan Isaac Rhenius, acting head of the Cape government; see entry 01, note 11.
12. The Commissioners-General Nederburgh and Frykenius; see entry 01, note 6.
13. The transcription reads *wobey die Rede auch auf Geis kam*; the normalised column has *Geist*, "spirit". The word is unclear. To be checked against the image.
14. *die ſich zuſammen haltenden erweckten Leute*: the society of awakened people in Cape Town described in entry 01.
15. The Heeren XVII, the directors of the Dutch East India Company.
16. *Vleck*: one of the Reformed ministers in Cape Town; not identified.
17. *Paſtor Colver*: probably Andreas Lutgerus Kolver, minister of the Lutheran church in Cape Town.
18. *Buytenplaats*: Dutch, a country estate outside the town. The Brewery (*Brouwery*) was an estate at Newlands.
19. Helperus Ritzema van Lier died on 21 March 1793; see entry 01, note 5.
