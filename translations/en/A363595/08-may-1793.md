<!-- lines 3092-3125 -->
On 1 May Brother Marsveld rode to the post to Baas Teunis. He had asked for a visit. The reason was that he wanted to know how things were going in the fatherland, since we had received letters. He was born in Maastricht [1], but has been here for 30 years. On the 2nd Brother Marsveld came home again. On the 3rd a farmer named Heinrich Fernhold came to stay the night with us; he came from the Paarl and was travelling to the Kaffer River [2]. On the 4th we were busy in the garden closing it in, so that no cattle could get in, since we have sown a small piece to get some vegetables. As today was the festival [3] of the single sisters, we remembered them in our morning blessing and commended these choirs to the dear Saviour for a blessing in a couple of verses. In the evening we partook of Holy Communion, with the dear Saviour acknowledging us poor ones. On the 5th we had a visit from a man named Redlichhausen, from Ganzekraal, six hours from here. He is a wagon maker. He and his family are very friendly towards us. He attended our church, and towards evening rode home again.

<!-- lines 3125-3158 -->
On the 6th we heard many unpleasant rumours, namely that a spirit of revolt against the government is rising strongly among the farmers. The two Commissioners have introduced various new taxes, which exist in Holland but not yet here: for example, a fixed sum to be paid every year for each slave, wagon and so on. The farmers will not submit to this. For this reason 300 farmers were at the Cape three weeks ago and asked for this ordinance to be withdrawn, because they could not bear this new tax. The answer was: "What is written is written." Upon this they went away. Recently 500 farmers were at the Cape again and told the government that they neither could nor would pay the new taxes; and if the government did not revoke the proclamations, they would tear them down and take up arms. What will come of it now, God knows. On the 7th we announced to the Hottentots in the meeting that from now on we meant to begin instruction with the children twice a week, and fixed Wednesdays and Saturdays for it. We also heard that all the Hottentots had to go to the Cape, because the French were on their way to the Cape [4].

<!-- lines 3159-3187 -->
On the 8th we began the instruction with the children; the number was 31. The class of children includes all who are not yet married, since for the present we cannot divide them into more than three classes, namely the men, the women and the children. Each of us has one class to look after. The number of those who come to school is about 80. The children were very attentive; the birth of our dear Saviour was read to them and spoken about thankfully. Today we also received a letter from Baas Teunis, brought by a Hottentot captain. Baas Teunis wrote that we should let all the Hottentots who can handle a gun go to the Cape. The captain had orders to command them, and they had to leave tomorrow. We could do nothing about it, and arranged for all who were leaving to come to us once more. We thought we would not have many left, for they can all handle a gun, which they learn with the farmers. No Hottentot may have a gun of his own; that is forbidden.

<!-- lines 3188-3225 -->
On the 9th, Ascension Day, we meant to hold church properly and set before them the importance of this day; but it was a very disrupted day. At 10 o'clock the captain came with all those who were going to the Cape. Two were left behind, a lame man and a sick one. When they were together, we asked them earnestly to think of what they had heard so far of Jesus, their Saviour, and to turn to him in all circumstances, and in a prayer on our knees we commended them to God's keeping and protection. How it will go meanwhile with the women and children, God knows, and where they are to get anything to live on; for now is one of the worst seasons, since there are no fruits and no vegetables for them to live on. Several have already had nothing but honey, which they look for in the mountains, for their keep. The Hottentot who is in our service was allowed to stay here. The captain said he would leave us this one, which pleased us very much; for when we have something to fetch, how are we to get it, since the others are all gone? We also still have so much work in the garden; we are expecting sheep any day; who would herd them? On the 10th, since the men are now gone, the men's school lapses for the time being. So we begin the school with the children, since their number is large, at half past 7 in the morning.

<!-- lines 3226-3264 -->
On the 11th, as we held no school today because it was Saturday, we were busy digging in the garden. We have to be busy at it whenever we have a little time, because the last sowing [5] will soon be ready for transplanting. We had meant to have some digging done, and thought there was still time. If we had known that all the Hottentots would have to leave, we would have had it done long ago. Now we can get nothing done, and not much gets finished in a day, for first one must cut out bushes and thorns, then one must dig 1½ ells deep, because everything is full of roots. On the 12th we were much in spirit in our dear Herrnhut, because they were having their congregation festival [6]. We had heard much about the warm bath (4 hours from here) and had promised the man who is in charge of it, whom we had got to know at the post, to visit him soon; we also thought it necessary to get acquainted with the farmers of this district, because a great deal of false talk about us is going round. So Brother Schwinn and Brother Kühnel decided to make this journey today. We rode there at 7 o'clock in the morning with our Hottentot servant, because we did not know the way, as one can still get over the river now; for the rainy season is expected to begin any day.

<!-- lines 3265-3333 -->
Two hours from here we came to the first farmer, named Maree, and had breakfast there. His wife had already been to see us. An hour from there we came to another farmer, but rode past. After another hour we came to a very fine place, which looked very grand, and there we stopped. Here the people came out to meet us and asked who we were. Answer: the teachers from Baviaanskloof; they knew of that. They were very friendly, had the horses unsaddled and fed at once, and wine brought for us at once. They asked whether the Hottentots were learning well. When we said that for the present we had the best hopes, they seemed satisfied. Soon, when the horses had eaten a little, we thanked them and rode on to the warm bath. The superintendent there is called Andreas Otte [7]. When we got to the bath he was not at home, but he was not far away, and he was sent for at once. He was very glad that we visited him. There were few visitors to the bath at present. A farmer and his wife promised to visit us when they were well again. They said they would like to hear how the Hottentots were learning, and seemed to be quite pleasant people. They had heard various false things said about us, about which we told them the truth. For example: that we had been sent here by the Company, and got our pay from it; that all the Hottentots we taught also got their keep from the Company; and so on. Towards evening we bathed. The bath is up on the hill, and the water fairly hot, so that one cannot bear it for more than a couple of minutes. There is no cold water that one could let in; the hill is full of warm water. The man said one could make 20 baths from it, for wherever one dug one would find springs of warm water. A short hour from the warm spring, down the hill, there is a strong spring of really fresh water. We stayed the night there. He wanted us to stay at least another day, but time did not allow it. He also wanted to give us various garden vegetables to take with us, but we could take nothing but cabbage and seedlings. He is a German, a pastor's son, and has studied; he came here as a soldier, and the government made him superintendent of the warm bath.

<!-- lines 3333-3418 -->
Early the next morning we rode home again. We took another road, to get to know still more farmers. About an hour from there we came to the first one. We meant to ride past; 3 men came riding who got there before us, and they stopped there. When we had passed the house, they called after us, why did we not come in, and so we rode back again. They showed us every possible friendliness, asked at once how things were going and whether the Hottentots were learning well. The other 3, who arrived with us and were riding to the Cape for guard duty, were also very well disposed. At parting they wished us much success in keeping school. The farmer's name is Peter Meelman. An hour from there we came to another farmer, and went in. Here too we were asked who we were; when we said, the teachers from Baviaanskloof, the people at once became so friendly towards us that it was a wonder; they did not know what to do first. The farmer's name is Otto Fries. Then we rode on to Christian Kuns, another hour from there. He is a very rich man. There we had the horses unsaddled and ate our midday meal. Here the house was full of farmers; field corporals and field commandants were here too, to command the Hottentots of this district to the Cape. Here we came as if into a bees' nest; they were all very friendly towards us, and we were asked many questions. One asked by what we taught the Hottentots. Answer: by the doctrine we find in the Bible. He said he had heard so much about us, among other things that we had quite a different doctrine; if our doctrine was according to the Bible, then it was no different; he had long wished to know what our doctrine was. Before he asked this question he begged us not to take it amiss. We were also asked whether we taught them writing too. We said that it had not got so far yet; the most necessary thing was that they learn to read God's word; when they could do that, we would first see what was to be done about writing. They said that this was not good; when asked what harm it could do, they had no answer to give. If they had had to tell the truth, the answer would have been: then we can no longer cheat them so. Then the field corporals rode away. When they had gone, our Hottentot came and said he had been commanded to the Cape and was to leave tomorrow. We were not pleased to hear this. Mrs Kuns said that we need not let him go, for it stood in the ordinance that whoever had two Hottentots in his service was to give up one of them, and we had only one, so we could give up none. If he had told us at once, we would have spoken with the commandant. After a stay of a couple of hours we rode on again. From there we had another 2 hours and met no more farmers.

<!-- lines 3419-3479 -->
We and the horses were quite exhausted, for we are not used to so much riding, and the Hottentots' horses are in poor condition too, for they get nothing to eat but what they find in the field; and since there is now such drought that one finds hardly a green blade of grass, what are they to live on? And still it will not rain, and it is still fairly warm. Towards evening we came home again. We have seen and spoken with many farmers, but they are all very friendly to our faces, and behind our backs they grumble and would rather wish us who knows where. When one talks with one of them, he seems to be our friend; afterwards we hear that he is our enemy. On the way we also heard that the government has taken the new taxes off the farmers again, and that things are now becoming quiet among them again, and that they are now willing to do service in the present circumstances. There is much complaint that they now have to go to the Cape to do guard duty, since it falls just at the time of sowing. Even someone who is only just beginning has to go off to the Cape; who is to plough and sow? The Hottentots are gone too. God grant that the danger that threatens us may pass, and be graciously turned away from us! From the Cape, everyone we know is sending [8] their best things up here into the country, and many families want to come into the country to their friends or acquaintances. The fear of the French is great. When we came home, we considered what we should do about our Hottentot servant, whether we should let him go or not, so that it might not turn to our disadvantage either. We wrote to Baas Teunis and asked him for good advice, and on the 14th in the morning sent someone there with the letter. He wrote back that since he was the only one we had, and we must have someone, young as he was, we should not let him go; and we meant to follow this. He also wrote that one of us should come to fetch the money his wife had brought for us from the Cape. His wife had gone to the Cape a fortnight before, and because we needed money, we had asked her to bring some. Brother Schwinn went there on the 15th to fetch it.

<!-- lines 3480-3505 -->
We have had letters from good friends at the Cape; they write that everything is in unrest and the greatest perplexity. If it is confirmed that war has been declared between Holland, England and France, how may things be with our dear brothers and sisters in Zeist? How things are with Neuwied, God knows! We think much of these dear congregations and commend them for protection to the one who holds fast in every need. We long very much to know how they are, but for now we cannot expect any news. So far, thank God, we have lived in peace; God only preserve it further! On the 16th Brother Schwinn came back from the post and said that Baas Teunis had said ships were to sail, but by another route; if we wanted to write, we should do so. We took this work in hand at once, wrote as much as time allowed, and on the 19th sent it to the post, from where it is sent on to the Cape.

<!-- lines 3506-3560 -->
The 19th: today, the first day of Whitsun, we had a very stormy day. In the night before a storm had already risen, which began to take the roof off our house. As soon as we got up, we had to see to tying [poles] onto the roof in the morning, so that the wind should not carry it away. When we were done, we had our usual [9] morning blessing; and because we were expecting visitors, we held a service of worship soon afterwards, so as not to be disturbed. We had to take care that the dear Saviour graciously acknowledged us [10]. We implored him that he would graciously acknowledge us and the poor Hottentots with thy Spirit, instruct us through thy Spirit, and give us words that are after thy heart and mind. At 10 o'clock we had a sermon, to which all the women came; their number is over 30. They came to all the meetings, and none of them misses one except in the greatest need. The talk was on today's Gospel; they were very attentive, as they are every day. It stormed all day, and on through the following night, and it also began to rain; this was very good. Because the weather was so unpleasant we had no visitors, which was very welcome to us. On the 20th, the second day of the festival, we again held a sermon and told them what this festival is about, and also remembered in prayer those who are at the Cape. In the afternoon it was very stormy again; we had to tie poles onto our house again, so that we should keep the roof. Our house stands just in front of a valley that runs up between the mountains; when a strong wind blows, it comes straight down the valley, and our house is the first thing it gets hold of. One would believe that the *queimason* [11], or winter, is setting in. Because among the Christians in this country no more than two holy days are known, we now also ended the holy days, sang Liturgy number 20 in the evening [12], and began the schools again the next day, the 21st.

<!-- lines 3560-3615 -->
On the 22nd we meant to go to Baas Teunis's son to congratulate him on his new estate. He was married at Stellenbosch and is said to be at home now with his young wife. Holding a wedding is not the fashion here; but after some time they hold a merrymaking, as they call it, and then it goes on as at a wedding, and the dancing is not forgotten either. Since we thought we might be invited too, we meant to get in first with our congratulations, so as to be better able to decline the invitation afterwards. But we were prevented by heavy rain. On the 23rd it rained very hard again, and it was very cold with it too; and when we got up in the morning, the high mountain behind our house was quite white on top, covered with snow. So one can reckon the beginning of this winter from the first day of Whitsun, namely from 19 May. The storm on 19 and 20 May also broke the peas coming up in our garden and covered various sprouting seeds with sand, so that we must sow again. The 24th and 25th were fine warm days, it is true. On the 26th it rained fairly hard again. The Sonderend River is now so big that one can get neither over nor back. The strong water has torn away part of the dam that was made to lead the water to our house and into the garden; this made us much work. We fear that more of it will wash away, for the dam is very poor, and we are not able to make it hold. To have it made would cost far too much money, for it ought to be of stones. There are stones enough, but they are too big for us to move. The dam is 500 paces long and must be 4 ells high in many places, for the water runs round the mountain, where it is deep on one side. Today we had to let church lapse because of the heavy rain.

<!-- lines 3615-3652 -->
Since we have been given some hens as a present, the Hottentots had built a coop for them. The wall was of clay, and the roof of clay too; it was not yet properly dry when the rain came. Because it rained so hard, the whole coop fell in today. From the 27th to the 30th it was fine again, and fairly hot too. In these days we were busy (as far as time allowed us) building another hen coop; we made it of bundles [of brushwood] [13] from the river. For if the hens do not have a coop that is tightly closed, an animal they call the *muishond* [14] comes and takes them at night and eats them. Before we had a coop it took 5 of ours too, which forced us to make a coop. On the 31st it rained again. The two Bastards who live here came back from the Cape. They had asked for permission to go home for the time being to sow [?] [15] something, which they have been given until further orders. One of them also means to do something for us, so that we need not buy everything. The land where sowing is to be done is on the far side of the river, for on this side the land is not so good: here one must have manure, but on the other side not, and we have no manure. But now the river is big, so that one cannot get over; if it does not soon go down, not much will come of the sowing.

## Notes

1. Baas Teunis. The transcription reads *Maſtricht*.
2. *Caffer = Revier*: probably the frontier region of the Great Fish River, which the colonists called the boundary with the Xhosa. The diarists' word for Xhosa people is kept; see `glossary.csv`.
3. The transcription reads *das Geſchäft der ledigen Schweſtern*, "the business of the single sisters"; probably a misreading of *Feſt*. The Single Sisters' choir festival was kept on 4 May.
4. France had declared war on the Dutch Republic on 1 February 1793, and the Cape expected a French attack.
5. *Geſäūbe* in the transcription, *Gesäue* in the normalised column: the seedlings from the last sowing.
6. Herrnhut kept 12 May as its congregation festival, in memory of the Brotherly Agreement of 12 May 1727.
7. The superintendent of the warm bath (see A363596). He should not be confused with the field corporal Andreas Otto of `glossary.csv`, though the names are close. To be checked.
8. The transcription reads *ſchreiben ... ihre beſten Sachen hier ins Land*, "write their best things into the country"; probably a misreading of *ſchicken*, "send".
9. The transcription reads *Jūſt. Morgenſegen*; the normalised column has *gerechten*. Perhaps an abbreviation of *gewöhnlichen*, "usual". To be checked against the image.
10. *Wir mūßten ſorgen*, "we had to take care", in both columns; perhaps a misreading of *flehen*, "implore". The prayer then turns from "he" to "thy", as in the manuscript.
11. *Queimason*: the Cape Dutch *kwaai moesson*, the winter season; see entry 03, note 3. The transcription reads *Man wird gelaūbt*, "one is believed"; the English gives the sense.
12. *die Litūrgie Nūmero 20*: a liturgy numbered in the Moravian liturgy book of the time; not identified here.
13. *Bürden*: bundles, probably of reeds or brushwood.
14. *Muuſe⸗Hund*: the Cape Dutch *muishond*, a name for the polecat and for small mongooses.
15. The transcription reads *um etwas zu hören*, "to hear something"; the next sentences, about sowing, suggest a misreading of *ſäen*, "sow". To be checked against the image.
