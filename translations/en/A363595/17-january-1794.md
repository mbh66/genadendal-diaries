<!-- lines 7477-7513 -->
On 1 January 1794 the sermon was again very well attended. In the afternoon we had our adoration, in which we gave ourselves anew to our dear Lord for his service. On the 2nd the schools began again. A woman came forward and said that it was her whole purpose to live only for the Saviour, and asked us to take closer care of her. Since we kept hearing more and more that the land at the Sergeants River is to be given away, and also that a place for a farmer is to be laid out close to us, we were in great difficulty; for then our place would be as good as lost. We resolved to let Brother Marsveld travel to the Cape to learn the truth from the government. On the 4th towards evening the field corporal, Andreas Otto, came and said that he had orders from the Landdrost to inspect a certain spot near us where a gentleman wants to settle. The place in view is 2 or 3 minutes from us. By the laws of the country every colonist must have the land free for half an hour round his place; so we would be on someone else's land, and in the end the Hottentots would not be able to stay here. Much in this matter is quite incomprehensible to us, so it was resolved that Brother Marsveld should set out for the Cape as early as the 6th.

<!-- lines 7514-7540 -->
On the 5th 6 gentlemen and 7 ladies came here from Hottentots Holland to attend the sermon. It was held under the pear tree, and the gentlefolk could not wonder enough at the stillness of the Hottentots and their beautiful singing. Had this company stayed over midday, we would not have known what to give them to eat; but luckily they drove on to young Teunis. Today we held our conference and together implored the Saviour to help us in our present anxious circumstances. 2 persons were chosen as candidates for baptism. We also thought of tomorrow's Festival of the Heathen, and would have had joy in proposing some for holy baptism; but since Brother Marsveld's journey could not be put off, and on such occasions we like to be all three together, it was resolved to let the keeping of this day stand over for this time.

<!-- lines 7540-7578 -->
Early on the 6th Brother Marsveld left us with a Hottentot, accompanied by our best wishes for his blessing. Most of the men who had been here on leave also went back to the Cape. Several declared that what hurt them most in going away was that they would no longer hear God's word; and we told them that the Saviour was present everywhere and would hear them if they held to him. On the 8th 2 women came forward and said that they had no rest day or night and wished from their hearts to become children of God. On the 9th several more came with the same concern. With one of our candidates for baptism we must have much patience. She is the one who learned to read from a former pupil of the late Brother Schmidt, and she has read a great deal in the Revelation of John and is full of visions. We have often told her that she does not understand this book and would do better to read the 4 Evangelists. But she comes time after time and tells what she has seen and heard again this night. It is altogether the way of the Hottentots to dream much and vividly. Otherwise we must say of this person that she is concerned from her heart to be saved.

<!-- lines 7579-7619 -->
On the 10th a woman told us that she had recently got drunk and been left lying on the road, and for that her husband had given her a sound beating. We said: "You deserved that." Answer: "Yes, so I did." They are often very open-hearted. On the 11th 4 were with us who declared a great longing to be saved. One who had recently become a candidate for baptism said that her thoughts were not yet still, but her greatest thought was to live wholly for the dear Saviour. Today we had to speak seriously to the children in their meeting, because several had behaved badly and frivolously. There was much weeping from big and small, and afterwards 10 came and named the guilty ones. We spoke with the parents, and they gave the children due punishment. 2 girls of about 17, who had not been present, came to us afterwards in the greatest distress, because the others had told them that they might no longer come to school and meeting, since they had got drunk at the harvest and behaved badly. They promised to do everything well, and so we allowed them to come again. This permission matters very much to them. The late Brother Schmidt often complained that they had no liking for learning. We have had no such complaint so far; if it lasted from morning into the night they would not tire, and they are always there punctually at the appointed time.

<!-- lines 7619-7645 -->
On the 12th the room could hold only the smallest part of the hearers at the sermon. We had thought (since most of the men have gone again) that we would have more room; but so many come from far away that we do not know where all the people come from. In the afternoon Baas Teunis was here with Mr Ferscke [1] from the Cape, the son-in-law of a certain Mr Dewed. This Mr Dewed is the one who has allotted the places here to new colonists, and his son-in-law, who looked at everything here, said he would report to him how everything was, and that the purpose would not be achieved here. In our difficulty this was some comfort to us. On the 14th 6 women and 4 children came and asked leave to come to school and to the meeting. For some time there has been a special stir; people come from far away, and not a day passes without some making known their concern to be saved.

<!-- lines 7645-7687 -->
On the 16th towards evening Brother Marsveld came back to us safe and well. Of his journey he reports as follows: "On 6 January I left with the blessing of my brothers, with a Hottentot who was to show me the way between the mountains and rocks. In the evening I spent the night at a farmer's. On the afternoon of the 7th I came to Stellenbosch, whose Landdrost has command over our district. I did not find him at home, so I rode half an hour further to a farmer, with whom I stayed the night. He asked about the progress of our mission. Early on the 8th I went to the Landdrost again and found him at home. I told him that we had heard our place would be taken from us, but that it stood in our documents that he was to see to our protection; it was said there that we should not have to fear the slightest hindrance for our persons and our work; and since there was now talk of our having to leave our place altogether, we could not help turning to him about it. He seemed to know about the matter, and gave me to understand that the opinion of the Company in the fatherland was that we should go to the Bushmen, to make peace there; for the rest, I should speak about it with the Honourable Commissioner [2] at the Cape, for he himself could do nothing further in it. So I travelled on, and on the evening of the following day arrived at our friend Martinus Schmidt's in Cape Town.

<!-- lines 7687-7728 -->
"He was surprised that I came alone, and said: 'That must mean something good!' I told him our circumstances, and he gave me good hope. On the 9th I went to the Commissioner, and after he had heard everything and examined my papers, he said I should come again in 2 days and receive an answer. Commandant Rhenius, whom I then visited, promised to do what he could in the matter. From there I went to Colonel Gordon and was received very kindly. He had just dined at the Commissioner's and faithfully promised to speak for us; and meanwhile I dined with his family, with whom I had pleasant conversations. I visited several friends, who were glad of the blessing on the mission. Here and there I had to tell a great deal, among other things of the singing of the Hottentots. All were of the opinion that no one could or would take our place from us, and promised their services. On the 13th I went to the Commissioner to get his answer. He told me I could go home comforted: as for our place, the matter that had been put forward would be changed. He wished us God's blessing. I would gladly have had something in writing about it, but he said that what we had already was enough, and plain and clear. I pressed to buy the land, but he thought that was not necessary; nor did they want any tax from us. He said the previous Commissioners had allowed us to live there, and so it should remain. On the 14th I left again, and on the 16th arrived with my dear brothers."

<!-- lines 7728-7753 -->
The diary now continues: on the 17th Brother Marsveld reported to us in detail on his business at the Cape. It was not quite what we had wished, namely more written security for the future, in case persons other than the present ones should come into the government. We also learned that we may no longer use our bell [3]. It has already been mentioned that Mr Brand, when he visited us, set our minds at rest about it for the time being; and since then he had also written to Baas Teunis that he had spoken about it with the Commissioners, and that we could ring unhindered. But since then so many complaints about it had come in again (which have their ground in the church order here) that Mr Brand himself instructed Brother Marsveld to stop the ringing again. This was made known to our brothers [and sisters] in the evening meeting, and now they must see how they can come at the right time.

<!-- lines 7754-7786 -->
On the 19th the sermon had to be held under the pear trees again. On the 22nd was the speaking [4] of the candidates for baptism, and we could rejoice at their astonishing declarations. On the 23rd a small child was buried, and towards evening its mother died too. We had not been able to speak much with her, since in her illness she was seldom in her right mind. Many are ill now, among them 2 of our candidates for baptism. One could not visit them without pity, because of their great poverty. They lie on a sheepskin spread on the bare earth, and have another skin for a blanket; their pillow is the tobacco bag. On the 25th in our conference 3 persons were chosen for holy baptism, and for the first time 2 men, namely the husbands of our Anna Maria and Eva; the third is a woman who formerly had a burgher's slave for her husband, but is now quite separated from him. We resolved to hold the baptism on a weekday, because on Sunday it is all too crowded with us. It took place on the 28th with a powerful feeling of the presence of God. Those baptised were named Jakob, Johannes and Hanna.

<!-- lines 7787-7801 -->
In the afternoon we held a lovefeast with our 10 baptised, where the Saviour likewise graciously owned us. In the meeting of the candidates for baptism on the 29th a special stir could be felt. On the 30th a woman came to us who is especially awakened. While still some distance from us she burst into loud weeping, and for tears could say nothing except that her heart was so troubled. On the 31st 2 families moved here from the Tigerhoek, 8 hours from here, with the desire to be converted.

## Notes

1. *H. Ferſché* and *Hrn Dewed*: not identified. Dewed is said to be the official who allotted farms to new colonists.
2. *dem edlen Herrn Commiſſarius*: probably Abraham Josias Sluysken, who governed the Cape from September 1793 with the title of Commissioner; the "previous Commissioners" are the Commissioners-General Nederburgh and Frykenius (see `glossary.csv`).
3. Both columns read *daß wir unſere Pferde nicht mehr brauchen dürfen*, "that we may no longer use our horses", and then *ungehindert treiben* and *das Treiben*, "drive". The complaints grounded in the church order of the colony, and the members having to "see how they can come at the right time", show that the matter is the bell, which the Reformed church allowed only to itself; the same misreading of *Klocke* as *Pferde* occurs in the October entry (line 6577). *treiben* may also be a misreading, perhaps of *läuten*, "ring". To be checked against the image.
4. The transcription reads *das Begräbniß der Tauf-Candidaten, und wir konten uns des Gegengig über ihre erſtaunenswürdige Erklärungen freuen*, "the burial of the candidates", which cannot be right with "their astonishing declarations". Probably *das Sprechen*, "the speaking"; *des Gegengig* is garbled. To be checked against the image.
