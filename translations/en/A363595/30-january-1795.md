Diary of the three Brothers Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn and Johann Christian Kühnel, at the post of Good Hope [1], from 1 to 31 January 1795.

<!-- lines 13180-13234 -->
Early on 1 January, already at 5 o'clock, the Hottentots came to wish us a happy New Year, and this went on until they had all been. At 10 o'clock was the meeting, which Brother Kühnel held. He spoke on Paul's words to the Ephesians, chapter 4, verses 22 to 25, and in a prayer implored our dear Lord for his blessing, not only today but in the whole year now begun, and that he would open the hearts that are still closed through his Holy Spirit, so that the word of life might find entrance, and so on. The meeting was very well attended. In the afternoon we prayed with our baptised and promised [2] God the Lord new obedience and faithfulness, and his peace could be powerfully felt. On the 2nd a Hottentot came from about 15 miles from here. He has long had a great wish to move here, but so much that is bad has been said to him about us that it is astonishing; among other things, that we taught people everything bad, for example stealing, murder and so on; that 40 Hottentots had once been hanged; and that after the New Year we would have to go away again. The farmer with whom he worked asked him whether he too wanted to go to the school. Answer: "Yes, Baas, I want to hear God's word too." The farmer told him that if he wanted to go there, he would shoot him. The Hottentot said: "I have been at the Cape and asked about the arrangements, but no one there knows anything about it. I had no rest; I had to come here myself, see it and hear it, and speak to my brothers and friends (he has a brother who is among the candidates for baptism). Now I have looked at the matter and heard it myself, spoken to my friends, and hear that it is all lies. I will now go back to the farmer and settle with him; then I ask that I may come again and live here too." And he thanked us for all that had been told him of Jesus' incarnation and death.

<!-- lines 13234-13261 -->
In our conference on the 3rd we implored our dear Lord on our knees for his help, counsel and support, and asked him to counsel us through his Holy Spirit and always to let us know his mind about what we are to do with this people, so that everything may happen according to his heart. We also asked him to keep us together in love, as in the past year, and promised him childlike obedience; and he let us feel his peace. Then we put one before him for baptism at the Festival of the Heathen, whom he approved. On the afternoon of the 4th a candidate for baptism sent for Brother Marsveld. She was very weak, so that he could talk little with her. Of her we are convinced that if she dies, the dear Saviour will take her to himself. Towards evening a son of young Mrs Lind came, from 3 miles from here. He stayed for supper; there was another man with him. They would not stay the night, but rode away again at 9 o'clock.

<!-- lines 13261-13319 -->
On the 5th we began the schools again, which were very full, for now they are back from the farmers. Since tomorrow is the Festival of the Heathen, we cancelled the schools and told them what a festival this was, and what an important day, especially for them. There was great emotion, especially among the children, when Brother Kühnel told them that the wise men from the East brought the dear Saviour gifts. If they too wanted to give him a gift that would please him, they should give him their hearts anew, wholly as his own, all of which he had bought with his precious blood. He added that those whose whole mind it was to renounce Satan and to live only for the Saviour in this world should stand up, kneel down with him, and ask the dear Saviour for mercy. Not a single one stayed sitting, but all fell on their knees with him, and most began to pray aloud; but it was not long before everything dissolved in tears, and the weeping grew so loud that the prayer had to be closed. Even the eyes of those of whom one has little hope did not stay dry. May the dear Saviour carry it on [3] with his mercy! Since we had thought and spoken much lately about what Baas Teunis announced here on 7 December last year [4], we finally resolved that Brother Marsveld should travel to the Cape to see how things stand with the government. Since we also cannot send our diaries and letters to the Cape now, for few farmers go to the Cape at present because they are busy threshing corn, and we would gladly give you news of us often, he was to take this with him, in the hope that a ship might soon sail for the fatherland. Father Schmidt, too, when he was with us, promised to put our cart, which is in bad condition, into good order if we would send it to the Cape. Since we also need various things from there, we resolved to send it along to bring back the necessities.

<!-- lines 13322-13369 -->
On the 6th, the Festival of the Heathen, we had a meeting early with our baptised, in which first a piece of the litany [5] was prayed, and then a couple of letters from the history of Greenland [6] were read. Soon after 8 o'clock was the baptism, which Brother Marsveld held; he spoke on Acts 8, verses 26 to 40. The baptised woman received the name Christina. The meeting was very large. At 2 o'clock in the afternoon we had another meeting with the baptised. We very much wished that we had accounts of other heathen stations translated into Dutch, so that we could tell them something of these too; but since we have nothing of the kind, we filled this meeting with stories of other heathen, and it became as it were a conversation, for they talked too. We also told them that today in the fatherland, in our congregations, they and all the heathen were remembered in prayer before the dear Saviour. They declared their thankfulness to the dear Saviour, who had provided for them so that they too now have teachers who have told them the way to salvation. Then this meeting was closed with a prayer on our knees. The Lord made this day a day of blessing for us and the Hottentots. In the afternoon our Brother Marsveld set off from here for the Cape, accompanied by our good wishes. Since Holy Communion falls in this week, it was not a matter of indifference to us to postpone it; but this time it could not be done otherwise, since if the journey did not happen now, it would have to be put off a long time again because of the moonlight. For now one cannot travel by day because of the great heat, since the oxen cannot stand it; and when there is no moonlight at night, it is dangerous.

<!-- lines 13369-13453 -->
On the 7th. Our sister Bedgen has long been badly treated by her husband, and has been beaten most days. She bore this with much patience. At her request we also spoke several times with her husband about it, and he promised to mend his ways, but that did not happen. Now, when she came home from the close of the year [7], he beat her without cause, to begin the new year (he was also in the meeting), and told her that she should go to us and tell us that he could not bear her any longer and wanted to be rid of her. This she now did, and said that she could not bear it with him any longer and would not go back to him. We put it to her to consider this well, for if she left this man she could not take another who was still a heathen, and if she did that, we could no longer own her as our sister. She said that she would remain without a husband. We had him come, and asked him what he had had his wife tell us. He said: "I cannot get on with her, and have quarrels with her every day, because I cannot bear her, and I would gladly be rid of her." We put his treatment of her to him, and said that if he could not bear her, it was better that he should leave her alone; such treatment could not go on, for she was no longer a heathen, and she too was tired of being with him. And so they were separated. They are a couple of very young people still; he is probably not yet 20 years old. They have had 2 children, but both have died. Today he came again and said that he regretted it and wanted her back. We told him that we could not force her to it. She also insisted that she would not go back to him, for he had often promised to do better, but it only grew worse. He came to her twice, for her to promise again to be his wife; but since she would not, he sent her word that she should come, or else he would fetch her at night. She was afraid, and came and told us. She has moved in with one of our baptised, and so there are only these 2 in a house which stands somewhat apart from the others. We had him called, spoke very earnestly with him, and put it to him that he himself had said that he could not bear her any longer and did not want her any more, and he should now stick to that; and if he did not leave her in peace, and even dared to fetch her at night, we would take the matter up, and then it would not end well for him. He said that he only wanted her to go with him to her father (her father is a slave of Baas Teunis, and is at the place he has from his son, an hour from here). We told him that if her father wanted to speak with her, he should come here; for we believe that if he got her away with him and was out in the field with her, he would give her a good share of blows and then go his way. What else should we do?

<!-- lines 13453-13500 -->
On the 8th several came who spoke with us about the distress of their hearts. One was quite inconsolable about the words that the people who do not turn to Jesus will cry out [8]: "Ye mountains, fall on us, and ye hills, cover us!" On the 9th the excluded candidate for baptism came and asked earnestly for forgiveness, but did not name her misdeeds. Lately, when we put her behaviour to her, she said that it was no more. We told her that she had grieved the dear Saviour very much and should ask him from her heart for forgiveness; and when she could say in truth that he had forgiven her, then we would forgive her too. On the 10th our sister Elisabeth came and said with many tears that she thanked the dear Saviour day and night that he had sent teachers here and given her the great grace of being baptised, and that she had the great grace of partaking of his body and blood in Holy Communion. This night she had become very uneasy that she had not yet thanked us for the great love that we had come over the great water to tell them the way to salvation; and our brothers in the fatherland too, who sent us to them. (Eva made the same declaration.) And she said: "Oh, how shall I thank the dear Saviour for it!" (Early on the 11th, already at 4 o'clock, young Mrs Grünewald [9] came with 4 of her daughters and one of her sons-in-law. She lives 3 hours from here. She is a well-to-do woman, but very mean. We have had to buy various things from her out of necessity, but she is much dearer in everything than other people. She has been with us once before, and then we did what we could to entertain her well; but this time it went off badly. In the afternoon they drove away again.)

<!-- lines 13501-13529 -->
On the 12th we picked [10] part of our beans, of which we have plenty this year. Here they have no other way of getting the beans out of the pods than to beat them out with horses; the farmers who have many tread them out with horses, like the corn. To beat them out with sticks takes much time; so we have made a couple of flails, and we thresh them out with these. It seemed something new to the Hottentots, and pleased them very much, because it goes quickly; but they have no great wish to thresh, because it makes the arms tired, and they prefer to leave such work to the horses, or take their time and beat the fruit out with sticks. It is really not easy here either, because one has no barns and must do it in the hot sun; that certainly costs many a drop of sweat. On the 13th we received a letter from Brother Marsveld, from which we saw that he was still well, but had achieved nothing with the Edle Heeren. Brother Marsveld also writes that he has heard that Neuwied [11] has been burned. At midday a farmer came who lives 16 days' journey up country. He stayed the night.) [12]

<!-- lines 13529-13566 -->
On the 14th Brother Jacob was out hunting at Baas Teunis's. He asked whether any of the Hottentots had moved away yet. Jacob said: "No!" He replied that he should tell the Hottentots to see to it that they got away; he would soon come here, and those who would not go he would take and bring to the Cape [13]. How we feel at such threats our dear brothers and sisters can easily imagine. He also said that 5 Hottentots had run away from the Landdrost at Swellendam who were supposed to be living here. (But that is not true; they do not live here.) And the Landdrost meant to complain about it to the Cape too. It is as if the devil were now quite loose, and had wholly possessed Baas Teunis and his wife. He has himself been to the Landdrost at Swellendam, and people say for certain that it was only on our account, to hatch something against us with him. As soon as he came back from there, he went off again the next day to the Cape, just on the 6th, when Brother Marsveld travelled there. Now he comes with such an order, and we wait longingly for Brother Marsveld, who is sure to bring something more definite. God must help, for all human help seems quite at an end. (Just think, dear brothers and sisters: such sad news from the fatherland, and here we are surrounded by nothing but enemies.)

<!-- lines 13567-13607 -->
On the 15th Baas Teunis again sent a message to the Hottentots that they should see to it that they got away, or else it would not go well. One can really [see them] thinking it over [14], since he comes on so sharply. On the 16th 15 men came and asked what they should do about the order to move away. We cannot advise them in these circumstances, and believe that it is best to keep quite still, because it is an order from the government; and when they come and ask for advice, to tell them that we can advise them nothing in this, but they must do as they think best. For if we wanted to advise anything, it might be said that we were stirring them up. At 10 o'clock in the evening our Brother Marsveld came home again, fairly well after all. He had got an ailment in his throat, so that he cannot swallow well; we hope it will soon pass. He also brought the Daily Texts for this year, and the 30 rixdollars maintenance money, and 2 books, the *Short Account of the Life of the Late Disciple* and the *Wholesome Doctrines* [15], and also a packet of weekly [congregation] news [16]. (The [...] [17] of which Brother Schmidt writes Domine Miller did not hand over, but took with him to Batavia. It cost much trouble to get these books; if the officers who lodged with Brother Schönberg had not helped to get hold of them, they too would have gone to Batavia.) Brother Marsveld writes as follows of his journey and his business:

<!-- lines 13608-13680 -->
"I set out for Cape Town on 6 January at 4 o'clock in the afternoon, with the best wishes of my dear brothers, and rode through the night to the Bot River [18], where I arrived at 1 o'clock. I unsaddled my horse and let it graze, and lay down to sleep on the bare earth in the open air. But when day began to break, I was quite stiff and cold from the dew and the night air. I now took my gun to hunt something; but as soon as I was up a small hill, I saw a chaise with 6 horses coming in the distance. I wanted to see who it was, and when I came down the hill again I saw that it was the Frenchman [19] mentioned in the last diary, who has come here because of the unrest of the war. His name is Zinkhank. He was very glad to meet me here, and when he saw that I was quite stiff with cold, he asked whether I would not take something. I said yes, whereupon he gave me 2 thimblefuls to drink, which warmed me greatly. He asked me to sit with him, but I thanked him and declined. At his request, however, I promised him to be at the farm Eckstein at 12 o'clock and to dine with him there. I looked for my horse, and the Hottentots for their oxen, and at 11 o'clock we arrived at that place, where we stayed until 4 o'clock, and then travelled on together towards the Cape. Since with my horse I got over the heights faster than he did with his wagon, I rode ahead and waited for him at Hottentots Holland Kloof. Here I got into the wagon with him, and we drove to Mr [20] Morgel, with whom we spent the night. On the morning of the 8th we continued our journey to the Cape. As we came near the town I experienced God's protection: several ox wagons met us, at which the horses took fright, and the leaders reared and sprang in among the oxen. I jumped out of the wagon in good time, and Mr Zinkhank soon found himself obliged to do the same; but everything passed off without harm. He had asked me several times to dine with him, and at last said that I need only let him know when I would come. I drove with him to his lodging, which was very fine, in the house of the Imperial Consul, who welcomed me very kindly. Mr Zinkhank at once told him of our Hottentots, that there was a great difference between them and those who lived further up country. At dusk I came to our old friend Martinus Schmidt, who with his whole family received me very lovingly and lodged me with them. He was very thankful for the greetings that I gave him by word of mouth [21], and resolved to write to Brother Reichel, which he did afterwards.

<!-- lines 13680-13732 -->
"On the 9th I went first to the Gezaghebber, Mr Rhenius, and told him that the order had come from the Commissioner, Mr Sluysken, that all Hottentots who had come to us with their cattle should take them back to their former dwelling places. I put it to him that the people could not subsist without cattle, and that several of them would then have to fetch their cattle for slaughter from as much as 3 days' journey away; and so I wanted to ask his good advice in this. He answered that I should go to the Commissioner himself, and apply to him privately [for the withdrawal] of that order [22], so that the Hottentots might stay with their cattle in our area, where there was no lack of room for them. I thanked him for this good advice. Mr Berg, the cashier, was also present, and said: 'This is simply the doing of Baas Teunis at the post.' So I went to the Commissioner, Mr Sluysken, who was quite friendly. I put my request modestly, but in vain. He said that he was overrun with complaints from the farmers that if the Hottentots were taught, they would get behind their secrets. I asked him politely to allow me to say what I thought about it, and not to take it amiss; to which he answered: 'You may speak quite freely.' So I said: 'What secrets have the farmers, other than ploughing, sowing and reaping? And those they have to learn from the Hottentots; otherwise there are no secrets.' I went on: 'If the farmers thought that the Hottentots might read their letters, they need only do as Your Honour and we do, and seal them; we too seal our letters, and then there is no danger.' His answer was: 'That is all very well, my dear man, but because of the war I cannot change it.' So I had to go away without having achieved anything; but I was to come to him once more before I left. After that I visited some good friends, such as Commandant Gordon and the chief of police, Mr Lesueur [23], who were very friendly.

<!-- lines 13732-13806 -->
"On the 10th I dined with Commandant Gordon, and we talked much of the fatherland and its present circumstances. Today I also heard the sad news that Neuwied had burned down entirely and that everyone had had to flee, which affected me greatly. I could not forget this dear congregation of ours, and it pressed many sighs from my heart to the dear Saviour. I visited the 3 Reformed ministers, who were friendlier than before, and also the Lutheran minister Kolver [24], who invited me to a meal, but I had to decline. On the 11th I went to the Reformed church, where the sermon was on Acts 9, verses 10 to 12. In the afternoon I wrote a little letter to Brother Rothe at Zeist. On the 12th I went to the Commissioner again, to take leave and to try once more whether I could achieve anything towards the withdrawal of the said order, so that the Hottentots could then keep their cattle at Baviaanskloof. But all was in vain. So I took a friendly leave and commended us to his protection, and he promised to look to our best. Then I went to the Gezaghebber, Mr Rhenius, who asked me how I had fared with the Commissioner. When I answered that everything must stay as it was, he shook his head and said that he too would have to answer for it; for his part he could do nothing in it now, as he had helped us as much as he possibly could before, when he still had that charge. I assured him that we were fully convinced of it, whereupon he added: 'Be assured that if I should be able to do anything for you in it, I shall not fail to do it'; and he wished us God's blessing on our work among the Hottentots. Mr Gordon, to whom I went next, asked me whether we had yet made a beginning at forging knives. Answer: 'We have no workshop for it yet.' Question: 'How is that?' Answer: 'The Commissioner, Mr Sluysken, has forbidden us to build anything at all, even a goat shed.' Mr Gordon was much surprised at this, and promised to speak with the Commissioner about the matter. The 13th I spent in farewell visits. On the 14th I left the Cape, quite overwhelmed [25] by the kindness I had enjoyed with our old friend Martin Schmidt. He treats us as if we were his children. On the 15th I arrived at Mr Morgel's, where I took some refreshment and then travelled on. But I had to leave my horse behind there, because it had put out its hip and so could not well make the journey. On the evening of the 16th I arrived at Baviaanskloof, full of praise and thanks to our dear Saviour, with my dear brothers, whom I found well and cheerful."

<!-- lines 13807-13857 -->
Now the diary continues: On the 17th, in our conference, the dear Saviour approved one as a candidate for baptism. Since we had postponed Holy Communion until Brother Marsveld came back from the Cape, we talked today about whether we should hold it now. We resolved to let it lapse altogether this time, because we and our fellow partakers were far too distracted in the present circumstances. We heard again today that a Hottentot had been at Baas Teunis's, [who said] that he was extraordinarily angry, and had railed violently against Baviaanskloof and uttered shameful threats against this place. Early on the 18th a farmer came (an inhabitant of [...] [26]) who [had a place] close to the [Kaffers], but was driven away by them, and his place was set on fire. He was looking here for Hottentots who had run away from him; but they were not here. In the afternoon he rode away again. On the 19th many Hottentots went into a forest 7 hours from here to dry peaches, which are there in great quantity and free for everyone. Farmers drive there and fetch whole wagonloads. The Hottentots stay there for a time so as to dry them at once, and if they are diligent they can dry a great deal, for there is no lack of peaches. The peach trees grow wherever a stone falls. On the 20th the Hottentots came and said that tomorrow they meant to go to the Cape to the government, to see whether they could not obtain leave to stay here. We told them that they should do as they thought best. Another Hottentot also came who had served a farmer about 10 miles from here. The farmer had given him a certificate that he had served his time. Baas Teunis had told him that he must leave his cattle where he had been.

<!-- lines 13857-13926 -->
On the 21st, at daybreak, the Hottentots set out for the Cape, and among them 2 of the candidates for baptism. Today we also had the speaking [27] of the candidates for baptism. On several points we could rejoice, but taken as a whole we wished for more life in their hearts. In these days we have had to exclude a man from the candidates for baptism. When he had been at a farmer's, he got drunk on the way. He has asked again to come to the candidates' meeting, but it seems as if he were quite dead, so we did not have the confidence to take him back for this time. At 10 o'clock Baas Teunis came, with another man. Today he came as a sergeant, in his uniform, with his sabre at his side; he has never shown himself here like that before. He was very friendly and proper, and we too treated him as friendly as we could. After a while he said that he came by order of the Governor to announce the often-mentioned order to the Hottentots, and to talk with us about how we thought things might stand in future with regard to the Hottentots. Then he gave us the letter to read in which he is authorised to carry out the matter: with the help of the field corporals he was to arrest those who did not obey the order and bring them to the Cape, where they would be punished as disobedient; but he must give us notice of it. It also said that he was to help us in everything where he could. [But it did not say that] he was to talk with us about what we [thought] of the future [28]. So we regarded this as a temptation: he wanted to hear what we thought of the authorities, and perhaps also whether we meant to resist the order of the authorities; for he is an all too wicked man.) We told him that we did not concern ourselves at all with these matters, because we did not understand them; nor could we say whether this place was too small for the cattle, because we did not understand the rivers [29]; this much we could say, that so far not a single head of cattle had died of hunger. He answered that that would still happen in the rainy season. Since the Hottentots were not at home [30], he could not announce the order, and said: "When they come back, I shall no doubt learn what they have achieved; I believe it will stay as it is." In the afternoon he rode home again.

<!-- lines 13926-13983 -->
On the 22nd several Hottentots raised an alarm in our garden [31]. When they were asked what it was, they said a boomslang [32], which was lying right on top of a gatepost. This snake cannot be killed without danger except by shooting. Brother Kühnel shot it. We have shot several already. A couple of days ago a farmer 3 miles from here was bitten by one of these in his garden in the evening, and by the morning he was dead. The dear Saviour has kept us so far. On the 23rd our sister Anna came and asked whether it was true: she had heard today in several places that a brother of ours was among the Kaffers and taught them just as we do, and proclaimed the Gospel to them, and that they were learning well. But the brother was said to be quite blind, and not from the fatherland but from another country. She was told that she should not believe it, for if it were true we would know something of it too [33]. On the 24th a Hottentot came who had served a farmer but had run away, and wanted to stay here a month. We told him that he might not, but should see to it that he got away from here. On the 25th young Mrs Mary came with one of her sons. On the 26th the Hottentot who was here on the 24th and wanted to stay a month came and said that he had thought better of it: he wanted to stay here longer, and also hear God's word, send his children to school and let them learn. We told him that we had not come here as schoolmasters; he should just go where he had said. But since he would not be turned away, we told him that he must have a certificate from the farmer he had served that his time was up. This man seemed strange to us. On the 27th a young woman was buried. She is now the third to die of the sickness that went about last year and is now beginning again. On the 28th many Hottentots went again to pick peaches.

<!-- lines 13983-14071 -->
Towards evening on the 29th the Hottentots who went to the Cape on the 21st, 16 in number, came home very sad. They were twice with the Edle Heer. The first time he said that they must go away again, and the second time that it remained as he had said and as Baas Teunis had it in writing, namely that they should take their cattle back where they had been, because it took the pasture from the cattle of the neighbouring farmers, so that these could not subsist; and [he asked] where they would live when they had finished learning [34]. The Hottentots answered him that they meant to stay there and not move away again. The Hottentots have more insight into our cause than the Christians; for these all say that when they have been taught and baptised they must move back to their places, and others come in their stead, and so it is to go on. The Hottentots say that they will not leave here; they want to stay where God's word is proclaimed, and not go back into the wilderness. So say those who are not yet more closely joined to us. Of the baptised, several have said that they will not let go of the word of our dear Lord, and will not leave us, however it goes and wherever it leads; for if they did that, it would be as if they denied our dear Lord [35]. These Hottentots were also with the Fiscal, named Reinveld [36]. He said that he would gladly arrange that they might keep their cattle there, but the Edle Heer insisted too firmly that they should take them away. Then they also spoke with the Hottentot captain [37] named Stoffel, who is on guard at the Cape. He said that Baas Teunis and he had brought the matter so far to [the] ears [of the government] that all the cattle must go. Baas Teunis gave him plenty to drink, and 2 shillings besides, so that he would confirm to the Edle Heer that the cattle of the farmers round about were quite crowded out by those of the Hottentots, and the farmers could not subsist; which this captain did, as he says himself, because he got plenty to drink and 2 shillings besides. This Hottentot, the captain of the Hottentots, lives on the place that Baas Teunis's son had [38], an hour from here. The other Hottentots complain much about this captain, that he too is to blame that Baas Teunis's son got this place. Had he sought the good of the Hottentots, that would not have happened; but he consented over a glass of wine, because he likes to drink. That the Hottentots' cattle take the farmers' pasture is not true; for the Hottentots' cattle go on no farmer's land except Baas Teunis's place, which he has from his son, from which he was not allowed to drive the Hottentots' cattle. Now, since the old man has had the place, they may not go there any more either. All that is said about the Hottentots is lies.

<!-- lines 14071-14134 -->
On the 30th we spoke with one of our candidates for baptism who had been at the Cape too. He told what has already been reported, and said further that they had wanted to speak with the Edle Heer about our dear Father, but he had ordered them to hold their tongues, and told them to see to it that they got away, or else he would have them soundly beaten. The Fiscal Reinveld asked them whether they believed in God. Answer: "Yes, and in Jesus Christ." Question: did they know, then, where Jesus Christ came from? Answer: "He was born of the Virgin Mary." At this the Fiscal said: "That is true; you should believe in him, for there is no salvation to be found except in Jesus Christ." On the 31st. Now that our old [39] Hottentots have come back from the Cape and told how things look, the others have become quite dismayed and very sad, and [cannot] say [40] what they are to do, since they do not want to leave God's word. We can advise them nothing but to obey the authorities. But how we feel about it, dear brothers and sisters, no one can believe. Against people who are anxious about their salvation, who would gladly hear of their Creator and Redeemer, other people are bought with force and wine to bear false witness before the authorities! To know this, and to have to keep silent about it besides, if one does not want to make the fire ten times worse, is no easy thing. Yet God still sits in the government, and has taken the power from the devil. He will know how to carry out his work here too, so that in the end it turns out to his glory, although we do not yet see how. Perhaps he means [to sift] these people who come here [41] in this way, since there are certainly many among them who do not know why they came; but that many who truly mean it sincerely from their hearts must suffer under it, this hurts painfully. Our prayer to the dear Saviour is that in these circumstances he will keep us with himself [42] and his wounds, and give us the insight we need into how we are to conduct ourselves; and for this we commend ourselves to the prayers of all our dear brothers and sisters in the congregations before our dear Lord. Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn, Johann Christian Kühnel.

Several of our sisters asked whether we would not soon write to our brothers and sisters in the fatherland, and asked us to write a few words from them, which follow here as they dictated them to us [43].

<!-- lines 14141-14162 -->
Sara: "I cannot thank our dear Saviour enough, and the dear brothers and sisters, that they have sent us teachers, so that I now know what is good and what is not good; that brothers have come to us, to so poor a people, though we can give them nothing at all, to make us acquainted with the dear Saviour; that I now know and feel that the dear Saviour has redeemed me too. A thousand thanks be to him for it! Yet I cannot thank him enough that we have teachers, or else we would not have come to know the dear Saviour. My prayer is that I may want nothing with which he is grieved; I will live for him until my end. I greet all the brothers and sisters a thousand times, and thank them."

<!-- lines 14163-14188 -->
Elisabeth: "I thank my dear Saviour that I now have such thoughts as I never had before, and feel something in my heart that I have not felt before. Before, I had nothing but bad thoughts; now I have thoughts of our dear Saviour, and feel the guilt of sin in my heart. Yet I can never thank him enough for it. I thank him day and night when I wake, and ask him to keep these thoughts in me and always to give me a thankful heart, until I come to him. Yet I cannot thank him, my dear Saviour, enough that we have teachers! And you, dear brothers and sisters, I thank from my heart that you maintain them; for we can give them nothing. For long years I have wished [44] to see some of the sisters who are in the fatherland; if it does not happen here, I shall get to see them with the dear Saviour, for I am old. I greet you a thousand times."

<!-- lines 14189-14222 -->
Martha: "I, who am not worthy to call you brothers and sisters, cannot give thanks enough that I have now heard the word of our dear Lord and hear it daily, of which I knew nothing and heard nothing in my life; for that too I cannot thank the dear Saviour enough. Oh, in eternity I cannot thank him enough! And you, dear brothers and sisters, we cannot thank enough that you have sent us teachers who proclaim to us the torment and death of Jesus. For if they had not come, my poor soul would have had to be lost for ever. And that you support them, for we are an all too poor people; we can give them nothing at all. Yet I cannot thank the dear Saviour enough that I have often partaken of Holy Communion already, a great grace of which I am not worthy at all. My prayer is that he will keep me with himself until my end, and take from me everything with which I grieve him, so that I may be a true child of God. I also long with heart and soul to have the good fortune of seeing sisters here; I long for that very much. I greet all the brothers and sisters, I who am the poorest and most unworthy. Martha."

<!-- lines 14223-14243 -->
Anna Maria: "I would gladly have something written to you, my dear brothers and sisters in the fatherland, but I cannot express in the Dutch language what my heart wishes. But I cannot thank our dear Lord enough that he has brought us teachers here, so that I too now know that I have a Saviour, and now feel in my heart what I did not feel before; for I have committed many sins, and knew nothing of our dear Father. Yet day and night I thank him for his great mercy! And I thank you a thousand times that you have sent us teachers, or else I would still know nothing of our dear Father and Saviour. I greet you a thousand times."

<!-- lines 14244-14259 -->
Eva: "Many, yes many greetings to you all, dear brothers and sisters! I cannot thank the dear Saviour enough for what I now feel, and did not feel before, in my heart: his peace and his love [45], so that nothing would be dearer to me now than to go to him. A thousand thanks to him for it! And you I cannot thank enough that you have sent us teachers and also maintain them with us. Oh, I thank you a thousand times for it!"

Bedgen: "My mind is to live wholly for the dear Saviour in this world, and I ask him to make me a true child of God. It is my daily prayer also to be obedient to my teachers. ## Notes

1. On *auf der Poſt der guten Hoffnung*, see entry 28, note 1.
2. The transcription reads *hatten wir mit unſeren Getauften das Ge⸗beten, und ermahnten Gott, dem HErrn neuen Gehorſam und Treue*; *ermahnten*, "admonished", must be a misreading, probably of *verhießen* or *gelobten*, "promised".
3. The transcription reads *Der liebe Heiland ſehe es fort mit Seinem Erbarmen*; probably *ſetze es fort*, "carry it on".
4. This date confirms that the visit of Baas Teunis and the announcement fell on Sunday 7 December; see entry 29, note 8.
5. The transcription reads *ein Stük aus der Loſung*, "a piece from the Daily Text"; the verb *gebetet*, "prayed", suggests *Litaney*, the litany. To be checked against the image.
6. *die Grönländiſche Hiſtorie*, probably David Cranz's *Historie von Grönland* (1765) with its continuation, which includes letters from the Moravian mission in Greenland.
7. The transcription reads *vom Herzensſchluß*, which makes no sense here; probably *vom Jahresſchluß*, the meeting at the close of the year on the night of 31 December.
8. The transcription reads *darauf auswerfen werden*; probably *ausrufen*, "cry out". The words are from Luke 23:30 and Revelation 6:16 (after Hosea 10:8).
9. *die junge Frau Grünewaldin*, probably of the family of the farmer Stoffel Grunewald (see `glossary.csv`).
10. The transcription reads *pflügten wir ... ab*, "ploughed off"; probably *pflückten*, "picked".
11. The transcription reads *Heuvied*; the account below shows that Neuwied is meant, the Moravian settlement on the Rhine. Neuwied lay in the war zone on the Rhine in 1794 and 1795; whether the report that it had burned down was true is not settled here. To be checked.
12. The closing parenthesis here has no opening one in the transcription.
13. The transcription reads *und diejenigen, ſo nicht wollten gehen, ſie jagen, wollte er nehmen, und nach der Caſe bringen*; *ſie jagen* does not fit the sentence.
14. The transcription reads *Man könne ſie wirklich in Überlegung nihmt*, which is garbled; the sense seems to be that the Khoekhoe of the mission were beginning to consider leaving.
15. The transcription reads *die Kurzegefaßte Lebensbeſchreibung des ſelgn Jüngers, und die Heilſamen Lehren*. The "Disciple" (*der Jünger*) was the Moravian name for Count Zinzendorf, who died in 1760; the first book is probably a short life of him. The second is not identified.
16. The transcription reads *einen Paquet Wöchentliche und Gewiſſens⸗Nachrichten*; probably *Wöchentliche Gemein⸗Nachrichten*, the congregation news (see `glossary.csv`).
17. The transcription reads *die Piſtolan*, "the pistols"; perhaps *Epiſteln* or another word for books or letters. To be checked against the image.
18. The Bot River; see entry 27, note 8.
19. The gentleman from Flanders who visited on 21 December 1794 (entry 29), who spoke French, English and Portuguese. His name, *Zinkhank*, is not otherwise known.
20. In Marsveld's account the transcription has *Br.*, "Brother", before the names of Morgel, Zinkhank, the cashier Berg, the Commissioner, Commandant Gordon and the chief of police; in each case it is probably a misreading of *Hr.*, "Mr", and the translation gives "Mr" or the title. None of these men was a Moravian.
21. The transcription reads *Für die Grüße, die ich ihm von der Weinſtöck, Wolke, ſten, unſerer mündlich ausrichtete*; the words between *von* and *unſerer* are garbled, perhaps names. To be checked against the image.
22. The transcription reads *und in Privatſachen jenes Befehls anſuchen ſollte*; a word such as *Zurücknahme*, "withdrawal", which appears on the 12th, seems to be missing.
23. *Polizeymſtr Leſieur*, the chief of police; the Mr Lesueur of the government in October 1794 (entry 27, note 20).
24. The Lutheran church in Cape Town had its own minister from 1780; the name *Colver* is probably a misreading, perhaps of Kolver. To be checked.
25. The transcription reads *ganz beſchenkt*, "quite presented"; probably *beſchämt*, "overwhelmed" or "put to shame".
26. The transcription reads *(ein Inwohner von Chelands,) welcher dichte bey der Röffenüwehr hat*; both words are garbled. The context (driven away and his place burned) suggests the eastern frontier and the Xhosa. The Dutch diary has "nearest of all to the Kaffers" (A363349 entry 19), which the words in square brackets follow. To be checked against the image.
27. The transcription reads *das Weichen der Tauf⸗Candidaten*; probably *das Sprechen*, the speaking.
28. This passage is garbled in the transcription: *Zu dieſer Anwendung ſtund aber nicht bloß er mit uns ſprechen ſollte, was wir von ihm künftigen verſtehen dörften*. The translation gives the probable sense. The closing parenthesis after *Mann* has no opening one.
29. *die Revieren*: the rivers, here in the sense of the grazing along them.
30. The transcription reads *Da die Hottentotten nun nichts zu Berge waren*; probably *nicht zu Hauſe*, "not at home", since many had gone to the Cape.
31. The transcription reads *geſchah den verſchiedenen Hottentotten in unſerm Garten Lerm, mit einem, ſungen ſie fremde und ſchrieen*; the sense is that they raised an alarm and shouted.
32. The transcription reads *eine Wam⸗Schlange ... auf einem Pfordt*; probably *Boom⸗Schlange*, the boomslang, a venomous tree snake, and *Pfoſt* or *Pforte*, a post or gate.
33. No missionary is known to have been working among the Xhosa in January 1795; the London Missionary Society's J.T. van der Kemp began that work in 1799. The rumour is not identified.
34. The transcription reads *und wo ſie dann nähren, wann ſie ausgelernt hätten, ſie wollten wenn die Bauern ihren Platz wäggenommen hätten*, which is garbled. The sense seems to be a question where they would live when they had finished learning, if the farmers had taken their places.
35. The transcription follows with *Das Leid in dieſer Zeit wurde doch nicht lange*, which is not clear and is left out.
36. The Fiscal Willem Stephanus van Ryneveld (see `glossary.csv`, *Fiſcaal Reinveld* in A363596).
37. *Hottentotten⸗Capitain*: a leader of a Khoekhoe community, recognised by the colonial government (see `glossary.csv`). Captain Stoffel is not otherwise identified here.
38. The transcription adds *(den er bey der Delta hat*, which is not clear.
39. The transcription reads *unſre alte Hottentotten*; perhaps *unſre andern*.
40. The transcription reads *können nun ſagen*; the sense requires *können nicht ſagen*.
41. The transcription reads *an Dich ziehen*, "come to thee"; probably *hieher ziehen*, "move here". The transcription also has *da gewiß viele da ſind, die nicht wiſſen, warum?*, and the sense of "sift" is supplied.
42. The transcription reads *bey Dich und Seinem Wunden*; probably *bey Sich*, "with himself".
43. The sisters presumably spoke in Dutch, or the Dutch of the Cape, and the brothers wrote their words down in German; Anna Maria says that she cannot express herself in Dutch. Sara, Elisabeth, Martha, Anna Maria, Eva and Bedgen are all in `glossary.csv`. Martha dictated another letter in May 1795 (A363596, entry 04).
44. The transcription reads *Ich war, lange Jahr, von den Schweſtern, die im Vaterlande waren, einige zu ſehen, da ich geſchieht es hier nicht*; probably *Ich wünſchte lange Jahr ... zu ſehen; geſchieht es hier nicht*.
45. The transcription reads *Sei nun Friede und Seine Liebe*; probably *Seinen Frieden und Seine Liebe*.
