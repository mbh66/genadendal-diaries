Diary of the three Brothers Marsveld, Schwinn and Kühnel at the Cape, from 1 to 28 February 1795.

<!-- lines 14277-14328 -->
Early on 1 February the farmer Harme came with his wife, who is a Hottentot woman; they live together as man and wife. There were 2 more Christians with them. They came to attend the meeting, in which Brother Marsveld spoke on the Gospel. In the afternoon they rode away again. On the 2nd one of the candidates for baptism came and said that he had sold a cow and 2 calves; now he still had 3 sheep, and if he might not keep them here, he would sell them too; for he could not move away and leave God's word. Baas Teunis has again sent word that they should see to it that they got away. Two have made a beginning and taken their cattle away. It is only so bad that they did not live together [before]; otherwise a couple could go there with the cattle to look after them; but as it is, one family lived here and another there. Baas Teunis says that if they want to come to the meeting on Sundays they can, only it will be a little further for them; the Christians have it so [1], who have 3 days' journey on horseback and much further still to a church. That is true; but if one inquires how often they go to a church, it is very often [only] the first time when they are baptised, and then when they have their own children baptised; and many let more than one child come together before they have them baptised, so that it is worth the trouble to go to church. There are many Hottentots here who have 30 miles and more to the places where they were before. The farmers grudge the Hottentots having God's word so near. The moving away goes very slowly, because, as they say, they will not leave God's word. So we must wait and see how it will go.

<!-- lines 14328-14364 -->
On the 3rd and [on other days] of this week [2] several came to speak with us about the salvation of their hearts, and declared their great longing to be saved. On the 4th was the speaking of the baptised, and we could rejoice over most of them. Some were very distracted in their thoughts, partly because of the matter of moving away, partly because of bad treatment by their husbands. In the communicants' meeting the 3 confirmands, Jacob, Eva and Rahel, were blessed for it with the laying on of hands, after they had first declared their mind to us once more, and then in the meeting, to give body and soul to Jesus Christ and to want nothing in the world but what their Redeemer and Saviour wants; and a real feeling of the presence of God prevailed. Then [several] [3] came one after another and lamented with many tears their lost state, and asked whether there was still grace with Jesus Christ for them too; for they had done all the sins that a person could do, and were not worthy to walk on the earth. They were directed to the dear Saviour, and told that they should fall at his feet, for he casts out none who knows himself from the heart to be a sinner and asks for grace at his feet.

<!-- lines 14364-14417 -->
On the 6th a woman came, wept bitterly, and said that she must now move away again with her 3 children, to the Bok River [4] (2 hours from here), where she lived before, to take her sheep back there and herd them there. The children have all gone to school. She said that she had had no wish to move here, but Baas Teunis gave her no peace (she lived 2 hours from here) and always told her that she could see now that everyone was moving to the school; did she not want to be converted too? Did she want to be left alone with her children? Because he gave her no peace, she finally decided to move here too. Now she has heard and found something different here from what she had thought, and was very glad that she was here; and the dear Saviour has certainly not left himself without witness in her heart. And now Baas Teunis has told her, and sent her word, that she may not stay here with her sheep but must go back where she was. She thought that this would pass over again, because he had given her no peace there until she moved here, and she did not want to obey this order of his at once. Now she said: "It is as if the devil had gone into him entirely, for he is like a madman, and I must leave here now. Since I cannot manage on my own, I must take my children with me; but as soon as I can, I will send some of my children back here." (On the 7th, in the conference, the dear Saviour assigned us our sister Susanna as a candidate for Communion, and one as a candidate for baptism.) In the evening we partook of Holy Communion, with the dear Saviour owning us poor ones. We had 3 truly blessed fellow partakers, after we had asked them once more about their mind beforehand, and they had declared once more that it was their whole mind to want nothing in this world but to live for the dear Saviour.

<!-- lines 14417-14464 -->
On Sunday the 8th Brother Marsveld spoke on the Gospel. Our Susanna also came and said that she had no rest; she had to come and declare her thankfulness to us for the grace she had received yesterday. She said: "For joy and thankfulness I do not know what to do; oh, I cannot thank the dear Saviour enough! For some time I have felt especially well, and in this happiness I could do nothing but weep and give thanks, and so it is today too." She is a poor widow; her husband was on guard at the Cape, and fell from a rock there and was killed. She has 2 children. On the 9th. For some time there has been very great heat, so that it was almost unbearable for the Hottentots; we could hardly bear it by day or by night [5]. Yesterday it began to rain a little and grew a little cool, which refreshed us greatly; but today it is already beginning to grow hot again. On the 10th the candidate for baptism who was lately excluded came, and asked with many tears that we would forgive her bad behaviour, for she saw herself as if she were shut out of the kingdom of heaven too. She said: "I ask the dear Saviour day and night for forgiveness." She has often been with us already and asked for forgiveness. The report that others give of her conduct is good. We forgave her, for which she was very thankful. The excluded man also came, and asked to be allowed to come to the little meeting again; but his conduct is such that we could not allow it.

<!-- lines 14460-14509 -->
On the 11th a servant of Mr Clüde [6], to whom Constantia belongs, came; he lives on one of his places, 4 days' journey further up country. He was looking for a Hottentot who had run away from him with his wife and 4 children, but had not come here. This man of the gentleman's is a German, but has already been 18 years here in the country. On the 12th, when we were having our house plastered where the rain had washed it off, the Hottentots were digging clay not far from our house and found a nest with 11 snake's eggs. When we opened them, there were already young snakes inside, 8 inches long, which were alive. The eggs were white like birds' eggs, and one could have taken them for such, except that the shells were not so hard. The nest was half an ell deep in the earth, in a mouse hole. On the 13th. Another Hottentot had taken his cattle away from here to the Bok River; when he came there, Dimeni's servant [7] chased him away again with his cattle. The Hottentot went to Baas Teunis, who sent him back there and said that he must put up with him. This Dimeni got that place (which always belonged to the Hottentots) only last year [8], on condition that he would not drive the Hottentots away. He is still building his house and does not properly live there yet; and already he is chasing the poor people away. That is how it goes with the poor Hottentots! On the 14th a sick man sent for Brother Kühnel, and asked him to tell him a few words about God, because he had not been able to come to the meeting to hear something of our dear Lord there. This man was brought here sick; he is the one of whom we have already said that he believes he is possessed [9]. Baas Teunis also sent Hanna word that she must take her cattle away.

<!-- lines 14509-14576 -->
On the 15th, the beginning of the Passion season, Brother Marsveld spoke in the meeting on the Gospel, Luke 18, verse 31 to the end. On the 16th most of the Hottentots who had gone to drive plough oxen came home again. They were not able to drive much, because it has often rained in this time, and many [of the oxen were lost] to them [10]. On the 17th our Sara, who had gone to a farmer's yesterday, came home full of sorrow and fear. The farmer had told her that the Landdrost of Stellenbosch would come to us in these days and bring the written order from the Edle Heer that we must leave our place within 3 days and go so far away that no Christian lives there; and if we did not obey, we were to be sent to Batavia. We had much comforting to do with her and several others. They said: "If only our teachers could stay in peace, they may do with us what they like; we are not going away." We had much comforting to do, and to direct them to our dear Saviour, without whose will nothing can happen; and if he allows something that seems hard to us, he also gives us strength to bear it if we ask him for it, and in the end we shall still give him praise and thanks for everything. We have much comforting to do, but often need comfort ourselves, since the rumours and prospects grow ever more sad; and if it pleased God to allow the enemies of thy kingdom [11], all the Hottentots, and we with them, would have to leave here. Today too Baas Teunis sent word to the Hottentots, and to us as well, that they should see to it that they got away; he would not like to do them the disgrace [12] of having them taken away by the field corporals. He also sent word besides that they might not keep any cattle for slaughter here, but must take everything away; they should slaughter it where it grazed and bring the meat here. Just think, dear brothers and sisters, what a demand that is, when many live 30 miles and more from here: they are to slaughter there and bring the meat here; and when one slaughters anything, it begins to stink by the next day; and the Hottentots cannot salt any, for many have no salt for their food, and a cask for it is not to be thought of. If only we could speak honestly and openly with someone [13]!

<!-- lines 14576-14620 -->
Early on the 18th, at 9 o'clock, we saw a wagon with 8 horses, and 2 men on horseback with it, coming towards us across the Sonderend River. We must say that when we saw this we did not feel easy, for we saw that it was no farmer. When they came to our river, the wagon stopped, those on horseback dismounted, and a gentleman got out of the wagon, and they came towards us on foot. Then we thought: that shows no friendship. Afterwards they said that this was done because the sickness was still among the Hottentots, so that the slaves who were with the wagon might not be infected. When they came nearer, we went out to meet them and welcome them. They seemed very friendly at once, and one gentleman asked whether we still knew him. Answer: "Yes, you are Mr [14] Clüde" (namely the Mr Clüde to whom Constantia belongs, where the precious wine grows). He had sent for us when we arrived at the Cape, on the recommendation of Mr Schwellengräver [15] in Holland, who lives at Doorn, 2 hours from Zeist. He said yes. The other was a gentleman from Stellenbosch named Hoffman, who has been a privy councillor [16]. The third was Andreas Otto, a field corporal; he lives about 3 miles from here. Mr Clüde now lives at Stellenbosch too. Mr Clüde brought us a greeting from Mr Schwellengräver. He said that the latter had complained to him that we had not written to him at all, and had put several questions to him which he was to answer for him. So he asked us to tell him: 1) How many Hottentots had we found here when we came? 2) How many had moved here? 3) How many had died of this sickness? 4) How many went to school?

<!-- lines 14621-14712 -->
On all these points we could hardly answer precisely; the rest, though, all the more precisely: 5) How many oxen, cows, sheep, goats [17] and how many horses did we have? Answer: "We have 3 oxen, 3 cows, 2 calves, 100 goats, 1 horse, 2 sheep." It made him laugh that we had so little. Then he said: "I have heard that the Hottentots are to take their cattle away from here; what is the reason for it?" We told him that we knew nothing except that Baas Teunis said that the place was too small and the cattle must die of hunger. He said: "That is not true. In this field alone, which runs from your house to the Sonderend River, 1000 sheep could live, without the other land. The Edle Heer has not been told the truth." Otto also said that he could not imagine what kind of Christians these were who kept these people from God's word and wanted to drive them away; it was to the harm of all the burghers if the Hottentots had to go; now one could still get people when one had work, but if they were gone, where should one get any? For all this nothing was to blame but Baas Teunis's [spite] [18]. Most of the poor Hottentots gathered in front of our house, in the hope that these gentlemen might perhaps be able and willing to help them; for Mr Hoffman had spoken with the Hottentots who were at the Cape to petition the Edle Heer, and had said that he would come himself and look at the place and the cattle. He went out in front of the house and talked with the Hottentots, and they told him that Baas Teunis had impounded some of their cattle, and they had had to pay a shilling for each head. Mr Hoffman told Brother Kühnel, and both [gentlemen] were very little pleased by it, and said they would like to know the names of them all. We had them come in one after another, and Mr Clüde wrote down all their names. Over 50 came whose cattle had been impounded. At 10 o'clock, after a friendly leave-taking, they drove and rode from us to Baas Teunis's place, where a Hottentot had to take them. There they looked at everything carefully and asked about everything, because this place belongs to the Hottentots, from which they have now been driven entirely. In front of our house Mr Hoffman asked the Hottentots why they had moved here. Answer: to hear and learn God's word. At this he said: "That is good, if you do this. You must not think that these people are put here by the government or by other men to teach you; they are not from men, but sent by God himself, and you must follow everything they tell you; and if you do that, you will become happy people." They said this also to those who met them on the way. Mr Clüde said that he had known this place very well since 1738, when he was with Georg Schmidt; he is said to have gone to school with Brother Schmidt [19]. We believe that the visit of these gentlemen has a solid reason behind it, for they inquired very carefully about everything; and that Mr Schwellengräver should have put these questions to him for him to answer, we do not find credible. Now, may God let everything turn out for the best of his own!

<!-- lines 14712-14774 -->
On the 19th our sister Lena [20] sent for Brother Marsveld; she is ill. He found her in a gladdening state of heart, and expecting that the dear Saviour would probably take her to himself soon. She said: "I have no pain, but am only so weak that I cannot walk." On the 20th a Hottentot came who had been with a farmer about 15 miles from here, with a certificate that he had served his time faithfully and honestly and was now free. The farmer he served has a smithy, and the Hottentot learned this trade with him. When he wanted to leave, the farmer would not let him go. Then the Hottentot said to him: "If Baas can vouch for my soul that it will not be lost, I will stay with Baas all my life; but Baas must vouch for me in that, and Baas shall have to answer for it if he does not let me go." (This Hottentot had been here once before, to stay here; but we had told him that he must bring a certificate from the farmer that he was free.) At this answer the farmer was taken aback, and said: "I cannot vouch for my own soul, much less for another's"; and so he gave him a certificate and let him go. On the 21st the man already mentioned, who believes that he is possessed [21], sent for Brother Schwinn and told him that when he served a farmer he had shot dead a Bastard Hottentot. Did his illness perhaps come from his not having done right in that? He had first wounded him, and then had had to shoot him dead at the farmer's command. That is how it goes here in this country: to shoot a Hottentot dead means nothing further. On the 22nd a carpenter came who has not long been free of the Company, and now lives with a farmer 2 hours from here. He is a German. Our Hanna also came again. She had been at Baas Teunis's and had asked that she might stay here with her pair of oxen and cows. But she got the answer that she might not keep her cattle there; they must all take their cattle away. She said: "I cannot go, and I am not going, whatever happens." So say very many. We do not know what is yet to come, for they will not bring themselves to move away, however sharply the orders come.

<!-- lines 14774-14825 -->
On the 23rd our sister Rahel gave birth to a little son. On the 24th his baptism took place in the meeting. Beforehand we spoke with her about the need for this child to have baptismal witnesses, and for these she named Brother Schwinn, Brother Jacob and Sister Sara. Brother Marsveld performed the baptism, and Brother Kühnel held the child at it [22]; he received the name Daniel. Afterwards we had the godparents come, and told them what a great and important thing it was to be a witness at a baptism. They had not only to commend this child to the Saviour with prayer and supplication at holy baptism; it must also be their concern that it should be brought up for him, as much as their own children. Afterwards several of our sisters came and said that they could not thank the dear Saviour enough that he had given them the great grace of seeing a child baptised into the death of Jesus [23]. On the 25th. The threshing floor [24] by our house had so far been only roughly marked out. This morning, when the children's meeting was over, we had the children carry stones, and laid it all round with stones; and we took a couple of Hottentots to work and had them dig it out. Setting the stones, which we did ourselves, was heavy work for us, for it was a very hot day. On the 26th several Hottentots came who had lived on the Breede River [25], about 20 miles from here, and who must now take their cattle away too. They had been to Baas Teunis together and asked that they might keep them here. But they got the answer that they might not keep even one head of cattle for slaughter there; they could slaughter it at their own places and bring the meat here. Some of them said that they would not go from here now, and one woman said: "Even if my husband goes, I will not go with him; I will stay here."

<!-- lines 14825-14904 -->
On the 27th we began to make a lean-to against our little house, to keep our pumpkins from the rain under it, for pumpkins are very good food for the Hottentots. We also have a good quantity of them, which will come in handy in the rainy season for our Hottentots who work for us. For of the other garden produce (except the beans) we have very little, since there were certain little creatures that ate everything off as it came out of the ground; and this has happened everywhere, one hears. So this rainy season we must rely on beans. We are often in great perplexity about space, and often at our wits' end. We could build something at little cost if building were not forbidden us. Dear brothers and sisters! You can imagine: in our house we have nothing but the little chamber where we sleep; the front room [26] and the kitchen we cannot use, because of the meetings and schools. The loft is so poor that we can put nothing but quite light things on it; and yet we are to provide ourselves with food for a long time, especially in the rainy season, because we cannot cross the river. Where, for example, are we to get corn and flour? If we want to go to the mill, we must cross the river. Someone might well ask: How did you manage last year, then? Answer: then Baas Teunis was still our friend; he helped us. But what he is now, our dear brothers and sisters will see from the diary. And our wish [grows] [27] to have a larger place where we could hold meetings and schools. Formerly we did wish to have a proper meeting house; but now we would only wish to be allowed to build a hut, as the Hottentots build; we would thank God from our hearts for it, if only we were sheltered from the sun, and from wind and rain. Until now we have held the schools mostly in the front room under the vine; but now the time is coming when the weather forbids it. When one of us holds school in the front room, the children's early in the morning or the women's in the afternoon, it is so full that not an apple could fall to the ground, and neither of the other two brothers can get into or out of our room, because one has to go through the front room. In the meetings we have opened the door and window, and let as many Hottentots as possible go into the kitchen, for even so there is far from room for all. But what shall we do when there is strong wind and rain? Well, the dear Saviour sees everything, and we ask him for help too. Today 4 more women and 3 children came who want to stay here. Since until now we had been reading the 4 Evangelists in the meetings, today we began the Acts of the Apostles, and Brother Schwinn read the first chapter.

<!-- lines 14905-14961 -->
On the 28th, in the conference, the dear Saviour approved four as candidates for baptism. Since today it was 2 years since we moved into our house, in our evening meeting we brought our most heartfelt thanks to our dear Lord and Saviour in a prayer on our knees, for his gracious help and protection in these 2 years, and for the blessing which, as he now lets us see, he has laid on the little word [28] of the Brethren of his name; and we asked him for forgiveness where we have not done, thought and acted according to his heart, and that he would continue his mercy, help us through in the time to come, and give us all that we need as faithful servants. For some time the Spirit of God has been especially active in the hearts of the Hottentots, but above all among the women. Hardly a day passes, indeed on some days very many come, mostly new people, who want to speak with us one by one about the unrest of their hearts, and say that they have no rest day or night, cannot sleep, and long for the forgiveness of sins. With this we will close our diary for this month again, and wish that you may soon receive it. It is almost, dear brothers and sisters, as if we were quite cut off from you; indeed not only from you, but from the Cape too. We have various things at the Cape which Brother Marsveld arranged in January to have sent to us at the next opportunity; but although it is now nearly 2 months, we do not yet have them, and do not know where the fault lies, and whether his brother [29] means to bring them to us; nor have we received a letter from there in this time. Now, we are all 3 still well, and live in love and peace with one another, and commend ourselves to the prayers and remembrance of all our dear brothers and sisters in the congregations. Hendrik Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn, Joh. Christ. Kühnel.

## Notes

1. The transcription reads *wir haben es denn die Chriſten*, which is garbled; the sense seems to be "the Christians have it [worse]", who travel days to a church.
2. The transcription reads *Den 3ten Chriſte und in dieſer Woche nie kamen*; *Chriſte* and *nie* are probably misreadings, and the translation gives the likely sense.
3. The transcription reads *Da kamen nacheinander*, without a subject; perhaps a word such as *etliche*, "several", is missing, or a date.
4. The transcription reads *aus Bocken⸗Revier* here and *aus Bock⸗Revier* on the 13th: probably a stream called Bok River near the mission, 2 hours away; not identified. The number 7 at line 14368 is a page number.
5. The transcription reads *wir mußten das bey Tag und Nacht kaum zu laſſen*, which is garbled; the translation gives the likely sense.
6. The transcription reads *ein Knecht des Eſſu Clude dem Conſtantia gehört*; *Eſſu* is probably *Herrn*. Mr Clüde is probably Hendrik Cloete of Groot Constantia, who received the missionaries in December 1792 (see `glossary.csv`); see also the visit of the 18th.
7. *Dimeni*, a farmer who had been given a place on the Bok River; not otherwise identified.
8. The transcription reads *erſt weniges Jahr bekommen*; probably *voriges Jahr*, "last year".
9. The man who believed he had walked over buried poison (entry 29, 17 December 1794) may be meant; on the 21st the transcription has *welcher glaubt, daß er bekehrt iſt*, "who believes he is converted", where the sense requires *beſeſſen*, "possessed", as here.
10. The transcription reads *und ſind ihnen viele verhaßt*, "and many are hateful to them"; perhaps *verreckt* or *verloren*, "died" or "lost". To be checked against the image.
11. *Deines Reiches*, "thy kingdom", addresses the Saviour directly, as in the manuscript.
12. The transcription reads *er wolle ihnen nicht gern die Schafe anthun*, "he would not like to do them the sheep"; probably *die Schande*, "the disgrace".
13. The transcription reads *Doch könnten wir doch mit einem Menſchen aufrichtig und offenherzig ſprechen*; it seems to be a wish, "if only we could".
14. In the account of this visit the transcription has *Br.* before the names of Clude, Schwellengräver and Hoffmann, probably a misreading of *Hr.*, "Mr"; compare entry 30, note 20.
15. *Hrn Schwellengräver in Holland, der in Doorn, 2 Stunden von Zeiſt, wohnt*: probably a member of the Swellengrebel family, which came from the Cape and settled in the Netherlands. Not identified with certainty.
16. The transcription has *der Geheimer Rath geweſen*, "who has been a privy councillor"; perhaps a member of the Council of Policy, or a *heemraad* of Stellenbosch. Andreas Otto is the field corporal of A363596 (see `glossary.csv`).
17. The transcription reads *Veerde (oder Ziegen)*, "Veerde (or goats)", here and in the answer; the diarists explain a Dutch word, perhaps *vee* or *bokken*, that the transcription has misread.
18. The transcription reads *Baas Teunis Opritz*; *Opritz* is not clear, perhaps a misreading of a word for spite or instigation (compare *Anſtiften* in entry 30).
19. Georg Schmidt worked at Baviaanskloof from 1738 to 1744. Hendrik Cloete of Constantia was born in 1725; a boy of his family could have known Schmidt. "He is said to have gone to school with Brother Schmidt" reports hearsay.
20. Probably old Lena, baptised by Georg Schmidt, whom the diarists count separately; the new Lena was baptised on 12 October 1794.
21. See note 9.
22. The transcription reads *hielt das Kind über dieſelbe*, "held the child over it"; the sense is that he held the child at the baptism.
23. The sisters' words suggest that this was the first baptism of a child at the renewed mission. To be checked against the earlier entries.
24. The transcription reads *Das Korn, oder die Dſchanna beÿ unſerm Hauſe war bisher nur ſo etwas ausgeſuchet*; *Dſchanna* is not clear. Laying a round with stones and digging it out suggests a threshing floor (*Tenne*), which the beans of January would need. To be checked against the image.
25. The transcription reads *am breiten Revier*, "on the broad river"; probably the Breede River, which is about this distance away.
26. *Vorhaus*: the front room of a Cape house (Dutch *voorhuis*). The phrase *im Vorhaus unter dem Weinbaum*, "in the front room under the vine", suggests that the schools were held in the open at the front of the house, under a vine, and the second mention in the room itself.
27. The transcription reads *Auch wird unſer Wunſch, einen größeren Platz zu haben*, without the verb that the sense needs.
28. *das Wörtlein Seines Namens Bruder*, "the little word of his name, Brethren": the preaching of the Moravian Brethren, who were called by his name.
29. Probably a son or brother of Father Schmidt of Cape Town; the transcription reads *ſein Bruder*, "his brother". Not clear.
30. Lines 14964 and 14965 read *RIJKS* and *EVA*, on page image 311 after two blank or unnumbered pages; probably parts of an archive stamp or label (*Rijksarchief*), not text of the diary. The diary for March 1795 continues in A363596.
