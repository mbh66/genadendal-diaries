<!-- lines 1553-1623 -->
On 2 March Baas Teunis came with his wife and 5 children to visit us. This visit was very dear and welcome to us, also because they brought us some vegetables, which we badly need. We have told our dear brothers and sisters of the abundance of food in this country. Now we will also say how things are with us at present. At Baas Teunis's we had a very good table for the two months. A farmer whose affairs are in order does well: meat in plenty. The farmers have many cattle, and they increase without much trouble. All year the cattle must find their own fodder in the field. The farmer does not concern himself with any fodder for his cattle, and so they multiply, and he need only slaughter. Then he sows some corn, and he has bread. His vegetables grow in the garden, and precious fruits too. We now live there [at Baviaanskloof]. Bread and meat we can buy, but nothing else. There is no butter to be had here; the farmers have no more cows than they need for their own house. If a farmer has 100 oxen, it is rare in this district to find him with more than 10 or 12 cows, and the 12 give hardly as much milk as 2 in the fatherland. When the cows are to be milked, the calf must always suck first; then it is put beside the cow, otherwise she will not let herself be milked. And that goes on with one calf until another is there in its place; and so one cannot get any butter to buy. No one will sell us vegetables, because they are not well disposed towards us, and the nearest lives 1½ hours from us. At Baas Teunis's we could get them, but that is 3 hours away. Now we must first learn to eat dry bread. The corn is only coarsely ground, so there is still coarse bran in it. The lack of mills is to blame for the corn being only coarsely ground. Our dear brothers and sisters may believe that to eat such bread dry, one must be hungry. Mutton must be salted at once, or it goes bad. One can eat a sheep fresh only once; then one has salt meat, and we have no other meat. The vegetables are cooked with sheep's fat; the sheep have very big tails, which are all fat.

<!-- lines 1623-1664 -->
Baas Teunis said that he would drive to the Cape on the 5th; whichever of us wanted to go with him must get ready. On the 3rd we held our first conference, since one of us must go to the Cape, partly to speak with the government about various matters concerning our place, partly about the Hottentots, and to buy many necessary things for ourselves. So we resolved that Brother Marsveld should go. Here in the country one can get nothing; what one needs must be fetched from the Cape or sent for. Everything one needs (except food, which is reasonable) is astonishingly dear. If we had to pay for our house and the materials that belong to it as is proper in this country, it would come very dear. But Baas Teunis has given us the materials cheaply or quite free. There is a fixed rate here: if one has a tradesman build a house or make anything else, and takes him on for the work, one must give him 1 Rixdollar a day and food and drink besides. And even so they do not want it that way, but would rather do it by contract. When one watches how they work, they take their time very well, and none of them overworks. Our dear brothers and sisters can imagine: food and drink and 1 Rixdollar a day, how dear a journey comes. There are very few tradesmen in the country. As the Hottentots' desire to learn is great, we decided to begin the school tomorrow, the 4th.

<!-- lines 1665-1698 -->
On Sunday, 3 March, we blessed [and] confirmed our moving in [1], since until now we had not been alone. The mason had slept with us until now; now he was finished and went away. The house is not quite finished yet; the Hottentots will finish it. We dedicated this house to our dear Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ as a dwelling where he and his Holy Spirit might live, and asked him from our hearts that he would graciously give us what we need for this his service, and that he would let the testimony we bear of his becoming man, his suffering and dying, be a blessing to the hearts of these poor people. Then we shared the cup of praise and covenant among ourselves [2] and promised the Saviour new faithfulness and obedience. He graciously acknowledged us poor ones. The text was: "He trusted in the Lord God of Israel. He clave to the Lord and departed not from him. And the Lord was with him [2 Kings 18:5-7] [3]. – With thee, Jesus, I will stay; fetch thy weak child thyself."

<!-- lines 1699-1745 -->
Today, 3 March, we had the old Hottentots who live with us come together, and told them that the school for the old people who still wanted and were able to learn would begin tomorrow, but that for the children it would be put off until Brother Marsveld came back from the Cape. Several parents said that they wished to be ahead of their children [4]. They said: "If my child comes home and I ask it, 'What did you learn at school today?', and I am no further on, the child can tell me whatever it likes; but if I am further on, that cannot happen." Then we told them that as soon as Brother Marsveld came back, something from the Bible would be read to them in the evening before sunset and explained. Because we are not yet masters of the Dutch language, we could not begin that yet. Then we wrote down the names of the old people and of the children they wanted to send, and the number came to 60 altogether; for the old people all want to learn, so that they can read God's word. Although we saw that many are too old to learn, we could not advise them against it, because they might think we did not love them as much as the young ones; and we thought: let them try. We gave each of them an A B C book, on the understanding that if they no longer wanted to learn they would give the book back to us. The school for the old people was to begin at 8 o'clock in the morning, then the children; in the afternoon the children again, and towards evening the old people again for instruction.

<!-- lines 1745-1811 -->
Already 8 houses or kraals (for they call a house a kraal) have moved to us to learn and hear God's word. It is sad to see: they are eager for the word of God, but live so scattered that they will seldom be able to come, and for more to move here is not possible, because the pasture for cattle here is poor. The cattle, especially the sheep, are said to die for the most part in winter on this side of the river (the Sonderend), because it is said to be colder on this side than on the other. Here it is close to the mountains; our house stands so close to the mountains that one could reach them with a musket ball. They are so high that we have never seen mountains as high as these. The farmers who live on this side of the river must bring all their cattle over to the other side in winter. In winter the river becomes so big that one cannot get over it or through it; so one must lay in the most necessary things for 2 to 3 months. For as to the winter, it is said to rain a great deal, and that is what winter chiefly consists of, namely rain. Some Hottentots still have a few cattle; many have nothing and must seek their bread with the farmers; in winter they cannot get over the river (it is 1 hour from us). And if they want to sow corn, which they have not done so far, the land for sowing is not very large either. How it will go with us when we have a few cattle we do not yet know. When we consider how our brothers had to struggle in the early days on the mission posts among the heathen to find one soul that was willing to listen to a word of Jesus! Here they are so eager and would gladly live with us. May God grant that it lasts and stays so, and graciously give us poor ones what we need for this cause, so that we spoil nothing in his work. We also talked together about our meetings and fixed them for set times.

<!-- lines 1811-1926 -->
On the 4th, early, 25 old people came to school. Some had been with us and told us that they wanted to look for food and could not come to school. Several of them had believed that they would get food from us; we told them that they would get nothing from us, but that we did not want anything from them either. At midday Brother Marsveld rode to Baas Teunis, to drive to the Cape with him tomorrow. We commended him to God's guidance and protection. On the 5th, 27 came to school. A child 2 days old had died the night before. The Hottentot came as soon as it was born to tell us, but [said] that it was ill and would probably not live long; this morning he came and said that it had died. We told him that when it was to be buried he should let us know; we would come to the burial. Towards evening he came and said that it was now to be buried, and we went. When we got there, all the Hottentots who live in this kraal were standing assembled in front of the kraal (or house); the women sat beside it smoking a pipe of tobacco. Then the man who was to carry the child crept into the house and brought it out. The child was wrapped in a sheepskin and tied up, so that nothing of the child could be seen. Then he went towards the grave, which was about 200 paces from the house. One man walked in front of the one carrying the child, and behind the one carrying the child came the father. When we asked what the first one represented, we were told he represented the cantor. Then came the others; several had spades on their backs [5]. The grave was more than 2 ells deep and bigger than would have been needed for a grown person. Then two climbed into it; [...] [6] in the side of the grave a hollow like a vault had been made. They took the child and set it in there; when it was inside, they took stakes and drove them in front of the hole where it had been set, then they took brushwood and laid it up against the stakes, then earth in front; but so close with brushwood that no earth can get in. When we asked what that meant, the answer was that it now lay as well as or better than in a chest (or coffin); a chest would soon be pressed in by the earth, but this would not fall in so soon. Then each of all who were there took a couple of shovels of earth and threw it into the grave. When that was done, they set to heartily filling it in. Earth was put on until the grave was very high; then a certain plant was laid on it, which is like a weed, and is a weed here too, which grows at once. They call this weed Hottentot figs [7]; it bears fruit which tastes very nourishing. When the fruit is [ripe], the Hottentots pick it and are content to live on it. Last of all they covered the grave with stones, so that the wolf [8] should not come and dig it up. Then everyone went back. In front of the house stood a wooden *bat* (or dish) [9], where each one washed his hands. The women did not go with them, however; meanwhile they enjoyed themselves over their pipe of tobacco and watched from a distance. The man had bought a goat for the occasion [10] and slaughtered it, and now it was eaten. I forgot to say that when we left the grave, which was now finished, the man who represented the clerk thanked them for having shown the child this love and accompanied it to the grave. We received special thanks and were asked whether it was well buried. We now went home. Where there is a Hottentot grave, it stays neat for a very long time, because they make it very high and cover it all over with stones. Behind our house, close by, there is one grave after another.

<!-- lines 1926-1971 -->
On the 6th our pupils came punctually again. Brother Kühnel has got a boil on his right knee, which causes him much pain, so that he cannot leave the house. It is bad that it comes just now, when there is so much work; for we must see to getting some of the garden in order, so that we can sow and plant something and have something to eat. Winter is coming on here now; but in winter one can have various things. For example, lettuce is said to do better now than in summer; in summer it bolts, but now the best head lettuce, turnips, cabbage and so on grow. And much work is needed before any of the garden is ready for planting. It would cost far too much to have it done; so much money goes everywhere on what we have to buy as it is. In the house there is a great deal to do; wherever one looks, something needs making. There is no glass in the windows yet either; Brother Marsveld must first bring that from the Cape. The necessary things cost astonishing sums, as our dear brothers and sisters can imagine from what was said above about timber. And even if one wanted to have some of the necessary things made, for example by a joiner, and wanted to pay for it at once, one still cannot get it; for we know of no joiner in the whole district. If one of us were a joiner it would come in very handy. We must work at it as well as we can.

<!-- lines 1971-2054 -->
On the 7th our pupils were all there again. It looks quite charming to see a crowd of people most of whose clothing consists of a sheepskin. Some of the men have a pair of breeches on; the rest is a skin. But among the women there are few who have any clothes. When one woman has clothes on and there are several who have none, you will see, when they walk, that the one with clothes goes in front and the others walk behind her. It is a bad thing that the Hottentots harbour so many lodgers in their kaross (or skin), lice and fleas, which are especially large. We have already inherited some of these little creatures too; since the Hottentots have no lack of this kind of livestock, they are happy to let those who have none have some. That we should get some cannot be otherwise: every day the house is full of such people; how could it be otherwise? The other day a kaross lay in front of the door that was thick with them. When we asked whose kaross it was, the owner came forward. When he was told that the kaross was full of lice, he answered: "Yes, I have not beaten it out yet today." When the skin is too thick with them, they beat it out with a stick; one can imagine, since the skin still has all its wool, how much good the beating does. Later on, when things are further along, we will admonish them as much as possible to get rid of these little creatures; but one cannot go at it seriously straight away; and if they themselves do not become serious about it, it will be hard, for their whole way of living is made for breeding lice. Something more about their kraals or houses. They build a house in one day, in the following way. They fetch thin poles from the river and stick them in the ground in a circle, so that the width inside from one side to the other is about 6 ells; the size depends on the size of the family. Then they are bound together at the top in a dome, so that the height in the middle, where it is highest, is 2½ ells. Then the floor is covered with skins, and they lay skins and mats on the poles, and so the house is roofed. On one side the door is left, but it is so small and low that one has to crawl in more or less on all fours. In front of the door they hang a skin or a mat, and so they all lie together, man, wife and children. Among the women work is not the fashion at all; they lie together and enjoy their pipe of tobacco. It seems they begin this quite young, for one sees boys and girls who are still young already smoking with them.

<!-- lines 2054-2133 -->
On the 8th our pupils came punctually again; old Lena, who was baptised by Brother Schmidt, always came with them. She, as well as the woman already mentioned who can read, helps the others on, so that in these days they have already learned so many letters that we would not have believed it. On the 9th we gave no school today; on Saturdays and Wednesdays none is to be held. Today another man came and said that he wanted to bring his house here so that he could come and learn. On the 10th we prayed the Church Litany together today. Several Hottentots came to visit us. We heard from several that beyond the mountains there are many Hottentots who want to move to us to learn; many of them serve the farmers, who will not let them go. It is said to be quite in uproar beyond the mountains; the Hottentots do not want to stay. Those who already live here also say: how is it to work if so many come to live here, since they cannot support themselves? That is true too; we do not know either how it is to work, but God knows. By human means it cannot work. When they come and say they want to move here to learn and hear God's word, we cannot say: you may not move here. That would be as much as to say: you may not come to the Lord, and you may not hear God's word. For if they do not live here they cannot come to us, or only seldom; if they live on the other side of the river, they cannot come to us for a couple of months because of the water (not counting the distance). What are we to do but call on the dear Saviour to make a way for it to work, since he has himself awakened the longing for the Gospel; otherwise it would not be so. It looks as if the hour of grace for this poor people had now struck, since the longing for the word of God is great. Several farmers have also visited us already (out of curiosity). When they come they are very friendly towards us, and we towards them. On the 7th someone came who had ridden three hours on horseback to visit us; that is a long way from here, because they ride very hard. On the 9th someone came again, from a good hour away. He is said to be a good man; we had to promise to visit him. He said: let us keep good neighbourliness.

<!-- lines 2133-2172 -->
On the 11th our pupils came again. A fine and good quality of the Hottentots, one that is really praiseworthy, is that they are honest. One can leave everything standing and lying in front of them; they are said to take nothing, unless hunger drives them to eat, or tobacco, or drink, and that is not said to happen easily. Money and money's worth are safe with them; one need not lock everything in the house; it lies as safe in front of the house as inside. So too when one loses something and someone finds it, he brings it at once; we have already had a couple of proofs of this. Today another man came and asked whether he might bring his house [11] here, and wanted to make a start today. Brother Kühnel has recovered from his bad knee. On the 12th a Hottentot woman, still young, came to us and said that the farmer she had served for two years had beaten her when she told him she wanted to go and learn, and said that if she ran away he would fetch her back. We told her that if she had done nothing else she should stay here in a kraal; the farmer would surely not fetch her. That is what they used to do: they came into the kraals, took the Hottentots and beat them miserably. She stayed here; three more came with her who want to learn; all four were properly dressed.

<!-- lines 2173-2211 -->
On the 13th we heard that a Hottentot who comes to school had struck his wife [?] on the head [12]. On the 14th, when they were all together, we asked him why he had done it. He said she would not put up with the teaching for him. We told him that he had said he wanted to become a child of God and was coming here to us to learn for that reason; we wanted those who behaved like that not to come to us. He should just give back his book now, and then he could go wherever he liked. And so it was done. To the others we said that among those who came to us to learn such a thing must not happen, or we would have nothing to do with them. On the 14th: a farmer had kept back the two-month-old child of a Hottentot woman who wanted to go and learn, because he did not want her to go. She came and told us. We wrote a little letter and sent her with it to Baas Teunis; he wrote to the farmer, and today we heard that she had it back again. On the 15th we had a Hottentot who has a wagon fetch two loads of poles, for bean poles and to fence as much of the garden as we still mean to sow and plant.

<!-- lines 2212-2260 -->
On the 16th a wagon went to the farmer who visited us on the 9th and whom we had to promise to visit soon; we took this opportunity and drove with it. He was very friendly to us, and we were to stay the night there; but we did not wish to, and drove home again that evening. On the 17th our pupils came diligently. The number grows all the time, and they really learn so that it is a marvel; most of them know all their letters. So far it lies very close to their hearts, and they take all possible trouble. On the 18th another Hottentot came to us, who has a son. Father and son had served a farmer; when the time was up, the son asked the farmer how much longer he was to serve. At this question the farmer beat him terribly with the sjambok [13]; after that he went away, and the father does not know where he has gone; this was four weeks ago already. We said that we could do nothing about it; he should go to the Landdrost and ask his advice. On the 19th we received a letter from the Cape, from a Sieur [...] [14]. He wrote that since we had left the Cape so quickly and he had not been able to give us any pleasure, he was sending us half an aum of wine [15] and wished us God's blessing. We do not know this gentleman. Today the Hottentot we recently sent away because he had beaten his wife so came and begged urgently for forgiveness and to be taken back, because he could not bear it any longer, and promised never to do it again. He now came to school again.

<!-- lines 2260-2298 -->
On the 20th we had wood cut in the river for the winter, because in the season called winter the rivers are said to be full of water and at that time one cannot get any. On the 21st we had to take a holiday, because we had overexerted ourselves in the garden all this while, and we are not used to this work. We were quite weak, so that we could do no more. The garden is full of bushes, and cutting them out in such heat wears one down; it is always hotter still than with you on warm summer days. On the 22nd we had a pleasant and distinguished visit, namely from the Landdrost of Swellendam with his wife and daughter; also from Domine Magrer [16]; further, a Baas from a Company post 10 days' journey from here, with his wife and daughter, and Juffrouw Teunis: eight persons in all. The Landdrost invited us to visit him; since we have no horses, he promised that we need only let him know when we had time and wished to come, and he would send us a wagon. It is a good two days' journey there by horse wagon. He showed great friendliness and promised to help us where he could. They stayed some hours with us, with pleasure.

<!-- lines 2298-2349 -->
On the 23rd: since the water runs a long way from our church [?] [17], a start was made today on leading it into our garden, to water it. In Brother Schmidt's time it also ran there, but it has become so choked with stones that one can hardly see it; [we] had to find out from the Hottentots that it had run there [18]. Five made a start on the digging. On the 25th, the feast of [...] [19], we were in the congregation with our hearts [?], as we had been especially since the 23rd, the beginning of Passion week. We implored our dear Lord and Saviour that he would graciously acknowledge us poor sinners, and in this truly dark country, where thy agony and death do not yet shine brightly, let thy agony and the form of thy death shine brightly in our hearts. In this country, dear brothers and sisters, things look poor among the so-called Christians as regards divine service. Many Christian children do not know a letter; so they grow old and cannot read God's word. Our dear brothers and sisters can imagine how seldom they hear it in a church, when they have to ride a couple of days on horseback or longer before they come to a church; it is sad. In this week Brother Marsveld came back to us from the Cape, safe and well, after an absence of three weeks. ## Notes

1. The transcription reads *ſegneten wir unſern Einzug b- ſtätigt*, which is garbled; the normalised column keeps *bestätigt*. Perhaps *ſegneten wir unſern Einzug ein*, "we consecrated our moving in". To be checked against the image.
2. *Lobs- und Verbindungs-Kelch*: the cup of praise and covenant, a Moravian rite in which a cup of wine was shared as a pledge of fellowship and renewed dedication.
3. The transcription reads *Blicke vertrauſte dem HErrn, dem Gott Iſrael. Er ging dem HErrn an*; the normalised column has *Blicke vertrauend* and *hing ... an*. The text is 2 Kings 18:5-7, given here in the words of the King James Version.
4. The transcription reads *daß ſie wünſchten den Kindern vor zu ſagen*, "to say [it] before the children". The reason the parents give shows the sense: they wanted to keep ahead of their children.
5. The transcription reads *verſchiedne Gatten Grabſcheiter auf dem Rücken*; *Gatten* is probably a misreading of *hatten*.
6. The transcription reads *mit ſohlen ins Grab*, "with soles into the grave", which is unclear. To be checked against the image.
7. *Hottentotten-Feigen*: the Cape succulent now called sour fig or *Carpobrotus*, whose fruit is edible. The transcription reads *wenn die Frucht iſt*, with *reif* ("ripe") missing.
8. *der Wolf*: at the Cape, the settlers' name for the hyena.
9. *Bat*, glossed by the diarists as a dish: perhaps the Dutch *bak*, a basin or trough.
10. The transcription reads *Dem hatte der Mann einen Bock gekauft*, "for it the man had bought a goat [or buck]".
11. The transcription reads *daß er ſein Pferd hierher bringen wolle*, "his horse"; since a few lines earlier a man wants to "bring his house here", *Pferd* may be a misreading of *Haus*. To be checked against the image.
12. The transcription reads *ſeinen Herrn zwingher in den Kopf geſchlagen*, "struck his master zwingher on the head"; the normalised column treats *Zwingher* as a name. The man's answer speaks of "she", and on the 19th he is "the Hottentot we recently sent away because he had beaten his wife". The words are probably a misreading of *ſeine Frau*. To be checked against the image.
13. *Sambok*: the sjambok, a heavy whip of hide.
14. The transcription reads *einen Brief von der Caß, von dem Eß⸗Sieur*; the normalised column reads *von der Kasse, von dem Essieu*, "from the cash office". *Caß* is the transcription's usual misreading of *Cap* in this file (lines 2876, 2951, 3135). *Eß⸗Sieur* is probably a name: in entry 05 (line 2375) Brother Marsveld calls the sender of the wine *Eß-Le sieur*, perhaps Le Sueur. The diarists did not know him. To be checked against the image.
15. *Ohm*: the aum, a measure of wine of about 150 litres.
16. *Domine Magrer*: the minister at Swellendam; the name as transcribed has not been identified. To be checked against the image.
17. *von unſerer Kirche*, "from our church", in both columns; the mission had no church yet. Perhaps *Küche*, "kitchen". To be checked against the image.
18. The transcription reads *es müßten es aber noch nachempfinden an den Hottentotten, daß es da gelaufen*; the sense seems to be that the missionaries had to learn from the Khoekhoe that the water channel had run there.
19. The transcription reads *als am Feſt aller Thore*, "the feast of all gates", which is no known feast. 25 March is the Annunciation (Lady Day); in 1793 Easter fell on 31 March, so 24 March was Palm Sunday. The words *waren wir mit unſerer Chriſte ... in der Gemeine* are also garbled; perhaps "we were in spirit with the congregation". To be checked against the image.
