III. Diary of the Brothers Heinrich Marsveld, Daniel Schwinn and Johann Christian Kühnel, from Baviaanskloof in Africa, from 1 September 1793 to 9 April 1794.

<!-- lines 6012-6033 -->
On 1 September Brother Schwinn went to the post, to drive from there to Cape Town with Baas Teunis. On the 2nd we had the 2 Bastards come, to speak with them about how the corn they have sown for the Hottentots could be guarded. By order of the authorities they have had to sow for those who have marched to the Cape, and this corn must be guarded against the wild horses and other cattle. This was also discussed on the 4th with the women whose husbands have been sent away, and it was settled that the 2 Bastards should see to the watch until Baas Teunis comes back and makes an arrangement among them.

<!-- lines 6033-6078 -->
In our conference we had often thought earnestly about the individual speaking and had seen that it was needed; but since our little congregation consists only of women whose husbands are away, we had always put it off. In our last conference, on 19 August, we came back to it, and resolved to make a beginning with our 6 baptised, and in such a way that the individual speaking should be done by the three of us together, or at least always by two. This was made known to them in their meeting on the 7th, and they were given an idea of this speaking, with heartfelt exhortations to search their hearts thoroughly in these days, so that its purpose might be reached. The speaking was fixed for the 11th. At this time we had arranged our meetings as follows, as far as the weekdays are concerned. Every evening we hold a general meeting, except on Saturday, when only the baptised meet. On Wednesday and Saturday mornings the candidates for baptism have a meeting, and at midday the children; and on Tuesday and Friday evenings there is also a singing meeting, or singing school. The general meetings are only for the grown-ups, because there is no room to take in the children as well; but the singing meetings are attended by everyone, so that the room is not large enough, for they are very fond of singing and sing so beautifully that there is little to correct.

<!-- lines 6079-6111 -->
On the 8th, when Brother Marsveld went out, he was called into a hut where a sick woman lay, in consumption, so that her end could be expected soon. He asked her whether she was afraid of death. Answer: no, for she must die some time. She was reminded of what she had heard in the meetings, and asked whether she believed that the Saviour had redeemed her too, and whether she had asked him for forgiveness of her sins. At this she was silent; but towards evening she had Brother Marsveld called again and asked him to repeat what he had said. Then she said she could believe it, and called on the Saviour for mercy; and she was commended to him in a heartfelt prayer. On the 9th she passed away. The others told us that in the night before she had often prayed: "My God! my Lord Jesus! forgive me my sins!" She had lived here about a quarter of a year, and as long as she could go out she had missed no meeting, and many a tear had been seen rolling down her cheeks.

<!-- lines 6111-6140 -->
On the 10th was the burial, which we attended. Beforehand we inquired about their customs. When we learned that before the body is carried out of the house it is kissed by everyone present, we told our baptised that they should leave this out, since a kiss serves no purpose with the dead. Since it is also usual to leave the bier, which is made of poles, lying on the grave, we told them that this too served no purpose, and they could use the poles for something else. All this was followed, and another direction we gave them as well, namely to give up the eating and drinking after the burial (which otherwise sometimes lasts 2 or 3 days, and through which they fall into debt with the farmers and must work it off afterwards). Only one loaf was cut, of which each received a very small piece. For the rest, the burial passed off as quietly and orderly as one could wish.

<!-- lines 6140-6178 -->
On the 11th was the individual speaking with the 6 baptised. We had imagined that they would not know how to say much to the purpose, but they had thought about themselves thoroughly and were as open-hearted as we could ask. They could not express enough their gratitude to the Saviour for all the mercy he shows them. Old Lena said again and again: "Ah, thanks be to the good Saviour that he has led you here, who teach us in thy word [1]." At the beginning it seemed as if they had forgotten everything from the late Brother Schmidt; but now one thing after another comes back to them, so that we often wonder at it. She says that in Brother Schmidt's time it did not go so far that meetings like these were arranged; but at his departure he told them that he would send them three brothers from the fatherland, and for these, she said, we have been waiting all along. We have heard more of this before: that there was a kind of tradition among them by which they expected teachers, and in particular three brothers from the homeland; and especially in the last years before our arrival there was much talk of it.

<!-- lines 6178-6204 -->
On the 12th we had a large and distinguished visit, among others from Mrs Teunis and her children, the commissioner from Plettenberg Bay [2] with his wife and children, a captain and a lieutenant, and so on. The whole company with their attendants came to 29 persons. They came after midday and drove on towards evening. Had they come for the midday meal, we would have been in great difficulty; but Mrs Teunis knows well that we have nothing, and had planned the journey accordingly. They were very friendly, and everything pleased them. When we see such companies coming, we close the school with the women, because they are ashamed; but with the children we do not close until the company is there, for everyone wants to hear them sing. Afterwards, though, we must close, for the schoolroom is our living room, visitors' room, church and everything.

<!-- lines 6204-6244 -->
On the 13th a whole herd of game [3] came over a mountain straight towards our house, with 2 huntsmen on horseback behind them. Our Hottentots ran to meet them, and they took another way; but one stuck fast in bushes and mud. At once our servant Hottentot sprang onto its back and held it fast, another cut through the hamstrings of its hind legs, and a third shot a ball through its head with our gun. So we too had a share in this booty. On the 14th a Hottentot woman declared her heart's longing for holy baptism, and said with many tears that her whole purpose was to live only for Jesus Christ in this world, and that wherever she went or stood her prayer was that he would make her a child of God. Early on the 15th the Hottentots said that they all meant to go hunting today, for the game would come back. These animals keep only to certain districts, and when they have been chased away by the hunters they afterwards return there. The day before yesterday the Hottentots had told us everything in advance, which way they would go and when they would come back. It seemed incredible to us, but everything came true to the minute. Meanwhile the horses had to be properly herded, and that lasted until the right time was missed, and so nothing was taken.

<!-- lines 6244-6272 -->
On the 16th, the special memorial day of the servants of the church, we joined in spirit, asked forgiveness for our debts, and vowed new faithfulness to our dear Lord. To our pleasure we received a letter today from our Brother Schwinn. He had sent various things for us to the post, which we were to fetch with our cart. Early on the 18th, when we got up, we saw that oxen had been in our garden, and had eaten some 80 heads of maize plants and trampled a great deal. This was because the Hottentots had come home late from a hunt the evening before and had not been able to find all their oxen to shut them in. On the evening of the 19th we heard singing in a hut, and when we went towards it unnoticed we found that it was a number of children singing together, very beautifully and sweetly, the verses they had learned at school. We heard one voice singing a very good second part.

<!-- lines 6272-6307 -->
On the 20th Brother Schwinn came back to us from the Cape, safe and well. Of his journey he writes the following: "On 2 September we left the post with 3 wagons. The crossing of the Sonderend River took us much time. The wagons were set on 4 casks and pulled across by means of a boat; the oxen had to swim across, and the people, 12 in number, followed in the boat. Today we crossed 4 more rivers, and in the evening lay down to sleep on the wagon, as far as the strong cold wind allowed. On the 3rd we passed a very dangerous place. The swollen water had made the proper road unusable, and we had to drive down a road so steep that it is very hard to get along it on foot. Baas Teunis said: 'If God does not help, there will be a great accident.' The back wheels were locked, and the front wheels too, by means of strong poles held on both sides. To stop the wagons overturning they were held with straps. Then the oxen slid down, and all went well. Then we came to a water. Here someone had to ride ahead to test the depth, and so we got through here too.

<!-- lines 6307-6360 -->
"At 4 o'clock we came to the great Hou [4], a mountain that is very high and steep, and the roads were quite ruined by the rain. We walked up on foot. Halfway up the pole of one wagon broke in two; but they had to help themselves so that the wagon got on. Then we came to a flooded river, which is dangerous to cross because of its muddy bottom and deep holes. 2 of our wagons got through safely, but the third stuck and could not be freed today. Early on the 4th it had to be unloaded, and it was pitiful to see how the people had to spend several hours in the water in the great cold. At midday we drove on, and at 2 o'clock we were at the top of the kloof, where the view struck me greatly. Until then we had been in the wilderness; now we saw before us a land like a paradise, with many houses, fine fields and vineyards, and the open sea as well. [...] because of the deep ground, all 4 wheels had to be locked again [5]. When we had got there, it was just like coming into a warm room, so great is the difference between the climate on this side of the mountains and the other. We stayed the night with a gentleman at Hottentots Holland, Wermand Guntermann [6], and that was the only time on this journey that we put up with anyone. He asked a great deal about the progress of our mission, was very glad of it, and said: 'In these days God sets an example before the eyes of us Christians through these poor people, so that we must be ashamed and have good cause to think about it,' and so on. The following night we slept on the wagon again, and could not lie down until midnight, because another wagon had stuck in the mud.

<!-- lines 6361-6397 -->
"On the 6th we met the Landdrost of Stellenbosch and the minister there. The latter asked about us and the Hottentots and wished us God's blessing. At 8 in the evening we arrived safely in Cape Town, and I was received with much love by our old friend Martinus Schmidt. Early on the 7th I went to Colonel Gordon, who was very glad to hear how eager the Hottentots were for God's word. The Landeshauptmann [7] showed the same joy and advised me to pay my respects at once to the new Governor [8]. The Governor was very friendly and inquired thoroughly into all our circumstances. He said: 'It is a hard undertaking, but praiseworthy,' gave me 120 rixdollars for our support, and said I should visit him every time I came here. I thanked him heartily and commended the mission to his protection. This gentleman has only recently come into his office. Then I visited the other good friends, who all take a heartfelt share in the work of God, and some of whom gave me clothes for our baptised. On the 9th I was with the 3 ministers, and with two of them, Mr Colver and Mr Fleck [9], I had good conversations.

<!-- lines 6397-6443 -->
"On the 10th I dined with Colonel Gordon. He is an Englishman [10], and his wife is Swiss; both take a great interest in the mission, and among other things they gave me various kinds of seed, and 50 rixdollars to buy sheep with. In these days I was also with a merchant named Schönberg, who is one of those who meet together for edification and attach themselves to the Brethren. He is related to the General Marshal von Schönberg of Schmochtitz. On the 12th Brother Schmidt [11] went with me to our Hottentots, who were camped an hour outside the town. As soon as we saw them they all ran up to me and were very glad. Besides this they had often visited me in the town. I reminded them of what they had heard of the Saviour with us, and they promised not to forget it. I had already spoken to the Governor about our Hottentots who must now do service at the Cape, and about their poor wives and children, who suffer the bitterest hunger because of it. Baas Teunis had done the same. On the 13th the Governor had me called and gave me 75 rixdollars for these women and children, so that they should not suffer hunger, with the instruction to let him know when this money was spent. On the 14th I took leave of our good friends, and on the 15th we began the journey back. On the 16th we were at a relative of Baas Teunis, where an auction was being held. On this occasion I became acquainted with many people, and our mission was often talked of. On the evening of the 19th we came to the post, and on the 20th I arrived safe and well with my dear brothers."

<!-- lines 6444-6482 -->
The diary now goes on: on 21 September we told the candidates for baptism that the individual speaking was now to begin with them too, and that a beginning would be made on the 25th. On the 22nd, after the sermon, we called together all the women whose husbands are at the Cape, and asked each of them how much flour she and her children needed in a week. Baas Teunis had asked us to do this, and he has orders from the Governor to procure the flour. On the 23rd we sent a Hottentot to Baas Teunis to fetch goats which he has bought for us. Sheep are not to be had just now except at a very high price, and Baas Teunis thinks goats will be more profitable for us, because they can bear more cold than sheep. We already have a cow, which has her first calf. On the 25th the candidates for baptism were spoken with individually. There were 10 of them, for one man who belongs to them is away. Ten [12] declared, much moved, how troubled they were that they did not always have the sufferings of Jesus before their eyes as they wished, and that they could not keep everything they heard in the daily meetings.

<!-- lines 6482-6502 -->
On the evening of the 27th, when the oxen were driven together, it was found that some were missing. When we then said to the Hottentots, "Now what the others left in our garden will be stolen outright," they set off at once, and although it was so dark that one could hardly see one's hand before one's eyes, they luckily found all the missing oxen. It is certain that the Hottentots see more sharply than Europeans, by day and by night, and we often have proofs of it that make us wonder. Sometimes we hear, "There comes so-and-so," when we can hardly see that it is a person, and they know for certain who it is. When 50 oxen are together, however dark it is, they can pick out the very one they want.

<!-- lines 6503-6553 -->
On the 28th we heard unpleasant news of a coming rebellion among the Hottentots, to tear their land from the farmers again; it was to break out at the first signal shot. The signal shot works like this. Cannon are placed all over the country. A watch is kept on top of the so-called Lion's Head at the Cape, and as soon as ships are seen at sea, and come near us in some number, a shot is fired, which is repeated by all the posts in the country. At this signal all the farmers, who are already divided into companies, must present themselves at once at the Cape to do soldiers' service. Now English troops are expected here to help defend the Cape, so that shots may be fired in any case, even when they are not hostile; and this moment, it is said, the Hottentots meant to use to take possession of the land. We had already heard of it some days ago, but would not believe it. But today a Hottentot told us that he had seen 39 captured Hottentots at the Drost's at Stellenbosch; and on the 30th Baas Teunis sent us a letter from the corporal at Cogmanskloof [13], which dealt with this same understanding among the Hottentots. He had taken 20 prisoner in his district and sent them to the Cape, and 40 more were said to be hiding in the mountains. The plan is said to have been to attack the country all at once on 4 December, and in the letter Baas Teunis was asked to keep a good watch on our Hottentots. But we believe nothing of the kind about the whole affair, and besides, most of the men are at the Cape. Baas Teunis asked Brother Marsveld to come to him, and he drove there on 1 October.

## Notes

1. Lena's words pass from "us" to "thy word", as in the transcription: *daß Er uns hieher geführt hat, die uns in Deinem Worte unterrichten*. A word such as *euch*, "you", after *uns* seems to be missing; translated to the sense.
2. *Commiſſarius von der Plettermansbay*: the Company's official at Plettenberg Bay.
3. The transcription reads *eine ganze Bande von Tiepſen*, then *die Tiepſe* and *Dieſe Tieger*; the normalised column has *Tigern*, "tigers" (leopards). A herd driven by mounted huntsmen, one of which was held by its back and hamstrung, cannot be leopards. The word is perhaps *Hirſche*, the colonists' "deer" for antelope such as eland or hartebeest. Translated "game". To be checked against the image.
4. *die große Hou*: the Houhoek pass over the mountains between the Overberg and the Cape; see `glossary.csv` (Houhoek).
5. The sentence begins at the foot of page 12 with *Wegen* and goes on overleaf with *tiefen Grundes angefahren*; words describing the descent have been lost at the page break.
6. *Wermand Guntermann*: not identified.
7. *Landeſhauptmann*: not identified. Gordon, the head of the military, is named separately; this may be another senior official of the Cape government.
8. The new Governor is probably Abraham Josias Sluysken, who took over the government of the Cape in September 1793; see `glossary.csv`.
9. *Colver* and *Fleck*: two of the Cape Town ministers, spelled *Kolver* and *Vleck* elsewhere in the diaries; see the notes in earlier entries.
10. Robert Jacob Gordon was in fact of Scottish descent, born in the Dutch Republic.
11. Brother Schmidt here is Martinus Schmidt, the diarists' host in Cape Town, called Father Schmidt elsewhere; not Georg Schmidt, the first missionary.
12. The transcription reads *Zehn erklärten ſich beſonders gefühlig*, "Ten declared themselves particularly moved", after saying there were 10 candidates present. *Zehn* may be a misreading of a word such as *Etliche*, "some". To be checked against the image.
13. The transcription reads *dem Wachtmeiſter von Caagmanskloof, d. Vorgewieſener von hier*; the last words are garbled and left out. *Caagmanskloof* is probably Cogmanskloof, near present-day Montagu.
