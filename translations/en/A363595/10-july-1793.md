<!-- lines 4326-4357 -->
On 1 July the 3 came back from Baas Teunis and said that he had promised to write. It is so bad that all who live here are so poor; if only some had something, there would be no hardship, for with food and drink they share a great deal. Lena, who was baptised by Brother Schmidt [1], has nothing at all except what she gets from the others. She goes round the kraals and looks where there is something; where she finds something, there she stays. Those who have something give gladly as long as there is anything. On the 2nd a Hottentot woman came and said that for some time she had been so sad. Question: whether some harm had come to her? She said no; but she was thinking so much about God now, and about what she had heard in the meeting; whether it came from that she did not know; and she added that she could not wait any longer to say it. She was directed to the dear Saviour, to lament her sadness to him, who can also comfort the sad. She thanked us and promised to do so. The 3rd was a day of rain; it rained so hard that we had to let the children's meeting lapse, and the evening meeting too.

<!-- lines 4357-4394 -->
On the 4th the Bastard Hottentot who has ploughed for us across the river came and said that the Baas, Wouter Gildenheuser, had come (he lives 1 hour from where the ploughing is being done) and had forbidden him to plough and sow there. If he did not stop, he would give him a sound thrashing. He says the land belongs to him; but that is not true: according to the Hottentots, the land belongs to the Sergeants River. We advised the Hottentot to go to Baas Teunis, which he did. On the 5th he came back; Baas Teunis has given him a letter to the Landdrost at Stellenbosch, which is 2 days' journey from here on horseback. As soon as the rivers are smaller, he will hurry there. One of the candidates for baptism also came and said that she wished to share in this great grace. Some days ago she was asked whether she thought much about baptism. She said: "Not so much about baptism as about this, that I can now believe that the Saviour has forgiven me my sins." On the 6th this and several other statements gave us occasion to consider various matters about baptism in our conference before the dear Saviour. He directed us to one [for baptism], and one as a candidate for baptism.

<!-- lines 4394-4446 -->
On the 7th we heard that our Hottentots at the Cape would probably not be allowed to go home (namely those for whom Baas Teunis recently wrote). The reason is said to be that they are to go against the Kaffers. The Kaffers are said to have already broken in far among the Christians of the colony near Graaff-Reinet [2], and to be murdering cruelly and burning everything that belongs to Christians; and they are said to be in such great numbers that a couple of hundred men can do nothing against them. When Brother Kühnel was at the post the other day, Baas Teunis had received a letter from the Landdrost of Swellendam, who also wrote to him about the cruel murdering and burning of this nation. Baas Teunis said that he had always said that this nation would one day take revenge on the Christians, and the Christians had deserved it ten times over, since they had treated them so unmercifully. They drove them out, took their land from them, and shot them like game. If a farmer shot a Kaffer, it meant just as much as if I shoot a vulture; and it was all the same to the farmer whether he shot a dog or a Kaffer. He said: "The Kaffers are a very good nation; I have had much to do with them." Dear brothers and sisters, it looks truly sad. The French are expected, and from the interior comes an enemy who murders and burns. It is remarkable that they have chosen this time, when the government has an enemy before it and needs all its strength to defend itself; so they are safer. From here, one hears, it is a month's journey to the Kaffers. So far, thank God, we live in peace and quiet. God only keep it so, and graciously turn away the danger that threatens.

<!-- lines 4446-4511 -->
On the 8th the Bastard Hottentot rode with the letter from Baas Teunis to Stellenbosch to the Landdrost. On the 9th an old Hottentot woman came and said that she wanted to stop learning to read, because she could not see. On the 10th a woman came who had heard singing very early this time [3]. She believed she was shut out, and she too would gladly hear the word of God, for that was why she had come so far. That was the meeting of the candidates for baptism, which is early, as soon as we have had breakfast. She was told what kind of people were in that meeting, namely those who wanted nothing more in this world than to live for Jesus, who renounced the world and sin, and wanted to do only what pleased Jesus, our Saviour and Redeemer. She said that this too was her whole purpose, to give herself to Jesus body and soul. She was told that if that was her purpose and came from the bottom of her heart (but she should know that God could see into the heart and knew whether it was so from the heart), she should ask God to forgive her her sins; then the time would come when she could go to this meeting. On the 11th another Hottentot woman came who lives here and, as she said, would gladly hear God's word. On the 12th: today we began a singing school with the children. The occasion was this: for some evenings they have come in great numbers and asked us to sing with them, which they like very much and have great delight in. They have now learned 7 verses, which they really do sing beautifully, for they have fine voices. These verses are sung both before and after school; but they would rather come every evening to sing, if only we had time. We have now set aside two evenings a week for it, namely Tuesdays and Fridays. They used to gather in the kitchen, but it was so full that one could not move. We told them to stay at home in the kraals and to watch for when they should come; we would give a sign with the bell (for it is only after our meeting). Now they watch carefully and gather in the nearest kraal. In this singing school we play the flute or the violin, which they love to hear.

<!-- lines 4511-4548 -->
On the 13th we asked the woman who is to be baptised what they talked about together in the kraals. Among other things she told us that they had talked much, and still talked, about this: her husband had once dreamed that 3 brothers would come who would proclaim the word of God to them and teach them. We told her that we were 3 brothers; not brothers by birth, but we called one another brothers and sisters, because we have God for our Father, and so, being children of one Father, we are brothers. We take this woman aside by herself every day, to give her a right idea of Holy Baptism and to explain to her the few questions written down in the liturgy book on page 122. She is one of those who cannot grasp anything; the other candidates for baptism all learn better; but the dear Saviour directed us to the very simplest. On the 14th there was a quite special stir in the sermon; the talk was on the Gospel, Mark 8. In the afternoon a woman came and lamented much that now, since she has been coming to the meeting, she becomes so restless in her heart, when before it had not been so with her. She was directed with this restlessness to the dear Saviour, to ask him for forgiveness of her sins.

<!-- lines 4548-4597 -->
On the 15th 2 Hottentot women had come back, each with a son. We wondered whether things would not still go on there according to their old custom, namely with dancing and leaping. We went to the kraals in the evening to see, because otherwise we could not find out, for so far they are still very secretive towards us about their heathen customs. But we found everything quiet, and them sitting by the fires in the kraals. They are great lovers of dancing; for when one comes to a farmer's where only a couple of them come together, dancing is soon taken up. Among those who live with us we have not yet seen it. When one watches them dance, one thinks they are all out of their minds. Their musical instrument consists of a *pottebas* [4]; that is a plant like a pumpkin; a little board is fixed to it, and 3 or 4 strings stretched over it. They pluck the strings with their fingers so that they sound; it always sounds the same, and that is their whole music, and they are very merry with it. We also received a letter from the Cape from Brother Heuser [5], who wrote us the latest news of the war, which an English packet boat had brought. We thought much about our dear brothers and sisters in Zeist, how things may be with them. According to this news, the French general Dumouriez meant to dine publicly in Amsterdam on 17 February and in London on 20 June [6]; that is saying a great deal. We do not know how things stand; we long very much for letters, but can promise ourselves none for the present. It looks sad; God help! On the 16th the Hottentot came back from the Landdrost.

<!-- lines 4597-4670 -->
He had not been able to cross the rivers because of the high water, and so had stayed away so long. The matter has turned out thus: for half an hour's distance round about the Sergeants River, [the land] [7] may be ploughed and sown by Hottentots, and the farmer may not forbid it. The place where sowing was to be done for us is hardly a quarter of an hour from there; the farmer would not allow it; now he must put up with them coming twice as far. Early on the 17th, before the meeting of the candidates for baptism began, we told the woman who is to be baptised that it would happen next Friday, the 19th; for until now we had not told her. We thought it might not be good for her to know it long beforehand, because she understands with difficulty and we wanted to make the most necessary things clear to her. Beforehand we made it known in the meeting of the candidates for baptism, but asked them to tell no one, because we wanted to do this ourselves, which happened in the evening meeting. The rejoicing among them, and especially among the candidates for baptism, was very moving to hear. On the 18th we received an answer to the letter we had written yesterday to Mrs Teunis. We had told her of our plan for tomorrow and reminded her of her offer, namely to be godmother. For while we were still with her, when our house was not yet finished, she had said that she would be godmother to the first woman to be baptised. At the time we heard this in silence, and thought: if God lets the testimony of the Gospel be blessed in the hearts of these poor people, we would surely not hold her back, if [...] [8]. Now that the dear Saviour has given us the joy of baptising one, we were really at a loss, and it cost us much deliberation. We could not do it without telling Baas Teunis, for he has a quite special charge from the Commandant to take care of us and the Hottentots and to keep an eye on us, and also to report to him how everything is going. Since none of us knows how this is managed on other posts, and we can find nothing in books either, and yet thought that there must be witnesses at a baptism (for if it were a man we would need no one, but since it is a woman we cannot avoid it), we resolved to remind her of her promise. She wrote that she rejoiced from her heart that God so blessed the preaching of his word in the hearts of these poor people, and they would come and share in the blessing too.

<!-- lines 4670-4737 -->
The 19th, the day appointed for the baptism, was a day which the Lord [9] had made for us. The text was: "And they kept the feast with joy: for the Lord had made them joyful [Ezra 6:22]. – Oh, what an unexpected guest, when he calls us his own!" And the doctrinal text: "Your sorrow shall be turned into joy [John 16:20]. – Though one must still sometimes wet one's cheeks, if only the heart can take its place there and be stilled." That we did it on a weekday and not on a Sunday had this reason: we were afraid that too many farmers might come, and we have no benches for them to sit on; also we would have to give them food and drink, and we are still very poorly off for that. At 9 o'clock a farmer named Plaisir came, a friend of ours, whom we had told of it; he is the one mentioned above who cannot read. At 11 o'clock Mrs Teunis came with her husband, and also her son with his young wife, and 4 more children. She was asked whether she would choose the name for the woman to be baptised; it seemed to please her that she should receive her name. Until now she had been called Hanna; in Holy Baptism she received the name Anna Maria. The baptism was at 2 o'clock in the afternoon. Since our house was too small to hold the people, we resolved to do it in front of the house. How many were present we cannot say; Baas Teunis said he had counted 78, but more had come after that. What feeling prevailed there we cannot describe; we wish from our hearts that it made a lasting impression on all. The others who have clothes had lent clothes to the woman to be baptised, so that she went almost all in white. Several have really fine clothes, but she had none. She laid aside her corals and beads gladly and willingly, which the candidates for baptism have also done gladly. We had thought this would come hard, for they set great store by it; the more strings of corals and beads they have round their necks and bodies, the finer they think they look. But when they were told that the dear Saviour's heart and body had not been adorned with corals or beads but with his holy blood, and that those who want to follow Jesus would not do [10] what they knew did not please Jesus, they were now quiet.

<!-- lines 4737-4797 -->
This too was very remarkable to us: her husband was at the Cape, and was one of those we had named, that leave might be given them; they got it, but only for 3 weeks. They had already asked for it twice but not got it, until the letter from Baas Teunis came. This husband of hers and 2 others arrived here 3 hours before the baptism; the others are still behind, but these, with their very strong urge to come home, had walked almost day and night. The first said he had not been able to sleep for longing to come home, and was now as glad as a child when he heard that his wife was to be baptised; he also wished very much that this grace might come to him. Another remarkable circumstance: some days ago we asked the baptised woman's sister, who is a candidate for baptism, whether she knew where the children were of those whom Brother Schmidt had baptised, and whether any were still living. She said that they were children of one whom Brother Schmidt baptised; their father was called Cupido, and in Holy Baptism received the name Jonas, which they still bore. There are 4 sisters, [...] [11] and 3 more, who are all three candidates for baptism. It is a curious thing that just these are the first, without our having known it. On the 20th 3 more came from the Cape, who said that Domine van Silk (N.B.: here a schoolmaster is called a Domine; the ministers are called *predikanten* [12]) had sought out [13] the Hottentots who go to us, and when he had found them he took them by themselves and gave them A B C books (for when they left us they gave the books back to us), and came to them every Saturday and told them something of Jesus. He had far to go, for their post was at least an hour from the Cape. Today we were busy planting the trees that Mrs Teunis brought us yesterday; they are peach, lemon, peach [14], fig and lemon trees. We plant diligently, and it costs us many a drop of sweat; but whether we shall enjoy any fruit from them, God knows.

<!-- lines 4797-4858 -->
On the 21st the last of those who have leave arrived. He came and lamented with remorse that the farmer Gildenheuser had given him so much wine to drink that he had got drunk and lain down on the road. That is a sad thing in this country: the farmers give the people so much to drink, and when they are drunk they make sport of them [15]. Among ours it has happened little so far, and when it has happened they have been remorseful and come of their own accord to tell us. We beg them, as much as we can, to let themselves be freed from this vice through God's help. This evening we came out of the door and heard someone praying, for it was close to our house. It was one of the candidates for baptism, lying on her knees; then the man we have in our service came too, and a boy of about 14, and they too knelt down and prayed, each one a piece after the other. On the 22nd: the other day we had dung fetched with our present wagon. The ground was very soft, and the wagon cut in so deep that it sank [?] almost 3 [feet] [16]. When the oxen were to pull, they sprang to one side and broke the shaft in two. This put us in no small difficulty. To borrow a wagon to load ours on and send it to Baas Teunis to have it mended there costs a great deal. We thought: if we only had a piece of wood, we would see whether we could not make a shaft. We sent to Christian Kuns, 2 hours from here, and asked for a piece of wood for a shaft. He sent us a piece and wrote that it cost a ducat. We were not a little shocked: a ducat for a piece of wood for a shaft! But when we inquired further, we were told that he had made it very cheap; it usually costs 2 Rixdollars, and to make a shaft, 4 Rixdollars. Now we have made it, and someone who understands such things has looked at it and said he could find nothing wrong with it. In this country one must turn one's hand to everything.

<!-- lines 4858-4959 -->
On the 23rd we began the school again with those who had come back, for the short time. On the 24th was the first meeting with the baptised Anna Maria. We had considered and resolved to take Lena, who was baptised by Brother Schmidt, into it; she comes to the general meeting every day and misses none; she does not fit into the meeting of the candidates for baptism. She was told this, and came along today. They were told what was now expected and could be expected of them, and asked to talk openly with us about how things were going; for we wanted to share joy and sorrow together: if things went well, we would thank the dear Saviour with them; if they did not go as one would wish, we would ask the dear Saviour to help make the feeling of his death and suffering really lively in their hearts. And Lena was reminded that she had come to Brother Schmidt and lamented that she was so distracted and did not feel the love of Jesus; another time, that she was content; she should do the same with us. On the 25th we had 7 oak trees fetched from Mrs Grunewald's, 2 hours from here, of which we mean to plant 4 in front of the house and 3 behind, to keep the wind off the house when they are big. In this country it is the fashion to have oak trees in front of the house, partly against the wind, partly against the sun. On the 26th we began to make a fence round a piece of land we have set aside for a God's Acre. There are 3 fairly big oak trees at the edge of our garden, and the acorns have come up in front of them; from these we mean to make the fence round our God's Acre, so that it is closed. Today, the 27th, in our conference we thought of our candidates for baptism before the dear Saviour, and considered whether the dear Saviour would grant us a baptism on 13 August [17]. He directed us to 3 for it: a sister of the first baptised, and a mother and daughter as well. After the recent baptism, the 2 Bastards who live here had declared to the Hottentots that they would never do as Anna Maria did at the baptism, namely lie on their knees; nor did they kneel with us; and although they saw that everyone knelt with us, they stayed standing. On the 28th this was spoken about, but without saying that we had heard it, for we thought it would not be good to say anything to them. The sermon was very well attended, moreover, with a great stir among them, and at the end this point was made: that we poor people had much cause to bow before God Almighty, and could also thank Jesus Christ, our dear Saviour, on our knees in humility for his becoming man, his suffering and dying. These Bastards believe that they are better than the others, and thought they would be the first; now they are angry that others are coming before them, and do not know what to say. They have also said that whoever wants to be baptised must hang a sheepskin round them and have no clothes (they have clothes), and the woman who was baptised has none; at the baptism she had only borrowed ones. But we believe that the Saviour will find them all the same. They are clever and have understanding, so that many a Christian might be ashamed. That they should come to Jesus as poor sinners and ask for forgiveness of their sins they cannot yet understand, for they are good and do many good works for others. One of them told us soon after we first came that we must give him the job of bringing us word whenever one of the others did not behave well; and this he wanted to be.

<!-- lines 4959-5017 -->
What we heard today of the one baptised last was also remarkable to us. Her sister told us that when she already knew she was to be baptised, she dreamed that she saw a white cloth lying in front of our house, and saw herself lying on it on her face. It is remarkable that she dreamed it just as it happened, since she did not know how it is done. On the 29th, since our firewood was used up, Brother Marsveld went with a couple of Hottentots into the forest, an hour from here, to fetch some. 3 cartloads were loaded and dragged out of the forest; and on the 30th we had it brought home. When one load was going down the mountain the cart overturned, but without damage. When one sees what roads they drive up and down, one would think it impossible. The Hottentots are altogether very practised at climbing mountains and driving wagons. When one asks: "Is there a road to this place or that?" Answer: "Yes, a footpath." When one goes with them, one can see nothing of a road; if one says, "Is this a road?", they say: "The road is good, if only one sees something of a road now and then." A place to which one has to walk as much as 2 hours they call close by. For example: "How far is it to this place or that?" Answer: "It is close by, just behind the rise." When we went, we needed 2 hours for it; and so we have often been deceived. But it is good that they consider such a distance close by, for there are several kraals an hour away from which they come to the meeting, and they prove by their coming that they think it close by. On the 31st we finished our fences, or rather the fence round the garden, which has cost us a great deal of work. It is of quince: little sticks have been cut and stuck in the ground; they are to sprout, and then one can weave them close together. If they take, one has hope of having a fence in a couple of years, so that the cattle can no longer get in.

## Notes

1. The transcription reads *Die Lena, die nun Sr. Schmidt getauft iſt*, "Lena, who is now baptised Sister Schmidt"; probably a misreading of *von Br. Schmidt*, "by Brother Schmidt", the phrase the diarists use of her everywhere else.
2. *Kaffern*: the diarists' word for Xhosa people; see `glossary.csv`. The report concerns the fighting on the eastern frontier near Graaff-Reinet in 1793, later called the Second Frontier War.
3. The transcription reads *die hatte ſingen gehört das Mal, ganz früh*.
4. *Pottebaß*: a stringed instrument made from a gourd with a board and 3 or 4 strings, probably the instrument now called the ramkie. The word itself is not otherwise known.
5. Brother Heuser at the Cape is not identified; he is mentioned again at line 5774.
6. Charles François Dumouriez, commander of the French army that invaded the Dutch Republic in February 1793. The boast reported here is a rumour of the time.
7. The transcription reads *rund um das Sergeants⸗Revier her, dem Bam und darf von Hottentotten gepflügt ... werden*; *dem Bam und* is garbled and the subject of the sentence is missing. To be checked against the image.
8. The sentence breaks off at *wenn*. The transcription continues *Das uns und der l. Heiland die Freude gemacht hat*, probably *Da uns nun der l. Heiland*.
9. The transcription reads *war ein Tanz; den uns der Eifer gemacht hat*, "was a dance which zeal made for us"; almost certainly a misreading of *war ein Tag, den uns der Herr gemacht hat*, after Psalm 118:24, "This is the day which the Lord hath made". To be checked against the image.
10. The transcription reads *und diejenigen, die Nachfolgen Jeſu wollen, den wollen, Thäten das*, which is garbled. The English gives the likely sense.
11. The transcription reads *die Spät ???*: the transcriber marked a name as unreadable. To be checked against the image.
12. *Predicanten*: the Dutch *predikanten*, ministers. The diarists' note is their own; in fact the Cape Dutch also called ministers *Domine* (see entry 01, note 5).
13. The transcription reads *aufgehetzt*, "stirred up"; the context shows that the man sought the Khoekhoe out and taught them. Perhaps *aufgesucht*. To be checked against the image.
14. Peach and lemon each appear twice in the list, in both columns (*Pfirſch⸗* and *Pfrſiſchen⸗*). One of the doubled words may be a misreading, perhaps of *Aprikosen*, "apricot".
15. *ſcherzliche Dinge*: perhaps *ſchändliche*, "shameful things".
16. The transcription reads *daß er alſo maſte faſt 3.*; the verb and the unit are unclear. To be checked against the image.
17. 13 August, the Moravian festival commemorating the communion at Berthelsdorf in 1727.
