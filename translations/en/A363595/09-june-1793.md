<!-- lines 3652-3711 -->
On 1 June we partook of Holy Communion, with the dear Saviour acknowledging us poor ones. On the 2nd, after church, Brother Marsveld and Brother Schwinn went on a visit to a farmer named Plaisir, 2 hours from here on foot. He had asked us a couple of times to visit him, and has been to see us a couple of times too. Brother Marsveld had not yet been to him. He would often like to visit us, but riding is too hard for him, because he is very stout, and old as well. When they got there, he did not know what to do for joy. It was timely, too: he had received a letter and two big sacks of clothes for the slaves, with instructions to have the clothes given out when a Christian came who could read. Each piece had a slave's name on it, and they needed them badly, for they went about fairly naked. The letter and the clothes were from his master [1], who lives 2 days' journey from here; for the place does not belong to him, but he is overseer on it, and has been for many years. This man can read neither print nor writing, which he lamented sadly. He also has a Hottentot woman in service, whose 2 daughters come to school with us; he diligently admonishes them to be obedient and to learn diligently. He is already looking forward to when they can read; then they are to read to him diligently from God's word. He said he was very glad that we had come here, and this is why: "I do believe that if I fall ill and send to you and ask for a visit, to tell me a word of Jesus, you will do it." Brother Schwinn had to read him something from the Reformed Confession of Faith, a book he had in the house, and he listened with the greatest attention. Baas Teunis's son came today with his young wife and his sister to visit us; they were very friendly. The Brothers Marsveld and Schwinn were sorry not to have been at home.

<!-- lines 3711-3759 -->
On the 3rd they came home again. Also 2 women came to us who have a Christian for a father and a Hottentot woman for a mother, to announce themselves, and to move here if they liked it; they live two days' journey from here. They were very properly dressed; if they had not said so, we would not have known that they were Hottentot women. They came to school and to the meeting, and left again on the 5th; at parting they said that they had liked it very much. Yesterday a boy had not behaved quietly in school; he chattered. A Hottentot woman who was in the kitchen heard this; she came in and gave the boy a couple of strokes with a stick. Soon afterwards the boy took his bread and went home. Today he came to the children's meeting. When they were all together, he was told that we did not want such disobedient children in the school: those who came to our school must behave quietly and properly, and their purpose must be to come to know the dear Saviour, their Creator and Redeemer, and to live to his joy in this world; those who did not want this could and should stay away. And this boy was sharply rebuked. Early the next morning he came and asked for forgiveness; he promised everything one could ask, and asked to be taken back into the school, which was done. On the 7th one of the 2 Bastard Hottentots who are back from the Cape said that if we could clear our garden of the tall grass and the roots and bushes, he would plough it up as far as he could, since we could get no one to dig. We were very pleased at this; but how to get it clear we did not know.

<!-- lines 3759-3797 -->
The 8th was Thursday [2] and so there was no school; we began at once to clear it. We sent to the kraals for women and children who would like to help us; about 30 came together at once to help us, which the children especially found great fun. They were very diligent, and at midday we gave them something to eat, but not much, for we have not much ourselves: dumplings were made, and each of them made do with a little. An unbelievable amount of tall grass and Hottentot figs was gathered together; one could certainly have loaded 30 wagons. All this had to be taken out of the garden, for one could not burn it; we tried once, but because it was still green it would not burn. How we were to carry so much out we did not know; but the Hottentot women knew what to do, in the following way. One sat down; then another came and filled the cloth [3] that she wears, which with some of the stoutest hangs to the ground. When it was full, the first took the lower corners over her shoulders and stood up, and so she had a big sack on her back; and in this way they carried everything out. When it was evening the garden was fairly clear; we thought that if they helped one more day it would be done.

<!-- lines 3797-3868 -->
On the 9th, when church was over, we asked them to help once more tomorrow. Since the school would have to lapse, we promised them to hold school on the Wednesday before. They were all very willing to help, since the school was to be fully made up, for they set great store by it, old and young alike. One need not force the children to go to school; they do it with pleasure. For the most part they are also very vain: if one praises one child for learning well, that annoys the others, and they then take all possible trouble to catch up with it. When school is over (and it lasts more than 4 hours), some still keep coming and ask to be allowed to say their lesson once more. So too in the evening; if one wanted, one could have them the whole evening. We have done this for some a couple of times, and now they came in great numbers already; but we cannot do this, for there are several who cannot come to school because of their work, and they come in the evening. Dear brothers and sisters, it puts one in a very pleasant frame of mind to hear such people in their kraals singing of the dear Saviour's agony in verses and praising him; we often have this pleasure. As Brother Kühnel has his 32nd birthday tomorrow, the 10th, we wanted to have something special. But since the work would not allow it tomorrow, we did it today, and it consisted of the following: a cock of ours had run off to the Hottentots; it kept coming into the garden and could not be caught. We shot it, and then it was roasted, and we were very merry over it. We remembered Brother Kühnel's birthday last year, which was the last day of our stay in Zeist, when it was kept with more ceremony; but here too we were merry over it, and after all we already had more to eat now than on Brother Schwinn's, on 18 March; then we had nothing but a piece of dry bread, and that was all. Things are also improving outwardly, for which we are heartily thankful to the dear God. It still looks meagre, but we have good hope, once the garden is far enough on that we can get something from it. We have already planted a piece with cabbage, which is growing well, and peas and white turnips too. We can have radishes for breakfast every morning. We also experience what they say: every beginning is hard.

<!-- lines 3868-3930 -->
Early on the 10th the Hottentot women and children came again and helped diligently all day. It was a pleasure to watch, especially the children; but they were all quiet and orderly. At midday we gave them some dumplings again, and in the evening a piece of bread; now the garden was clear for ploughing. On the 11th and 12th it was ploughed up; but much still remains to be dug that cannot be ploughed, partly because of the trees standing in it, partly because of the bank, where the oxen [...] [4]. The least is 8 oxen in front of the plough; they have up to 12 in front of it, and still do not plough very deep; what is ploughed we must still dig; it is only a help. When ploughing is done there must always be 3 people: one goes in front and leads the front oxen on a thong; the second has a very big whip, which takes some work to manage with both hands, and he drives the oxen; the third guides the plough. The oxen look as big and strong as in Europe, but they do not pull as much; they go faster, though, for when one drives with the wagon it does not go much at a walk, but mostly at a trot. Our dear brothers and sisters will think: what a big garden that must be! Yes, it is big too; for vegetables we would not need it so big, of course, but we also want to plant potatoes in it. For if one wants to plant anything in the field, one must have a watchman with it, or the cattle eat everything off, because there are no herdsmen for the horses, oxen and cows; they run where they like. Towards evening someone goes and looks for the cattle and brings them home; and if he finds them an hour from the house, that is called not far. Some let them run and do not look for them either. It happens that a farmer does not know for a whole week where his cattle are, until he wants oxen for pulling; then he sends out and has them fetched home; and then he can often look for them all day and still not find them all. It often happens that something of his is missing for a fortnight; then he hears that it is with this or that farmer's cattle. If it does not turn up at all, the wolf has taken it; it kills many a horse and ox here.

<!-- lines 3930-3968 -->
The 13th was a day of rain. We had sown some barley in the garden to get feed for the hens. On this patch there were *Hunden* [?] [5]; we had been told that we must sow barley there, and then the *Hunden* would go away. On the 14th it was fine weather again. On the 15th, in our conference, we had to consider various matters with the dear Saviour, and he directed us to accept 4 of them as candidates for baptism. A fortnight ago we got the first candidate for baptism; now we have 5. We have resolved to begin two meetings a week with them, namely on Wednesdays and Saturdays, since there is no school then. On the 16th a Bastard Hottentot came who wanted to live here and learn. Towards evening our two Bastards who live here came and said that he had stolen a horse a couple of days' journey from here. One of them had already taken the horse into safekeeping. They asked whether they might speak to him in our presence, which was done. He did not confess it, but one could tell from his words that he had stolen it. The horse was kept; he was to bring the man from whom he had bought it, which he promised to do. Then he went off to fetch the man; but he will hardly bring him. It does not often happen that someone steals something.

<!-- lines 3968-4025 -->
On the 17th two old women came who could see little any more, and asked whether they grieved our dear Lord by making gardens too. They were told that they did not grieve the dear Saviour by it, but that it would not help them to be saved either; they should rather lay themselves at Jesus' feet and ask for forgiveness of their sins, so that Jesus might graciously accept them as children of God and cleanse their hearts through his blood, which he shed for them on the cross; but if they wanted to go on in their heathen ways, with that they grieved God. They should only attend the meetings diligently and ask God to open their hearts through his Spirit, so that the word of Jesus' suffering and death might find entrance with them. They promised to do so. We have often advised such old people to stop [coming to school], since all the trouble is in vain because they can no longer see (for they really do take a great deal of trouble), and told them that they could become children of God and Christians even if they could not read; if only they sought forgiveness of their sins at Jesus' feet, believed, and their purpose here in this world was to live only for Jesus and to walk according to his mind. On the 18th a Hottentot woman who lives here came and said that today she had been with a farmer's wife named Grünwald [6], who had shown her brandy, which she used to like drinking; but this time she had become so afraid that she drank very little, because it had occurred to her whether she might not be grieving Jesus by it. We told her that she did indeed grieve our dear Lord and Saviour by it, and if she wanted to become a child of God she must avoid it entirely. She asked for forgiveness and promised not to do it any more.

<!-- lines 4025-4078 -->
On the 19th we had the first meeting with the five candidates for baptism. We had them fetched from the kraals; they did not know what we wanted. We told them that we had remembered them in our prayer before the dear Saviour, and since they had several times expressed their longing for Holy Baptism, we had gained the confidence to take them into closer instruction and tell them what Holy Baptism is and what we come to share in through it. What feeling prevailed in this meeting cannot be described. They all said: "Oh, if God would have mercy on them and let this great grace come to them, to be received and taken into the covenant with God through Holy Baptism, this would be the longing of their hearts." On the 20th another came who said that she very much wished to be baptised. She had sighed to the dear Saviour with this longing [7], and was told that if she truly wished from her heart to live only for Jesus in this world, he would let this great grace come to her too. On the 24th [8] we heard from the Hottentots who had come back from the Cape for a time that some of ours would also come. On the 22nd Brother Marsveld and Brother Schwinn went to Baas Teunis's son to congratulate him, because they had not been at home when he visited us with his young wife. On the 23rd it rained fairly hard; all this while it has been fine and warm. A week ago it also rained, and snowed on the mountains; some of this snow can still be seen today, although it is warm by day. On the flat land we have not seen any snow yet. But the weather is fairly cool, so that there is hoarfrost, but no ice.

<!-- lines 4078-4168 -->
On the 24th Brother Kühnel rode to the Jagt [9] to Baas Teunis to fetch a broken wagon. He had made it for someone who changed his mind and had a four-wheeled one made. For we cannot manage without such a wagon: when we want wood, which we have to fetch from the river an hour away, or have to go to the mill for grinding, which is a day's journey from here, how are we to get it there and back? The Hottentot we have in service has four oxen, which we have been lent willingly for a year. We can borrow oxen from the Hottentots, but not a wagon; for a wagon that looks like a dung cart in Germany costs 250 Rixdollars here. If one wants a farmer to do a haul, one must pay for the *schaft* [10], that is, a day, 2 Rixdollars for the wagon and 2 shillings for each ox, which is about 8 guilders; and when they drive, 8 oxen is the fewest they put to it. If one has a wagon with only eight oxen drive for one day, it costs 4 Rixdollars. Baas Teunis has let us have this wagon or cart for 60 Rixdollars; if we had had it made at the Cape it would have cost 100 Rixdollars. When Brother Kühnel asked at the Jagt about various provisions and where one could buy them, the young Mrs Teunis said that the young Mrs Lind, a farmer's wife another hour from the Jagt, had told her that when we came to fetch the cart she should tell us to come to her; she would give us some vegetables, because she thought we needed them. So Brother Kühnel drove there and asked for butter and potatoes, to buy them. She was very friendly; she took payment for the butter, but not for the potatoes, and gave dried peaches, beans, pumpkins and some heads of cabbage. This was very welcome to us. It is a lean year in everything, according to the inhabitants. Mrs Teunis said that she had never in any year been short of green vegetables through the whole winter, but always had enough in the garden; this year, though, she had little, and much none at all. First it was too dry; now it is too cold for anything to grow. On the evening of the 26th Brother Kühnel came home safely again. It is dangerous now to make such a journey because of the rivers, which one has to cross three times on the way to the Jagt. In summer they are small, but now the water came up to the horse's saddle. It is not good to drive with the wagon now, for one has to stand on the rails, since the water comes halfway up the rails; and these are only small rivers. That is the reason why one cannot get to the Cape now. And when one drives through a river, a Hottentot must always go ahead to lead the front oxen. But the Hottentots are used to going into the water up to their necks. It is a good thing that they need little time to undress, for if we always had to stop until one of them had taken off his clothes, it would cause much delay; but as it is, it is soon done, for their kaross or skin is quickly off when they see that it is deep.

<!-- lines 4168-4234 -->
We have often talked together about Lena, the one who was baptised by Brother Schmidt. She misses no meeting. She also wanted to come to school and learn to read better; but because she can hardly see any more, she was told that this would be in vain. But she came all the same [11] and listened. We have often talked with her and reminded her of the covenant she made with the dear Saviour at her baptism, and asked whether she still remembered it; but she seemed to know little more, and there seemed to be little life left in her. When one asks: "Lena, how are you?" Answer: "Good and bad; it is very cold and my kaross is torn; it is torn." Question: whether she did not sometimes think of Jesus our Saviour and want to be saved? Answer: "God grant it!" We thought she must surely be able to remember her baptism, for she can still tell much about Brother Schmidt: she can still say at what hours the school began, and that every evening he read to them from the big book (that is, the Bible) and talked about it, as we do. We meant to take her aside by herself some time and ask her what Brother Schmidt had said to her before her baptism; that happened today, the 25th. She said she did not know that he had spoken with her before the baptism; when she had been baptised, he had told her that she had now made a covenant with the dear Saviour to live only for him in this world. Before that he had said nothing to her, only that he would baptise her; and that happened by the little tree [12] that is still standing, about 50 paces from our house, where the river runs; there he had baptised her, and nobody had been present. Question: whether she believed, then, that she would be saved when she died? Answer: "Yes, now I believe that the Saviour will take me to himself." She said further that her sister had also wanted to be baptised; but Brother Schmidt had said he could not do that on his own authority, he must first have it from the dear Saviour. "But afterwards she was baptised too, and I was present, but again at another river." She said that three men had also been baptised, but where they were baptised she did not know [13].

<!-- lines 4234-4264 -->
On the 27th one of the candidates for baptism came and said that she had been chopping some wood when she became so sad that she fell on her knees and asked the dear Saviour heartily for forgiveness of her sins; then it seemed to her as if someone said to her: "Thy sins are forgiven thee." At this she had become so joyful, and so [eager] to tell it. She was directed to the Friend of sinners, to ask him to keep her in this feeling and make his becoming man, his suffering and dying for poor sinners ever clearer and more tangible in her heart. As for the daily meetings, we must say that the Spirit of God is powerfully at work in the hearts to glorify Jesus' agony and death; the feeling and the many tears bear witness to it, for, as they feel [...], it lies close to their hearts to be saved [14]. On the 28th a Bastard Hottentot came from Hottentots Holland, two days' journey from here. He came at midday and wanted to attend the meeting; he helped dig in the garden without anyone telling him to.

<!-- lines 4264-4326 -->
On the 29th a whole crowd of Hottentot women came and complained bitterly that they had nothing to eat; they and their children must die of hunger. When Brother Kühnel was at Baas Teunis's on the 25th, Baas Teunis asked how things were with the women and children, whether they had anything to live on. Brother Kühnel told him that things were sad; they were waiting anxiously for their husbands to come back from the Cape; it had been said now that they were coming, but it had not been true. Baas Teunis said that it could be a year and a day before they came back; if they asked for leave they could probably get it for some time; but they were not troubled about it, for their wives and children did not weigh on them, and at the Cape they suffered no want: they have enough to eat and drink. He said: "If they would only come to me and complain, I would write to the Cape to Mr Gordon (he is the head of the soldiers) and ask for leave for them." So when a number of them came today and complained bitterly, we advised them to go to Baas Teunis with these complaints and ask him to intercede at the Cape for their husbands, so that they might come home for a time, which they promised to do tomorrow. When the men are at home, they go to the farmers and do some work, for which they get corn (that is wheat, which is called corn here); they boil this and eat it; on that they live [15]; it hardly comes to baking bread. It is sad to see now: whoever still had a sheep or anything has slaughtered it, so that now everything is eaten up. How it is to go on, God knows. In the evening we partook of Holy Communion, and the Saviour graciously acknowledged us poor ones. Early on the 30th all the Hottentot women came and asked us to write to Baas Teunis for their husbands to come back from the Cape. But we could not ask for all of them, only for those whose wives are old or who have small children. We told the young ones that they should work and try to earn their bread. We gave them a letter for Baas Teunis and named in it 5 with whom the need is greatest.

## Notes

1. *ſeinem Pfarrer*, "his pastor", in both columns. The context (the owner of the farm, who sent clothes for his slaves and employed Plaisir as overseer) suggests *Herrn*, "master". To be checked against the image.
2. *Am 8t war es Donnerstag* in the transcription, "the 8th was Thursday"; but 8 June 1793 was a Saturday, and Saturday was one of the diarists' days without school. The next day, the 9th, was a Sunday with church, as the diary says.
3. The transcription reads *das Güll was ſie aufhaben*; the normalised column has *Gürteltuch*, "girdle cloth". The garment was probably the kaross or a skin apron. To be checked against the image.
4. The transcription reads *andern Theils wegen des Ufers, wo uns der Ochſen*, and the sentence breaks off. Something is missing, perhaps "where the oxen cannot go".
5. The transcription reads *auf dieſem Fleck waren Hunden ... dadurch gingen die Hunden weg*, "there were dogs on this patch ... that would drive the dogs away". The word is probably a misreading, perhaps of a weed or a pest. To be checked against the image.
6. *Grünwaldin*: a farmer's wife named Grünwald; compare Stoffel Grunewald in `glossary.csv` (A363596).
7. The transcription reads *Sie würde mit dieſem Verlangen zum l. Heiland geſeufzet*; probably *wurde ... gewiesen*, "she was directed with this longing to the dear Saviour to sigh to him". The English follows the likely sense.
8. *Am 24t* in the transcription; the entries that follow are dated the 22nd and 23rd, and the next 24th follows them. The date is probably a slip or misreading for the 21st. To be checked against the image.
9. *auf die Jagd*, "to the hunt", in both columns; from the context, a place: the farm where young Teunis and his wife lived, perhaps named De Jagt. It was reached by crossing three rivers.
10. *die Schaft*: the Cape Dutch *skof*, a stage of travel or of work; here a day's hire. "8 guilders": *8. gl.* in the transcription.
11. The transcription reads *Sie kan aber doch, und hörte zu*; probably *kam*, "came".
12. The transcription reads *bey dem Berdchen welcher noch ſteht*; the normalised column keeps *Berdchen*. Perhaps *Bäumchen*, "little tree", or the pear tree Georg Schmidt planted (entry 03). To be checked against the image.
13. Lena's account of her baptism and her sister's; she is the only one of Georg Schmidt's baptised still living (entry 02).
14. The transcription reads *denn es liegt ihnen, wie ſich empfinden wa⸗ ren urlaub haben, von Herzen an, ſelig zu werden*, which is garbled across the page break. To be checked against the image.
15. The transcription reads *lange leben ſie*, "long they live"; probably *davon leben ſie*, "on that they live".
